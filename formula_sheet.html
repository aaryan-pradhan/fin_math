F1.Probability Space & σ-Fields

F1.1  The Triple

$(\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$
$\Omega$ = outcomes  |  $\mathcal F$ = events we may assign probability to  |  $P$ = the measure
event $A \subseteq \Omega$ happened $\iff \omega \in A$
Use when: every definition question starts here. $\mathcal F$ is not decoration — it is which questions you are allowed to ask.
Q: “short note on the probability space”.
Trick: one line each: $\Omega$ is what can happen, $\mathcal F$ is what you can ask, $P$ is how likely. Then give the die example.

F1.2  σ-Field Axioms

(i) $\varnothing \in \mathcal F$
(ii) $A \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow A^{c} \in \mathcal F$
(iii) $A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow \bigcup_i A_i \in \mathcal F$
Use when: “is this collection a $\sigma$-field?” Check in order. Closure under complement is the usual failure — test it first, it is fastest.
Q: die, is $\{\varnothing, \{6\}, \Omega\}$ a $\sigma$-field? No — $\{6\}^c = \{1,2,3,4,5\}$ is missing.
Trick: intersections come free: $A\cap B = (A^c \cup B^c)^c$. You never check them separately.

F1.3  Probability Measure Axioms

$P(A) \ge 0$;   $P(\Omega) = 1$;   $P\big(\bigsqcup_i A_i\big) = \sum_i P(A_i)$ (disjoint)
$P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$,   $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow P(A) \le P(B)$
$P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$,   $P\big(\bigcup A_i\big) \le \sum P(A_i)$
Use when: the subadditivity (union bound) on the last line is the one that does real work — it is step 6 of Borel–Cantelli (F2.5).
Q: die, $A=\{1,2\}$, $B=\{2,3\}$: $P(A\cup B) = \tfrac12 \le \tfrac13+\tfrac13$.
Trick: the union bound is loose exactly by the double-counted overlap.

F1.4  Continuity of Measure

$A_n \uparrow A \;\Rightarrow\; P(A_n) \to P(A)$  (from below)
$A_n \downarrow A \;\Rightarrow\; P(A_n) \to P(A)$  (from above)
Use when: two places on this paper — right-continuity of the d.f. (F5.3) and step 5 of Borel–Cantelli (F2.5). Both times you need the decreasing version.
Trick: spot the nesting first. If the sets shrink you may swap $\lim$ and $P$; if they neither grow nor shrink you may not.

F1.5  Counting σ-Fields (finite $\Omega$)

generated by a partition into $k$ atoms $\Rightarrow$ exactly $2^k$ events
smallest: $\{\varnothing,\Omega\}$  |  largest: $2^{\Omega}$, i.e. $2^{|\Omega|}$ events
Use when: “how many events are in $\sigma(\xi)$?” Count the distinct values of $\xi$, call it $k$, answer $2^k$. Never enumerate.
Q: die. $\sigma(\text{even/odd})$: 2 atoms $\to \mathbf{4}$ events. $\sigma(\omega)$: 6 atoms $\to 2^6 = \mathbf{64}$. Constant r.v.: 1 atom $\to \mathbf{2}$.
F2.Almost Surely & Borel–Cantelli

F2.1  Almost Surely

“$A$ holds a.s.” $\iff P(A) = 1$
equivalently $P(A^c) = 0$ — exceptions allowed, but only on a null set
Use when: a.s. is not “always”. A continuous r.v. satisfies $\xi \ne 0.5$ a.s. even though $\omega$ with $\xi = 0.5$ exist.
Trick: in a finite $\Omega$ with all $P(\omega) > 0$ there are no null sets, so a.s. and always coincide. That is why die examples never show the distinction.

F2.2  The limsup Event (“infinitely often”)

$E = \limsup_n A_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\bigcup_{i=n}^{\infty} A_i}_{B_n}$
$= \{\omega : \omega$ belongs to infinitely many $A_i\}$
Use when: the words “infinitely often” or “i.o.” appear. Note $B_n \downarrow$, which is what licenses F1.4.
Read it aloud: “for every $n$, there is some $i \ge n$ with $\omega \in A_i$” — i.e. you never run out of later hits.
Q: coin, $A_i = \{$toss $i$ is H$\}$. $\omega = HTHTHT\ldots \in E$; $\omega = HHTTTT\ldots \notin E$.

F2.3  Borel–Cantelli I

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i) < \infty \;\Longrightarrow\; P(\limsup A_n) = 0$
i.e. a.s. only finitely many $A_i$ occur
No independence needed.
Use when: the sum converges. One direction only — a divergent sum tells you nothing on its own.
Q: $\Omega=[0,1]$ uniform, $A_i = [0, 1/i^2]$. $\sum P(A_i) = \pi^2/6 < \infty$, so $P(E) = \mathbf{0}$ — and indeed $E = \{0\}$.
Trick: the conclusion is “there is a random cutoff $N(\omega)$ after which $A_i$ never happens again”.

F2.4  Borel–Cantelli II (needs independence)

$A_i$ independent and $\displaystyle\sum_i P(A_i) = \infty \;\Longrightarrow\; P(\limsup A_n) = 1$
Use when: divergent sum and independence. Drop independence and the conclusion dies.
Counterexample: $A_i = [0, 1/i]$. $\sum 1/i = \infty$, but the $A_i$ are nested (maximally dependent) and $E = \{0\}$, so $P(E) = 0$, not $1$.
Trick: state which lemma you are using and verify its hypothesis — that is where the mark is.

F2.5  The Proof Chain (BC I)

$0 \le P(E) = \lim_n P(B_n) \le \lim_n \sum_{i\ge n} P(A_i) = 0$
Use when: asked to prove it. Exactly three ingredients, in this order:
1. $B_n \downarrow$   2. continuity from above (F1.4)   3. union bound (F1.3).
The final $0$ is because a convergent series has vanishing tails.
Trick: write the sandwich $0 \le \cdots \le 0$ explicitly. It is worth full marks on its own and takes four lines.
F3.Random Variables & Measurability

F3.1  Definition

$X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable if
$[X \in B] := X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega : X(\omega)\in B\} \in \mathcal F \quad \forall B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)$
A r.v. = an $\mathcal F$-measurable function on a probability space
Use when: the reason for the definition is the marked point: it is exactly what makes $P(X\in B)$ well defined.
Trick: a random variable is not random and is not a variable — it is a deterministic function of $\omega$. All the randomness lives in which $\omega$ occurs.

F3.2  You Only Check the Generators

$X$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable $\iff \{X \le x\} \in \mathcal F$ for every $x \in \mathbb R$
Because $\{(-\infty,x] : x\in\mathbb R\}$ generates $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$.
Use when: always. Never attempt to test every Borel set — test the half-lines and cite that they generate.
Q: $\Omega=\{H,T\}$, $X$ = #heads, $\mathcal F = 2^{\Omega}$. Pre-images of $(-\infty,x]$: $\varnothing$ ($x<0$), $\{T\}$ ($0\le x<1$), $\Omega$ ($x\ge1$). All in $\mathcal F$ ✓

F3.3  Measurability Is Joint in $(X,\mathcal F)$

the same $X$ can be $\mathcal F$-measurable and not $\mathcal G$-measurable
Use when: the question shrinks the $\sigma$-field (“you are only told whether the die was low or high”). Measurability is a statement about information, not about the formula for $X$.
Q: die, $\mathcal G = \{\varnothing,\{1,2,3\},\{4,5,6\},\Omega\}$.
$Y = \mathbb 1\{\omega\ge4\}$: $Y^{-1}(\{1\}) = \{4,5,6\}\in\mathcal G$ ✓ measurable.
$X = \mathbb 1\{\omega$ even$\}$: $X^{-1}(\{1\}) = \{2,4,6\}\notin\mathcal G$ ✗. Being told “high” does not tell you “even”.

F3.4  ⭐ The Atom Test

$X$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable $\iff$ $X$ is constant on every atom of $\mathcal G$
Atoms = the finest blocks $\mathcal G$ can distinguish.
Use when: every discrete measurability question on the paper. It is also, unchanged, the test for adapted (M1.2) and for Doob–Dynkin (F4.4). Learn this one test and you get three topics.
Trick: list the atoms, then scan $X$ down each block. Two different values inside one block $\Rightarrow$ not measurable, and you have your counterexample for free.
F4.Generated σ-Fields & the Doob–Dynkin Lemma

F4.1  $\sigma(\xi)$

$\sigma(\xi) = \{\xi^{-1}(B) : B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R)\}$
the smallest $\sigma$-field making $\xi$ measurable
atoms of $\sigma(\xi)$ = the level sets $\{\xi = v\}$
Use when: “find $\sigma(\xi)$” means “find the level sets, then take all unions of them”. Pre-images automatically satisfy the $\sigma$-field axioms, so nothing needs checking.
Q: die, $\xi(\omega) = \omega \bmod 3$. Atoms $\{3,6\},\{1,4\},\{2,5\}$, so $|\sigma(\xi)| = 2^3 = \mathbf 8$.
Check: $\xi^{-1}([0.5,5]) = \{1,4\}\cup\{2,5\} = \{1,2,4,5\}$ ✓

F4.2  σ-Field of a Family & the Separation Principle

$\sigma(\xi_i, i\in I) = \sigma\Big(\bigcup_{i\in I}\sigma(\xi_i)\Big)$
$\omega_a,\omega_b$ separable $\iff \exists\, j$ with $\xi_j(\omega_a)\ne\xi_j(\omega_b)$
Use when: comparing how much information different observables carry. Count atoms via the separation principle, then use F1.5.
Q: two tosses, $\xi_1,\xi_2$ the indicators and $S = \xi_1+\xi_2$.
$\sigma(\xi_1)$: 2 atoms $\to$ 4 events. $\sigma(\xi_1,\xi_2)$: 4 atoms $\to$ 16. $\sigma(S)$: $HT,TH$ both give $S{=}1$ so they merge → 3 atoms $\to$ 8.

F4.3  Borel Functions Compose

$f$ Borel, $\xi$ a r.v. $\Longrightarrow f(\xi)$ is $\sigma(\xi)$-measurable
proof: $\{f(\xi)\in B\} = \xi^{-1}\big(f^{-1}(B)\big)$
Use when: two-line proof, always the same two lines. Note it says $\sigma(f(\xi)) \subseteq \sigma(\xi)$ — applying a function can only lose information.
Q: die, $\xi(\omega)=\omega$, $f(x)=x^2$. $\{f(\xi)=16\} = \xi^{-1}(\{-4,4\}) = \{4\} \in \sigma(\xi)$ ✓
Trick: $f$ can merge atoms ($f(x)=x^2$ merges $\pm$), never split one.

F4.4  ⭐ Doob–Dynkin Lemma

$\eta$ is $\sigma(\xi)$-measurable $\iff \exists$ Borel $f$ with $\eta = f(\xi)$
Use when: “show $\eta$ is a function of $\xi$” or “is $\eta$ $\sigma(\xi)$-measurable?”
Meaning: knowing the value $\xi(\omega)$ pins down $\eta(\omega)$ — no leftover randomness.
The one-line test: does $\xi$ ever take the same value at two $\omega$ where $\eta$ differs? If yes → not measurable, and those two $\omega$ are your counterexample.
Q: $\xi(\omega_1){=}\xi(\omega_2){=}2$, $\xi(\omega_3){=}5$; $\eta = 9,9,25$. Ties in $\xi$ match ties in $\eta$ ✓, so $\eta = f(\xi)$ with $f(2){=}9$, $f(5){=}25$.
F5.Distribution Measure & the d.f.

F5.1  Push-Forward (Distribution) Measure

$P_\xi(B) = P(\xi\in B) = P\big(\{\omega : \xi(\omega)\in B\}\big)$
A probability measure on $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))$.
Use when: $P_\xi$ transports probability from the abstract $\Omega$ onto the real line. Once you have $P_\xi$ you never need $\Omega$ again (that is E2.1).
Q: two tosses, $\xi$ = #heads, vs. a uniform $\omega\in[0,1]$ cut at $0.25$ and $0.75$. Completely different $\Omega$, identical $P_\xi$.
Trick: $P_\xi$ records values and their probabilities and forgets everything else.

F5.2  Distribution Function

$F_\xi(x) = P(\xi\le x) = P_\xi\big((-\infty,x]\big)$
$P(a < \xi \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$
Use when: note the interval formula is for $(a,b\,]$ — half-open, matching the $\le$ in the definition. Getting this wrong only matters at a jump (F5.4).
Q: #heads in two tosses: $F = 0,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac34,\ 1$ on $x<0$, $[0,1)$, $[1,2)$, $x\ge2$.
$F(1.99) = \tfrac34$, $F(1) = \tfrac34$, $F(0.5) = \tfrac14$.

F5.3  The Three Properties

(i) non-decreasing
(ii) right-continuous: $\lim_{h\downarrow0}F(x+h) = F(x)$
(iii) $F(-\infty) = 0$,   $F(+\infty) = 1$
Use when: “is this function a d.f.?” or “prove the properties”. All three, and right-continuity is the one they mark.
Proof of (ii): $x_n\downarrow x$ gives $A_n = \{\xi\le x_n\}\downarrow\{\xi\le x\}$; apply continuity from above (F1.4). That is the whole argument.
Note: left limits may differ — $F(1^-) = \tfrac14 \ne \tfrac34 = F(1)$ above.

F5.4  Jump Size = Point Mass

$P(\xi = x) = F(x) - F(x^-)$
$F$ continuous at $x \iff P(\xi = x) = 0$
Use when: deciding whether $<$ and $\le$ can be swapped. At a jump they cannot. For an absolutely continuous r.v. there are no jumps, so they always can.
Q: #heads: $P(\xi\le1) = \tfrac34$ but $P(\xi<1) = F(1^-) = \tfrac14$.
Trick: this is why every Brownian-motion formula on this sheet ignores strict vs non-strict — those laws are continuous.
F6.Densities & Joint Distributions

F6.1  Absolutely Continuous

$P(\xi\in B) = \displaystyle\int_B f_\xi(x)\,dx$,   $f_\xi \ge 0$,   $\int_{\mathbb R} f_\xi = 1$
$f_\xi(x) = F_\xi'(x)$ wherever $F$ is differentiable
Use when: two traps live here. (a) a density is not a probability and may exceed $1$ (uniform on $[0,0.5]$ has $f=2$). (b) $P(\xi = x_0) = 0$ for every single point.
Q: $\xi\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)$: $P(\xi>1) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$, $P(1<\xi\le2) = e^{-1}-e^{-2}\approx 0.233$.

F6.2  Discrete

$P(\xi\in B) = \displaystyle\sum_{x_i\in B} P(\xi = x_i)$
Use when: mass sits at isolated points. Only the listed values inside $B$ count — anything between them contributes nothing.
Q: fair die, $P(\xi\in[2,4.5]) = P(2)+P(3)+P(4) = \tfrac12$. The endpoint $4.5$ carries no mass.

F6.3  Joint Density in $\mathbb R^n$

$P\big((\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)\in B\big) = \displaystyle\int\!\cdots\!\int_B f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,dx_1\cdots dx_n$
marginal: $f_{\xi_1}(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} f(x,y)\,dy$
independent $\iff f(x,y) = f_{\xi}(x)\,f_{\eta}(y)$ (factorises)
Use when: independence is tested by factorisation, not by computing a correlation. Zero correlation does not imply independence — except inside a MVN (§1.5).
Q: unit square, $f\equiv1$: $P(\xi_1+\xi_2\le1)$ = area of the triangle = $\tfrac12$. $P(\xi_1=\xi_2) = 0$ (a line has zero area).

F6.4  Events as Regions

$\{\xi_1 \le a,\ \xi_2 > b\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; B = (-\infty,a]\times(b,\infty)$
Use when: translate the worded event into a region first, then integrate. For a uniform density the integral is just the area.
Q: unit square, $P(\xi_1\le0.3,\ \xi_2>0.5) = 0.3\times0.5 = \mathbf{0.15}$.
Trick: this is exactly the skill §14.3 needs — a joint probability is a region, and a rectangle is four corner evaluations.
F7.Multivariate Normal & Joint MGF

F7.1  MVN Construction

$\mathbf X = A\mathbf Z + \boldsymbol\mu$,   $\mathbf Z \sim N(\mathbf 0, I_n)$ i.i.d. standard normals
$E[\mathbf X] = \boldsymbol\mu$,   $\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf X) = A A^{\!\top}$
Use when: the full toolkit — linear combinations, joint MGF, conditional law — is in §1.5–§1.9. This card is the construction those rest on.
Q: show Brownian motion is MVN. $W_1 = Z_1$ and $W_3 = W_1 + (W_3-W_1) = Z_1 + \sqrt2 Z_2$, so
$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigma = AA^{\!\top} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$ — exactly $\min(s,t)$ ✓

F7.2  Joint MGF Determines Everything

$\phi_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf t) = E\big[e^{\mathbf t^{\!\top}\mathbf X}\big] = \exp\!\big(\mathbf t^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\mu + \tfrac12\mathbf t^{\!\top}\Sigma\,\mathbf t\big)$
Use when: an MVN is completely specified by $(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ — so to identify a distribution you need only match mean and covariance. That is the whole of the §5.2 Wiener checklist.
Derivation: $Y = \mathbf t^{\!\top}\mathbf X$ is scalar normal with mean $\mathbf t^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\mu$ and variance $\mathbf t^{\!\top}\Sigma\mathbf t$; apply $E[e^Y] = e^{\mu_Y + \sigma_Y^2/2}$ (§1.2). Two lines.
E1.Expectation as a Lebesgue Integral

E1.1  Integrability

$\xi \in L^1 \iff E|\xi| = \displaystyle\int_\Omega |\xi|\,dP < \infty$
then $E(\xi) = \displaystyle\int_\Omega \xi\,dP$
Use when: “is $\xi$ integrable?” Always test $E|\xi|$, never $E\xi$. A valid distribution can still fail to be integrable.
Q: St Petersburg, $P(\xi = 2^k) = 2^{-k}$: $E|\xi| = \sum 1 = \infty$. Not integrable.
Q: $P(\xi = k) = c/k^2$, $c = 6/\pi^2$: $E|\xi| = c\sum 1/k = \infty$. Not integrable, though $P$ is a perfectly good distribution.
Trick: bounded $\Rightarrow$ integrable, free.

E1.2  Why the Absolute Value

$\xi = \xi^{+} - \xi^{-}$,   $\xi^{+} = \max(\xi,0)$,   $\xi^{-} = \max(-\xi,0)$
$|\xi| = \xi^{+} + \xi^{-}$, so $E|\xi| < \infty \iff$ both parts finite
Use when: if both parts are $\infty$ then $E\xi$ is $\infty - \infty$, which is undefined — not $0$, even when the distribution is symmetric.
Q: $P(\xi = 2^k) = P(\xi = -2^k) = 2^{-k-1}$. Perfectly symmetric, yet $E\xi^{+} = E\xi^{-} = \infty$ and $E\xi$ does not exist.

E1.3  Indicators

$E[\mathbb 1_A] = P(A)$
Use when: the hinge between probability and expectation. It turns any probability into an expectation and back, and it is step one of the construction ladder.
Trick: it needs no $\omega$-by-$\omega$ sum, so it works identically on a die and on $[0,1]$. That generality is the point of the Lebesgue integral.

E1.4  Simple Functions

$\eta = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_i\,\mathbb 1_{A_i}$,   $A_i$ pairwise disjoint
$E(\eta) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_i\,P(A_i)$
Use when: disjointness is for the representation to be unambiguous; linearity of the integral holds regardless. The $A_i$ need not cover $\Omega$ — $\eta = 0$ off their union.
Q: die, $\eta = 10\cdot\mathbb 1_{\{1,2\}} + 5\cdot\mathbb 1_{\{3\}}$: $E\eta = 10(\tfrac26) + 5(\tfrac16) = \tfrac{25}{6} \approx \mathbf{4.17}$ ✓

E1.5  The Construction Ladder

indicators $\to$ simple $\to$ non-negative (MCT) $\to$ general ($\xi^{+}-\xi^{-}$)
Use when: “construct / define the Lebesgue integral”. Name all four rungs and say which theorem carries you up each one — MCT for rung three.
Q: approximate $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ by $\eta_n = \lfloor 2^n U\rfloor/2^n$. $E\eta_n = \tfrac12(1-2^{-n}) \uparrow \tfrac12 = EU$ ✓

E1.6  Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen

$P(|\xi|\ge a) \le \dfrac{E|\xi|}{a}$
$P(|\xi-\mu|\ge a) \le \dfrac{\sigma^2}{a^2}$
$h$ convex $\Rightarrow E[h(\xi)] \ge h(E\xi)$
Use when: a bound is asked instead of an exact value, or a comparison between $E[h(\xi)]$ and $h(E\xi)$.
Q: $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$. $E(U^2) = \tfrac13 > \tfrac14 = (EU)^2$ (convex ✓). $E\sqrt U = \tfrac23 \approx 0.667 < \sqrt{0.5}\approx0.707$ (concave, inequality flips).
E2.Change of Variables for Expectation

E2.1  The Theorem

$E\big(h(\xi)\big) = \displaystyle\int_\Omega h(\xi(\omega))\,dP(\omega) = \int_{\mathbb R} h(x)\,dP_\xi(x)$
$h$ Borel, $h(\xi)$ integrable.
Use when: this is the theorem that lets you stop working on $\Omega$. Everything downstream (densities, MGFs, the whole BM sheet) is this identity.
Q: two tosses, $\xi$ = #heads, $h(x)=x^2$.
On $\Omega$: $\tfrac14(4+1+1+0) = 1.5$. On $\mathbb R$: $0(\tfrac14)+1(\tfrac12)+4(\tfrac14) = 1.5$ ✓
Trick: the $\mathbb R$ version merges $HT$ and $TH$ — fewer terms, same answer.

E2.2  The Two Working Forms

continuous: $E[h(\xi)] = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(x) f_\xi(x)\,dx$
discrete: $E[h(\xi)] = \displaystyle\sum_i h(x_i)\,P(\xi = x_i)$
Use when: every numerical expectation you will ever compute. The measure-theoretic statement collapses to one of these two lines.
Q: $Z\sim N(0,1)$, $E[e^Z] = \int e^x\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}dx = e^{1/2} \approx \mathbf{1.649}$.
Trick: complete the square — that single computation is §1.2 and powers all of GBM.

E2.3  $E[h(\xi)] \ne h(E\xi)$

apply $h$ to each value before averaging, never after
Use when: the single most common conceptual error in the subject. It is why GBM has an $e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$ and why the mean of a log-normal exceeds its median.
Q: die, $E(1/\xi) = \tfrac16(1+\tfrac12+\cdots+\tfrac16) \approx \mathbf{0.408}$, whereas $1/E\xi = 1/3.5 \approx 0.286$. Not equal.

E2.4  Evaluations Worth Memorizing

$E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.798$,   $Z\sim N(0,1)$
$\xi \ge 0$:   $E\xi = \displaystyle\int_0^{\infty} P(\xi > t)\,dt$  (tail formula)
$\xi\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$:   $E[\xi(\xi-1)] = \lambda^2$
Use when: the tail formula turns an awkward density integral into a survival-function integral — it is how $E[\tau]=\infty$ is shown in §12.5.
Q: $\xi\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)$: $E\xi = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt = \mathbf 1$ with no integration by parts.
Note: $E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}$ reappears as $E[M(t)] = \sqrt{2t/\pi}$ in §13.4, because $M(t)\overset{d}{=}|W(t)|$.
E3.Conditional Probability & Independence

E3.1  Definition

$P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$,   $P(B) > 0$
Use when: conditioning rescales: outcomes outside $B$ get probability $0$, and $B$ becomes the new sample space of total mass $1$.
Q: die, told “even”. $P(\{6\}\mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \tfrac13$, $P(\{\ge4\}\mid B) = \frac{2/6}{1/2} = \tfrac23$.

E3.2  Subset & Disjoint Special Cases

$A\subseteq B$:   $P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}$  (not 1),   $P(B\mid A) = 1$
$A\cap B = \varnothing$:   $P(A\mid B) = 0$
Use when: the $A\subseteq B$ case is a standard trap — conditioning on the larger event makes $A$ more likely, not certain.
Q: $A = \{6\} \subseteq B = \{2,4,6\}$: $P(A\mid B) = \tfrac13$ but $P(B\mid A) = 1$ (a six is always even).

E3.3  Total Probability & Bayes

$P(A) = \displaystyle\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$   ($\{B_i\}$ a partition)
$P(B_j\mid A) = \dfrac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)}$
Use when: the problem describes a two-stage experiment (“pick a machine, then a part”). Write the denominator as the full sum first — that is the whole method.
Q: prevalence 1%, sensitivity 99%, false-positive rate 5%. $P(+) = .99(.01)+.05(.99) = .0594$, so $P(\text{sick}\mid+) = .0099/.0594 \approx \mathbf{0.167}$.
Trick: only ~17% — the base rate dominates, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones.

E3.4  ⭐ Pairwise $\ne$ Mutual Independence

two events: $P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$
$n$ events: the product rule for every sub-collection of size $2,\ldots,n$
that is $2^n - n - 1$ equations ($n{=}3 \to 4$, $n{=}4 \to 11$)
Use when: the question says “mutually independent”. Checking only the pairs is the intended mistake.
Q: two dice. $A$ = first even, $B$ = second even, $C$ = sum even. All three pairs give $\tfrac14 = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$ ✓, but $P(A\cap B\cap C) = \tfrac14 \ne \tfrac18$ ✗.
Trick: any two of them determine the third — that is the giveaway.

E3.5  Disjoint Is the Opposite of Independent

$P(A),P(B) > 0$ and $A\cap B = \varnothing \Rightarrow$ dependent
$A \perp B \Rightarrow A \perp B^c$,   $A^c \perp B$,   $A^c \perp B^c$
Use when: disjoint means knowing $A$ rules out $B$ — maximal dependence, not independence. Students swap these two words constantly.
Proof of the second line: $P(A\cap B^c) = P(A)-P(A\cap B) = P(A)(1-P(B)) = P(A)P(B^c)$ ✓ One line.
E4.Conditional Expectation & σ-Fields

E4.1  It Is a Random Variable

$E(X\mid Y{=}y)$ — a number
$E(X\mid Y) = E(X\mid\sigma(Y))$ — a random variable
$E(X\mid\mathcal G)$ — the general version, $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$
Use when: know which one is being asked for. Martingale theory is entirely about the random-variable version.
Q: two dice, $X$ = sum, $Y$ = first die.
Number: $E(X\mid Y{=}4) = 4 + 3.5 = \mathbf{7.5}$.
Random variable: $E(X\mid Y) = \mathbf{Y + 3.5}$, which equals $7.5$ at $\omega=(4,1)$ and $5.5$ at $\omega=(2,6)$.

E4.2  Defining Properties

(i) $E(X\mid\mathcal G)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable
(ii) $\displaystyle\int_G E(X\mid\mathcal G)\,dP = \int_G X\,dP \quad \forall\,G\in\mathcal G$
unique a.s.
Use when: “define” or “prove” questions. Every rule below is derived from these two, and a proof is always: check (i), then check (ii).
Reading it: (i) says it cannot see finer detail than $\mathcal G$ allows; (ii) says it must have the right average on every block. Together: it is the block-average of $X$.

E4.3  ⭐ Partition Formula

$\mathcal G = \sigma(A_1,\ldots,A_k)$ a partition, $P(A_i)>0$:
$E(X\mid\mathcal G)(\omega) = \dfrac{E[X\,\mathbb 1_{A_i}]}{P(A_i)} = \dfrac{\int_{A_i}X\,dP}{P(A_i)}$   for $\omega\in A_i$
Use when: every finite or discrete problem. No integration — it is the plain average of $X$ over the atom containing $\omega$.
Q: die, $X=\omega$, $\mathcal G = \sigma(\{1,2,3\},\{4,5,6\})$. Low: $\tfrac{1+2+3}{3} = \mathbf 2$. High: $\tfrac{4+5+6}{3} = \mathbf 5$.
Trick: write the atoms, average $X$ inside each, done. Derivation: put $Y = c_i$ on $A_i$ into (ii) and divide by $P(A_i)$.

E4.4  Tower Property

$E\big[E(X\mid\mathcal G)\big] = E(X)$
$\mathcal H \subseteq \mathcal G \Rightarrow E\big[E(X\mid\mathcal G)\mid\mathcal H\big] = E(X\mid\mathcal H)$
Use when: the smaller $\sigma$-field always wins. This one line proves the multi-step martingale property (M2.3) and constant mean (M2.4).
Q: die low/high: $\tfrac12(2)+\tfrac12(5) = 3.5 = EX$ ✓
Q: random coin, bias $p\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, $X$ = heads in 10 tosses. $E(X\mid p) = 10p$, so $E(X) = E[10p] = \mathbf 5$.

E4.5  ⭐ The Three Operational Rules

1. linearity: $E(aX+bY\mid\mathcal G) = aE(X\mid\mathcal G)+bE(Y\mid\mathcal G)$
2. taking out what is known: $Z$ $\mathcal G$-measurable $\Rightarrow E(ZX\mid\mathcal G) = Z\,E(X\mid\mathcal G)$
3. independence: $X \perp \mathcal G \Rightarrow E(X\mid\mathcal G) = E(X)$
Use when: this trio solves every conditional-expectation computation on the paper, including all the Brownian ones in §7.6. Name the rule you are using at each step.
Q: two dice: $E(X_1X_2\mid X_1) = X_1E(X_2) = \mathbf{3.5X_1}$ (rules 2 then 3).
Q: $E\big[(X_1{+}X_2)^2\mid X_1\big] = X_1^2 + 7X_1 + \tfrac{91}{6}$ (expand, then 1, 2, 3).

E4.6  The Two Extremes

$X$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable $\Rightarrow E(X\mid\mathcal G) = X$  (full information)
$\mathcal G = \{\varnothing,\Omega\} \Rightarrow E(X\mid\mathcal G) = E(X)$  (no information)
Use when: every martingale proof is these two extremes plus E4.5. Split $X_{t+1}$ into a known part (extreme 1) and an independent increment (extreme 2), and you are done.
Trick: that split is exactly the increment trick of §6.3, seen from the $\sigma$-field side.

E4.7  Conditional Jensen & Variance Decomposition

$h$ convex $\Rightarrow E[h(X)\mid\mathcal G] \ge h\big(E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big)$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big)$
Use when: conditional Jensen is why $|X_t|$ and $X_t^2$ are sub-martingales whenever $X_t$ is a martingale — a standard T/F.
Q: $X_t$ MG $\Rightarrow E[X_{t+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_t] \ge (E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t])^2 = X_t^2$. So $X_t^2$ is a sub-MG, and $X_t^2 - t$ is the correction that restores the martingale (§7.2).
M1.Filtrations & Adapted Processes

M1.1  Filtration

$\mathcal F_0 \subseteq \mathcal F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathcal F$
$\mathcal F_t$ = everything observable by time $t$
Use when: information only accumulates — the inclusion never reverses. That nesting is what every tower-property argument leans on.
Q: two tosses. $\mathcal F_0 = \{\varnothing,\Omega\}$ (2 events); $\mathcal F_1 = \{\varnothing,\{HH,HT\},\{TH,TT\},\Omega\}$ (4); $\mathcal F_2 = 2^\Omega$ (16).
Note: $\{HT\}\notin\mathcal F_1$ — after one toss you cannot tell $HT$ from $HH$.

M1.2  Adapted

$(X_t)$ is adapted $\iff X_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable for every $t$
atom test: $X_t$ is constant on every atom of $\mathcal F_t$
Use when: “is $X_t$ adapted?” This is F3.4 with $\mathcal G = \mathcal F_t$ — the same test, relabelled.
Q: two tosses. $X_1$ = heads so far: $1,1,0,0$ on $HH,HT,TH,TT$ — constant on each atom ✓ adapted.
$Y_1$ = heads in both tosses: $2,1,1,0$ — differs across $HH$ and $HT$, one atom ✗ not adapted (it needs toss 2).

M1.3  Natural Filtration

$\mathcal F_t = \sigma(X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_t)$
Use when: no filtration is stated. It is the smallest one to which $X$ is adapted, so “adapted” becomes automatic and the real content moves to the martingale equality.
Trick: if the question says “let $\mathcal F_n = \sigma(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n)$”, it has handed you the natural filtration and is telling you not to waste time on condition (i).

M1.4  Functions Preserve Adaptedness

$f$ Borel, $(X_t)$ adapted $\Rightarrow (f(X_t))$ adapted
Use when: justifying that $S_t^2$, $e^{S_t}$, $|S_t|$ etc. are adapted. Same two-line pre-image proof as F4.3.
Trick: $f$ can merge atoms but can never split one, so information can only be lost. That is the whole content.

M1.5  Increments Are NOT $\mathcal F_t$-Measurable

$Y_t := X_{t+1} - X_t \in \mathcal F_{t+1}$,  not $\mathcal F_t$
Use when: this is precisely why a betting stake must be predictable (M7.2) and why $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]$ is a real computation rather than a substitution.
Q: binomial $S_0{=}100$, $u{=}1.2$, $d{=}0.8$. On the atom $A_u = \{uu, ud\}$ of $\mathcal F_1$: $S_2 - S_1 = +24$ on $uu$ but $-24$ on $ud$. Not constant on the atom ✗
M2.Discrete-Time Martingales

M2.1  ⭐ Definition — Three Conditions

(i) $(X_t)$ adapted
(ii) $E|X_t| < \infty$  (integrable)
(iii) $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t] = X_t$   $P$-a.s.
Use when: “Is $X_n$ a martingale? (Yes/No)”. State all three, but (iii) is what carries the marks — show the one-line conditional computation.
Paper: CT-2’25 Q3 — $X_0{=}1$, $X_n = 2^nU_1\cdots U_n$, $U_i\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$.
$E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n] = 2X_n\,E[U_{n+1}] = 2X_n\cdot\tfrac12 = X_n$ ✓ Yes.
Trick: take out $X_n$ (E4.5 rule 2), then independence (rule 3). Two steps, every time.

M2.2  Sub- and Super-Martingales

sub-MG: $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t] \ge X_t$  (favourable to you)
super-MG: $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t] \le X_t$  (unfavourable)
Use when: the naming is counter-intuitive, so fix it once: a SUPERmartingale goes DOWN. Sub goes up.
Q: roulette on red (18/37): $E\eta = -\tfrac1{37}$, a super-MG — the casino’s game, and you lose about 2.7 paise per rupee staked.

M2.3  Multi-Step Property

$E[X_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_t] = X_t$   for every $s \ge 1$
Use when: conditioning across more than one step. Proof is the tower property applied repeatedly, peeling one step at a time.
Proof: $E[X_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_t] = E\big[E[X_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_{t+s-1}]\mid\mathcal F_t\big] = E[X_{t+s-1}\mid\mathcal F_t] = \cdots = X_t$ ✓

M2.4  ⭐ Constant Mean

$E[X_t] = E[X_0]$ for every $t$
Use when: the recurring MCQ. Proof: take $E[\cdot]$ of M2.3 and use the tower property with $\mathcal H = \{\varnothing,\Omega\}$.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q8, all four sets — “Which statement is TRUE?” The correct option is always worded “the mean of a martingale process is constant with respect to time”; only its letter moves: Set-1 (c), Set-2 (a), Set-3 (d), Set-4 (b).
Trick: don’t read the distractors — find that sentence.

M2.5  The Standard Examples

$S_n = \sum_{i\le n} Y_i$, $\;E[Y]=0$  →  MG
$S_n - n\,E[Y]$  →  MG for any i.i.d. $Y$ (drift removed)
$S_n^2 - n\operatorname{Var}(Y)$  →  MG when $E[Y]=0$
$\prod_{i\le n} Y_i$, $\;E[Y]=1$  →  MG
Use when: the first and last are §2.1 / §2.2; the middle two are the discrete twins of $W_t$ and $W_t^2 - t$ (§7.1, §7.2).
Q: biased walk $p=0.6$: $E[Y] = 0.2$, so $S_n$ is not a MG — but $S_n - 0.2n$ is.
Trick: if it is not a martingale, subtract the drift and it becomes one. That is the whole idea behind the $-t$ in $W_t^2-t$.
M3.The Binomial Market Model

M3.1  The Tree

$S_t = v_t S_{t-1}$,   $v_t \in \{u,d\}$,   $0 < d < u$
$\Omega = \{u,d\}^T$;   after $k$ up-moves: $S_t = S_0\,u^{k}d^{\,t-k}$
Use when: draw the tree with numbers before anything else. Almost every error in this topic is a mis-drawn tree, not a mis-applied formula.
Q: $S_0{=}100$, $u{=}1.2$, $d{=}0.8$, $T{=}2$: $S_2 \in \{144, 96, 96, 64\}$ on $uu,ud,du,dd$.

M3.2  Filtration & Recombination

$\mathcal F_t = \sigma(S_0,\ldots,S_t)$;   $2^t$ atoms at time $t$
Use when: the tree recombines ($ud$ and $du$ give the same price) but the filtration does not — $\mathcal F_t$ records the whole path, not just the current price.
Q: above, $S_2 = 96$ on both $ud$ and $du$. $\sigma(S_2)$ alone cannot separate them; $\mathcal F_2 = \sigma(S_0,S_1,S_2)$ can, because $S_1$ differs ($120$ vs $80$).
Trick: that distinction is exactly why path-dependent options exist.

M3.3  Martingale Condition for the Stock ($r = 0$)

$E[S_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t] = S_t\big(pu + (1-p)d\big)$
MG $\iff pu+(1-p)d = 1 \iff p = \dfrac{1-d}{u-d}$
Use when: “for which $p$ is $S_t$ a martingale?” Larger $p$ → sub-MG; smaller → super-MG.
Q: $u{=}1.2,d{=}0.8$: $p = \tfrac{0.2}{0.4} = \mathbf{1/2}$.   $u{=}2,d{=}0.5$: $p = \tfrac{0.5}{1.5} = \mathbf{1/3}$.
Check: $\tfrac13(2)+\tfrac23(0.5) = 1$ ✓

M3.4  ⭐ Risk-Neutral Probability (with interest $r$)

$p^{*} = \dfrac{1+r-d}{u-d}$
$\dfrac{S_t}{(1+r)^t}$ is a martingale under $p^{*}$
Use when: an interest rate is given. $p^{*}$ has nothing to do with the real probability — the real $p$ never enters a price. With $r=0$ this reduces to M3.3.
Paper: CT-2’25 Q1 — $u{=}1.25$, $d{=}0.25$, $r{=}0.15$: $p^{*} = \tfrac{1.15-0.25}{1.00} = \mathbf{0.9}$.

M3.5  Growth of the Mean

$E[S_t] = S_0\big(pu+(1-p)d\big)^{t}$
Use when: “find $E[S_3]$”. Compute $E[v] = pu+(1-p)d$ once, then raise it to the power $t$. Never expand the tree.
Q: $S_0{=}50$, $u{=}1.1$, $d{=}0.9$. $p{=}0.5$: $E[v]=1$, MG, $E[S_3] = \mathbf{50}$.   $p{=}0.7$: $E[v]=1.04$, $E[S_3] = 50(1.04)^3 = \mathbf{56.24}$.
M4.No-Arbitrage Pricing in the Binomial Model

M4.1  No-Arbitrage Condition

$d < 1 + r < u$
Use when: “show the market is arbitrage-free”. If $1+r \le d$ the stock beats the bond for sure; if $1+r \ge u$ the bond beats the stock for sure. Equivalent to $0 < p^{*} < 1$.
Q: Endsem’25 Q5(b)(i) — $S_0{=}5$, $S_1\in\{6, 40/9\}$, $r{=}1/9$. Then $u = 6/5$, $d = 8/9$, $1+r = 10/9$. Check $\tfrac89 < \tfrac{10}9 < \tfrac65$ ✓

M4.2  Risk-Neutral Pricing Formula

$V_0 = \dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{T}}\,E^{*}\big[\text{payoff}\big]$
one period: $V_0 = \dfrac{p^{*}V_u + (1-p^{*})V_d}{1+r}$
Use when: pricing anything. Multi-period: work backwards node by node, discounting one step at a time.
Q: CT-2’25, put with $K{=}225$, $S_0{=}200$, $u{=}1.25$, $d{=}0.25$, $r{=}0.15$.
$V_u = (225-250)^+ = 0$, $V_d = (225-50)^+ = 175$, $p^{*}=0.9$.
$V_0 = \tfrac{0.9(0)+0.1(175)}{1.15} = \tfrac{17.5}{1.15} = \mathbf{15.22}$ ✓

M4.3  ⭐ Replicating Portfolio

$\Delta = \dfrac{V_u - V_d}{(u-d)\,S_0}$  (shares)
$B = \dfrac{u\,V_d - d\,V_u}{(1+r)(u-d)}$  (cash / bond)
$V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$
Use when: “construct the replicating portfolio, of the form $(\Delta, B)$”. $\Delta$ is the hedge ratio; it is negative for a put.
Paper: CT-2’25 Q1 — put, $V_u{=}0$, $V_d{=}175$, $u-d = 1$, $S_0{=}200$, $r{=}0.15$.
$\Delta = \tfrac{0-175}{1\cdot200} = \mathbf{-0.875}$;   $B = \tfrac{1.25(175)-0.25(0)}{1.15(1)} = \tfrac{218.75}{1.15} = \mathbf{190.22}$.
Answer $(\Delta,B) = (-0.875,\,190.22)$ ✓ and $V_0 = -0.875(200)+190.22 = \mathbf{15.22}$ ✓

M4.4  Two Routes, One Answer

$\Delta S_0 + B \;=\; \dfrac{p^{*}V_u + (1-p^{*})V_d}{1+r}$
Use when: they are algebraically the same. Use replication when the question asks for $(\Delta,B)$; use risk-neutral when it asks only for the price — and use the other one to check.
Trick: in a two-part question (“price it”, then “replicate it”) do replication first — you get the price for free from $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$.

M4.5  ⭐ The “no sure win” / arbitrage-$c$ question

$S_0$ today; at time 1 either $S_u$ or $S_d$ (already present values)
option to buy $y$ shares at price $Ky$ costs $cy$
1. $\;p = \dfrac{S_0 - S_d}{S_u - S_d}$
2. payoffs $(S_u-K)^{+}$ and $(S_d-K)^{+}$
3. $\;c^{*} = p\,(S_u-K)^{+} + (1-p)(S_d-K)^{+}$
no sure win $\iff c = c^{*}$;   arbitrage $\iff c \ne c^{*}$
Use when: this exact question has appeared in three consecutive papers. Prices are given as present values, so there is no discounting — three lines, no $r$.
Paper — all three instances:
Midsem’25 Q1(c): $50\to\{25,100\}$, $K{=}85$. $p = \tfrac{25}{75}=\tfrac13$, payoff $15$ or $0$, $c^{*}=\mathbf 5$. Key: “$c \ne 5$” ✓
CT-2’25 Q5: $40\to\{25,100\}$, $K{=}75$. $p = \tfrac{15}{75}=\tfrac15$, payoff $25$ or $0$, $c = \mathbf 5$ ✓
Midsem’24 Q1(a): $75\to\{50,200\}$, $K{=}120$. $p = \tfrac{25}{150}=\tfrac16$, payoff $80$ or $0$, $c = \tfrac{80}{6} = \mathbf{13.33}$

M4.6  Put–Call Parity

$C - P = S_0 - \dfrac{K}{(1+r)^{T}}$
Same strike, same maturity, European.
Use when: the paper asks for both a call and a put on the same tree (Endsem’25 Q4 does). Price one properly, get the other in one line instead of rebuilding the tree.
Proof in one line: the portfolios “call + cash $K/(1+r)^T$” and “put + one share” have identical payoff $\max(S_T,K)$ at $T$, so they must cost the same today.

M4.7  Self-Financing

$\Delta_t S_{t+1} + B_t(1+r) \;=\; \Delta_{t+1}S_{t+1} + B_{t+1}$
Use when: “verify the replicating strategy is self-financing”. Meaning: at each rebalancing date the new portfolio costs exactly what the old one is now worth — no money in, none out.
Paper: Endsem’25 Q4(iii).
Trick: compute the old portfolio’s value at the new prices, then the new portfolio’s cost, and show they match at every node.
M5.Stopping Times

M5.1  Definition

$\tau : \Omega \to \{0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}$ with
$\{\tau \le t\} \in \mathcal F_t$   for every $t$
Use when: plain meaning: at time $t$ you can already tell whether you have stopped, using only what you have seen. No peeking at the future. $\infty$ is allowed — the event may never happen.
Q: three tosses, $\tau$ = first H. $\{\tau\le1\} = H\_\_ \in\mathcal F_1$ ✓, $\{\tau\le2\} = H\_\_\cup TH\_ \in\mathcal F_2$ ✓ Yes.
Q: $\tau = 1$ if toss 2 is H else $2$: $\{\tau\le1\} = \{\_H\_\}\notin\mathcal F_1$ ✗ No.

M5.2  Equivalent Characterisation

$\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t\ \forall t \iff \{\tau = t\}\in\mathcal F_t\ \forall t$
Use when: $\{\tau = t\}$ is almost always the easier one to write down. Prove the equivalence with the two identities below — it is a standard 3-mark part.
($\Rightarrow$) $\{\tau=t\} = \{\tau\le t\}\cap\{\tau\le t-1\}^{c}$, both in $\mathcal F_t$ by nesting.
($\Leftarrow$) $\{\tau\le t\} = \bigcup_{s=0}^{t}\{\tau=s\}$, a finite union of $\mathcal F_t$-sets ✓

M5.3  First Entry Time Is a Stopping Time

$\tau = \min\{n : X_n \in B\}$   is a ST for adapted $(X_n)$
$\{\tau = n\} = \{X_1\notin B\}\cap\cdots\cap\{X_{n-1}\notin B\}\cap\{X_n\in B\}$
Use when: covers every $\tau$ on this paper — first hit of a level, first hit of a moving line, ruin time. Each piece of the intersection sits in $\mathcal F_n$ by adaptedness plus nesting.
Q: walk with steps $+1,-1,+1,+1$, $B=\{2\}$: $X = 1,0,1,2$ so $\tau = \mathbf 4$. At time 4 you check only $X_1,\ldots,X_4$ ✓
Steps $-1,-1,-1,\ldots$ never reach 2, so $\tau = \infty$ — which is why $\infty$ is in the codomain.

M5.4  ⭐ Last Exit Time Is NOT a Stopping Time

$\tau = \max\{n : X_n\in B\}$   is not a ST
Use when: the standard counterexample question. Also not stopping times: “the time at which $X_n$ attains its maximum over $n\le N$”, “the day before the crash”. All need the future.
Q: three-step walk, $\tau$ = last $n\le3$ with $X_n = 1$.
Steps $(+1,-1,-1)$: $X = 1,0,-1$, so $\tau = 1$.   Steps $(+1,-1,+1)$: $X = 1,0,1$, so $\tau = 3$.
Both begin $+1$, so they lie in the same atom of $\mathcal F_1$, yet $\tau$ differs $\Rightarrow \{\tau=1\}\notin\mathcal F_1$ ✗
Trick: exhibit two paths agreeing up to time $t$ with different $\tau$. That is a complete proof.

M5.5  Closure Operations

$\tau_1\wedge\tau_2$ ✓   $\tau_1\vee\tau_2$ ✓   $\tau+1$ ✓   $\tau\wedge c$ ✓
$\tau - 1$ ✗  (stopping one step before something you cannot yet see)
Use when: $\tau\wedge c$ is the workhorse — it is how you manufacture the bounded stopping time that the Optional Stopping Theorem requires (M6.3).
Proofs, one line each: $\{\tau_1\wedge\tau_2\le t\} = \{\tau_1\le t\}\cup\{\tau_2\le t\}$;   $\{\tau_1\vee\tau_2\le t\} = \{\tau_1\le t\}\cap\{\tau_2\le t\}$;   $\{\tau+1\le t\} = \{\tau\le t-1\}\in\mathcal F_{t-1}\subseteq\mathcal F_t$.
M6.Stopped Process & Optional Stopping

M6.1  The Stopped Process

$Z_t = X_{t\wedge\tau} = \begin{cases} X_t & \tau > t \ \text{(running)}\\ X_\tau & \tau \le t \ \text{(frozen)}\end{cases}$
$Z_{t+1} = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{t} X_k\mathbb 1_{\{\tau=k\}} + X_{t+1}\mathbb 1_{\{\tau>t\}}$
Use when: the explicit formula is the one you differentiate the proof from — every term in it is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable except $X_{t+1}$.
Q: steps $+1,+1,-1,+1$ so $X = 0,1,2,1,2$; $\tau$ = first hit of 2, so $\tau=2$.
$Z = 0,1,2,2,2$. After $\tau$, $Z$ ignores $X$: $X_3 = 1$ but $Z_3 = 2$.

M6.2  Preservation Theorem

$(X_t)$ MG $\Longrightarrow (X_{t\wedge\tau})$ MG
sub-MG $\to$ sub-MG;   super-MG $\to$ super-MG  (type is inherited)
Use when: the key step is $\mathbb 1_{\{\tau>t\}} \in \mathcal F_t$, which holds because $\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ — this is the only place the stopping-time property is used. Say that sentence.
Proof: pull the $\mathcal F_t$-measurable pieces out of M6.1, then $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t] = X_t$ gives
$\sum_{k\le t}X_k\mathbb 1_{\{\tau=k\}} + \mathbb 1_{\{\tau>t\}}X_t = Z_t$ ✓

M6.3  ⭐ Optional Stopping Theorem

$(X_t)$ MG, $\tau$ a bounded ST ($\tau\le c$ a.s.) $\Longrightarrow E[X_\tau] = E[X_0]$
Use when: no stopping rule beats a fair game. Its power on this paper: it converts an unknown distribution of $\tau$ into one line of algebra.
Four-line proof: 1. $Z_t = X_{t\wedge\tau}$ is a MG (M6.2). 2. So $E[Z_t] = E[Z_0] = E[X_0]$. 3. Put $t = c$; since $\tau\le c$, $Z_c = X_\tau$. 4. Hence $E[X_\tau] = E[X_0]$ ∎

M6.4  ⚠ When OST Fails

boundedness of $\tau$ is essential
Use when: if your algebra returns something impossible — a negative expected time, or $E[X_\tau]\ne E[X_0]$ — the hypothesis failed, not the arithmetic. Check before you write.
Counterexample: symmetric walk from $0$, $\tau$ = first hit of $+1$. Then $X_\tau = 1$ always, so $E[X_\tau] = 1 \ne 0$. $\tau$ is finite a.s. but $E[\tau] = \infty$.
Q: $\tau = \min\{t: W_t = 1+2t\}$ would give $E[\tau] = -\tfrac12 < 0$. Impossible — the line escapes upward and $P(\tau=\infty)>0$.

M6.5  OST Recipe #1 — hitting a moving line

$\tau = \min\{t : W_t = a + bt\}$
$E[W_\tau] = 0$ and $W_\tau = a + b\tau \;\Rightarrow\; E[\tau] = -\dfrac{a}{b}$
Needs $ab < 0$.
Use when: three lines: apply OST, substitute the barrier equation, solve. See also §10.7.
Paper: Endsem’25 Q1(d) — $W_t = 2 - 3t$: $0 = 2 - 3E[T]$, so $E[T] = \mathbf{2/3}$.
Q: $W_t = 3 - 6t$ gives $E[\tau] = \mathbf{1/2}$.

M6.6  ⭐ OST Recipe #2 — gambler’s ruin with two martingales

walk from $0$, absorbed at $+A$ or $-B$:
use $X_n$:   $Ap - B(1-p) = 0 \Rightarrow p = \dfrac{B}{A+B}$
use $X_n^2 - n$:   $E[\tau] = E[X_\tau^2] = A^2p + B^2(1-p) = A\,B$
Use when: both parts of the ruin question in one framework. $E[\tau]$ always collapses to the product $AB$ — compute it either way as a check.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q10 — stop at $+10$ or $-5$, so $A{=}10$, $B{=}5$.
$p = \tfrac{5}{15} = \mathbf{1/3}$ ✓  |  $E[\tau] = 100(\tfrac13)+25(\tfrac23) = \mathbf{50}$ ✓ $= 10\times5$ ✓
Set-2 ($20,5$): $\tfrac15$, $100$. Set-3 ($30,10$): $\tfrac14$, $300$. Set-4 ($6,5$): $\tfrac5{11}$, $30$. All match §3.1–3.2.

M6.7  Justifying an Unbounded $\tau$

$\tau$ unbounded but $X_{t\wedge\tau}$ bounded (trapped between barriers)
$\Rightarrow$ dominated convergence lets you pass $t\to\infty$ in $E[X_{t\wedge\tau}] = E[X_0]$
Use when: the gambler’s-ruin $\tau$ is not bounded, so M6.3 does not literally apply. Write this one sentence and the marks are safe.
Trick: the path is confined to $[-B, A]$, which is exactly the domination you need. Contrast M6.4, where the walk can sink arbitrarily low — no bound, no theorem.
M7.Gambling Strategies & Fairness

M7.1  The Game Process

$\eta_n$ = winnings per unit stake in game $n$;   $\Xi_n = \sum_{k\le n}\eta_k$;   $\mathcal F_n = \sigma(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n)$
fair $\iff$ MG  |  favourable $\iff$ sub-MG  |  unfavourable $\iff$ super-MG
Use when: the dictionary between the gambling words and the martingale words. Translate first, then it is M2.
Q: roulette on red, 18 of 37 pockets: $E\eta = \tfrac{18}{37}-\tfrac{19}{37} = -\tfrac1{37} \approx -0.027$. Super-MG — you lose 2.7 paise per rupee staked, whatever you do.

M7.2  ⭐ Predictable Strategy

the stake $\alpha_n$ must be $\mathcal F_{n-1}$-measurable  (predictable)
Use when: the whole subject rests on this. $\mathcal F_n$-measurable would mean placing the bet after seeing the outcome. Say why, not just what.
Allowed ✓: $\alpha_n = 2$ if you won game $n{-}1$ else $1$; $\alpha_n = n$; doubling after each loss; $\alpha_n = |\Xi_{n-1}|+1$.
Forbidden ✗: $\alpha_n = \mathbb 1\{\eta_n = +1\}$ (“bet only on games you will win”) — it gives $E[Z_1] = \tfrac12 > 0$ in a fair game. Predictability is exactly what rules it out.

M7.3  Wealth Process (martingale transform)

$Z_n = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_k\,\eta_k = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_k(\Xi_k - \Xi_{k-1})$
recursive: $Z_n = Z_{n-1} + \alpha_n(\Xi_n - \Xi_{n-1})$
Use when: this is the discrete Itô integral — a predictable integrand against a martingale increment. §7.5 is the same statement in continuous time.
Q: doubling after each loss, results $+1,-1,-1,+1$: stakes $1,1,2,4$, gains $+1,-1,-2,+4$, so $Z = 1,0,-2,\mathbf 2$.

M7.4  Preservation Theorem

$\Xi$ MG, $\alpha$ predictable and bounded $\Rightarrow Z$ MG
$\Xi$ sub-MG, $\alpha$ predictable, bounded and $\alpha_n \ge 0$ $\Rightarrow Z$ sub-MG
Use when: “why is $\alpha_n\ge0$ needed?” is a standard follow-up. Because a negative stake against a favourable game turns a positive expected gain into a loss.
Proof: $E(Z_n\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}) = Z_{n-1} + \alpha_n\underbrace{E(\Xi_n-\Xi_{n-1}\mid\mathcal F_{n-1})}_{=\,0} = Z_{n-1}$ ✓
Both $Z_{n-1}$ and $\alpha_n$ pull out because both are $\mathcal F_{n-1}$-measurable — that is where predictability is used.

M7.5  You Cannot Beat a Fair Game

$E[Z_n] = 0$ for every predictable bounded strategy
Use when: a betting system changes the shape of the payoff distribution, never its mean. That sentence is the answer to most conceptual parts here.
Q: doubling until the first win, capped at 3 games. Win $+1$ with probability $\tfrac78$; lose $1{+}2{+}4 = 7$ with probability $\tfrac18$. $E = \tfrac78 - \tfrac78 = \mathbf 0$ ✓
Trick: a frequent small win paid for by a rare huge loss. Uncapped it appears to beat the game — but then $\tau$ is unbounded and M6.3 does not apply.

M7.6  American vs European (short-note)

European: exercise only at $T$.   American: exercise at any ST $\tau \le T$.
American $\ge$ European;   equal for a call on a non-dividend stock
Use when: the short-note question. Say why they are equal for a call: the discounted price is a MG, early exercise throws away time value, and OST says no stopping rule improves a fair game. For a put early exercise can be optimal.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q2(a) — “short note on American and European call options” [2 marks].
Trick: definition, the inequality, the call-equality with its one-line reason, and the put caveat. Four sentences.

1.Normal & MVN Toolkit — the plug-in layer

1.1  Standard Normal cdf

$\Phi(-x) = 1 - \Phi(x), \qquad \Phi(0) = 0.5$
$P(a < Z \le b) = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a)$
Use when: always. Standardize first: $X \sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) \Rightarrow P(X\le x) = \Phi\!\big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$.
Table values worth memorizing: $\Phi(1){=}.8413$, $\Phi(1.5){=}.9332$, $\Phi(2){=}.9772$, $\Phi(2.5){=}.9938$, $\Phi(3){=}.9987$.

1.2  Exponential Moment (log-normal mean)

$X \sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) \;\Longrightarrow\; E[e^{X}] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$
$E[e^{aZ}] = e^{a^2/2}, \qquad Z\sim N(0,1)$
Use when: an exponential of a normal appears — GBM means, $\operatorname{Cov}(e^{W_s},e^{W_t})$, exponential martingale, Black–Scholes.
Trick: the whole GBM section is this one identity. $E[e^{W_t}] = e^{t/2}$ because $W_t\sim N(0,t)$.

1.3  Log-normal Variance

$\operatorname{Var}(e^X) = e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}\big(e^{\sigma^2}-1\big)$
$X \sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$.
Use when: asked for $\operatorname{Var}$ or $\operatorname{Cov}$ of a GBM / of $e^{W_t}$.
Q: $Y=e^{W_2}$, find $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$. Here $\mu{=}0,\sigma^2{=}2$: $e^{2}(e^{2}-1)=\mathbf{47.21}$.
Trick: $\operatorname{Var} = (\text{mean})^2(e^{\sigma^2}-1)$ — compute the mean first, square it.

1.4  Even Moments of a Normal

$E[Z^{2n}] = (2n-1)!! = 1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n-1)$
$E[Z]{=}0,\ E[Z^2]{=}1,\ E[Z^3]{=}0,\ E[Z^4]{=}3$
$E[W_t^{2n}] = (2n-1)!!\,t^{\,n}, \qquad E[W_t^4] = 3t^2$
Use when: any odd power → $0$ by symmetry. Any 4th moment → $3t^2$.
Paper: CT-1 prints “$E(Z^3)=0$, $E(Z^4)=3$” at the top of the sheet — they expect you to need it in Q2.

1.5  MVN — Definition by Linear Combinations

$\mathbf{X}$ is MVN $\iff$ $\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} = \sum_i a_i X_i$ is univariate normal for every $\mathbf{a}$
Use when: proving something is a Gaussian process. This is the definition — do not try to write a joint density.
Q: CT-1’26 Q3 — is $e^{-t}W(e^{2t})$ a Gaussian process?
Trick: it is a fixed linear combination of BM values, so every $\sum a_i X(t_i)$ is normal. Yes, with zero computation.

1.6  Variance of a Linear Combination

$\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\sum_i a_i X_i\Big) = \sum_i\sum_j a_i a_j \operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)$
Use when: the master identity for every “$aW_s+bW_t\sim\,$?” question. Plug $\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t)$.
Q: $\operatorname{Var}(W_1 - W_2 + 3W_4)$.
Trick: write the $\min$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&2\\1&2&4\end{pmatrix}$, sandwich with $\mathbf a = (1,-1,3)$ → $\mathbf{31}$.

1.7  MVN Linear Transformation

$\mathbf{X}\sim N_n(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma) \Rightarrow A\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{b}\sim N_m(A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{b},\,A\Sigma A^{\!\top})$
Use when: a process is built as a fixed matrix times BM values — e.g. the Brownian bridge $B(s)=W(s)-\frac st W(t)$.
Q: show $B(s) = W(s) - \tfrac st W(t)$ is Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov} = \min(u,v)-\tfrac{uv}{t}$.
Trick: $B$ is a fixed matrix times $(W(s),W(t))$, so MVN is automatic — then just compute $A\Sigma A^{\!\top}$.

1.8  MVN Joint MGF

$M_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf t) = \exp\!\big(\mathbf t^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\mu + \tfrac12 \mathbf t^{\!\top}\Sigma\,\mathbf t\big)$
Use when: you need $E[e^{W_s+W_t}]$ — set $\mathbf t = (1,1)$. Fastest route to $\operatorname{Cov}(e^{W_s},e^{W_t})$.
Q: $E[e^{W_1+W_2}]$.  $W_1+W_2 \sim N(0,\,1+2+2(1)) = N(0,5)$ → $\mathbf{e^{5/2}}$.
Trick: set $\mathbf t = (1,1)$; the MGF hands you $\exp(\tfrac12\sum_i\sum_j\Sigma_{ij})$ in one step.

1.9  MVN Conditional Distribution

$\mathbf X \mid \mathbf Y{=}\mathbf y \sim N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_X + \Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}(\mathbf y - \boldsymbol\mu_Y),\ \Sigma_{XX} - \Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\big)$
Use when: any “given $W(t)=b$, find the law of $W(s)$”. Conditional variance never depends on the observed value.
Trick: for BM this collapses to §9.4 — don’t invert matrices, quote the bridge result.
2.Random Walks — Additive & Geometric

2.1  Additive Random Walk

$X_0 = 0,\quad X_n = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n,\quad Y_i \text{ i.i.d.}$
$\{X_n\}$ is a martingale $\iff E[Y_1] = 0$
Needs $E|Y_1| < \infty$.
Use when: the walk is built by adding i.i.d. steps. Sub-MG if $E[Y]>0$, super-MG if $E[Y]<0$.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q1(b) — “$\{X_n\}$ is a martingale if $E(Y_1) = \underline{\ \ }$” → $0$.

2.2  Geometric Random Walk

$X_n = X_0 \prod_{i=1}^{n} Y_i, \quad Y_i > 0 \text{ i.i.d.}$
$\{X_n\}$ is a martingale $\iff E[Y_1] = 1$
Use when: the walk multiplies. Proof is one line: $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n] = X_n E[Y_{n+1}]$ by independence + taking out what is known.
Paper: CT-2’25 Q3 — $X_n = 2^n U_1\cdots U_n$, $U_i\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$. Step is $Y_i = 2U_i$, $E[2U]=2\cdot\tfrac12=1$ ⇒ Yes, MG.

2.3  Log-normal Steps — Drift Correction

$Y_i = e^{Z_i},\ Z_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) \Rightarrow E[Y_i] = e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}$
martingale $\iff \mu = -\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2}$
Use when: geometric walk with normal log-steps. The $-\sigma^2/2$ is the same correction that appears in GBM (§11.4) and Black–Scholes.
Q: log-price steps $Z_i \sim N(\mu, 0.04)$; for what $\mu$ is the price a martingale? → $\mu = \mathbf{-0.02}$.
Trick: this $-\sigma^2/2$ reappears in GBM (§11.4) and as $d_2$ in Black–Scholes. Same correction, three places.

2.4  Moments of a Symmetric $\pm1$ Walk

$E[X_n] = 0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_n) = n$
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_m, X_n) = \min(m,n)$
Use when: discrete analogue of $\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t)$. Same proof: overlap of independent increments.
Q: $\operatorname{Cov}(X_3, X_7)$ for a $\pm1$ walk $= \mathbf{3}$.
Trick: only the shared first $\min(m,n)$ steps contribute; everything after is independent and mean zero.
3.Gambler’s Ruin — probability and duration

3.0  Setting Up the Word Problem

“stops when up $\,W\,$ or down $\,L$” $\;\Longrightarrow\; i = L,\quad N = L + W$
Use when: the exam always phrases it as “won $W$ rupees or lost $L$ rupees”, never as “start at $i$, barriers $0$ and $N$”. Do this translation first — it is where marks are lost.
Paper: CT-1’26 Set-1 — “won 10 or lost 5” ⇒ $i=5$, $N=15$. Set-3 — “won 30 or lost 10” ⇒ $i=10$, $N=40$.

3.1  Ruin Probability — Fair Game

$P_i = P(\text{hit } N \text{ before } 0 \mid X_0 = i) = \dfrac{i}{N}$
$p = \tfrac12$.
Use when: “equal chances”, “fair coin”, “wins or loses one rupee with equal probability”.
Paper: CT-1’26 all four sets. Set-1: $5/15 = \mathbf{1/3}$. Set-2 (won 20, lost 5): $5/25 = \mathbf{1/5}$. Set-3: $10/40=\mathbf{1/4}$. Set-4 (won 6, lost 5): $\mathbf{5/11}$.

3.2  Expected Duration — Fair Game

$D_i = E[\text{number of rounds}] = i\,(N - i)$
$p=\tfrac12$. Equivalently $D = L \cdot W$ in the word-problem variables.
Use when: part (b) of the ruin question — “expected number of rounds before stopping”. It is always asked alongside 3.1.
Paper: CT-1’26 Set-1: $5\times10 = \mathbf{50}$. Set-2: $5\times20=\mathbf{100}$. Set-3: $10\times30=\mathbf{300}$. Set-4: $5\times6=\mathbf{30}$. Trick: it is literally $L\times W$ — no formula needed.

3.3  Ruin Probability — Unfair Game

$P_i = \dfrac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^N}, \qquad q = 1-p,\ p\neq\tfrac12$
Use when: win probability is given as anything other than $1/2$ (e.g. $2/3$ and $1/3$). Let $r = q/p$ and evaluate numerically — do not expand.
Q: $p = 2/3$, stop at $+3$ or $-2$. Translate: $i{=}2$, $N{=}5$, $r = q/p = \tfrac12$.
$P = \dfrac{1-(1/2)^2}{1-(1/2)^5} = \dfrac{0.75}{0.96875} = \mathbf{0.774}$.
Trick: compute $r$ once, then it is two powers and a division.

3.4  Expected Duration — Unfair Game

$D_i = \dfrac{i}{q-p} - \dfrac{N}{q-p}\cdot\dfrac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^N}$
$= \dfrac{1}{q-p}\big(i - N P_i\big)$
Use when: duration with $p\ne\tfrac12$. Trick: compute $P_i$ first (3.3), then this is one subtraction.
Q: same numbers. $q-p = -\tfrac13$, so $D = \dfrac{2 - 5(0.774)}{-1/3} = \mathbf{5.61}$ rounds.
Trick: always get $P_i$ from 3.3 first — never expand the full expression.

3.5  Infinite Bankroll Limit

$\lim_{N\to\infty} P_i = \begin{cases} 1-(q/p)^i & p > \tfrac12 \\[2pt] 0 & p \le \tfrac12\end{cases}$
Use when: “plays forever against an infinitely rich opponent” / “$N\to\infty$”. Ruin is a.s. certain unless there is a genuine edge.
Q: “plays forever against the house with $p = 0.49$, starting with 10”. $p < \tfrac12$ → ruin probability $\mathbf{1}$.
Trick: any fair-or-worse game against an infinite bankroll ends in ruin almost surely. No arithmetic needed.

3.6  Derivation Hook (first-step analysis)

$P_i = p\,P_{i+1} + q\,P_{i-1}, \quad P_0 = 0,\ P_N = 1$
$D_i = 1 + p\,D_{i+1} + q\,D_{i-1}, \quad D_0 = D_N = 0$
Use when: the question says “derive” or “using first-step analysis” (Midsem’25 Q2(c) asks for a short note on gambler’s ruin). The $+1$ in the duration recursion is the round just played.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q2(c) — “write a short note on gambler’s ruin” [2 marks].
Trick: state the recursion, state the boundary conditions, then quote 3.1/3.3 as its solution. That is the whole answer.
4.Random Walk → Brownian Motion (scaling limit)

4.1  Diffusive Scaling — Driftless

steps $\pm\Delta x$ every $\Delta t$, $\ \Delta x = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$
$X_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor} \;\xrightarrow[\Delta t\to0]{d}\; \sigma W(t)$
Use when: a limit of a symmetric walk is taken. The only scaling that survives is space $\sim \sqrt{\text{time}}$ — that is what forces $\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=t$.
Q: “why is $\operatorname{Var}(W_t) = t$ and not $t^2$?”
Trick: $n = t/\Delta t$ steps of size $\Delta x$ give variance $t(\Delta x)^2/\Delta t$. That is finite and non-zero only if $\Delta x \propto \sqrt{\Delta t}$. Everything else degenerates.

4.2  Scaling with Drift

$p = \tfrac12\Big(1 + \dfrac{\mu}{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}\Big),\quad \text{steps } \pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$
$\Longrightarrow\ X(t) \to \mu t + \sigma W(t)$
Use when: the question hands you a walk with $p$ depending on $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and says “argue the limit is BM with drift $\mu$”.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q3(b) — steps $\pm\sqrt{\Delta t}$, $p=\tfrac12(1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t})$ (so $\sigma=1$).
Two-line proof: per-step mean $=\sqrt{\Delta t}(2p-1) = \mu\,\Delta t$; per-step variance $=\Delta t\big(1-(2p-1)^2\big) \to \Delta t$. Over $n=t/\Delta t$ steps: mean $\to\mu t$, variance $\to t$. CLT finishes it.
5.Wiener Process — Axioms & Identification Tests

5.1  Wiener Process Axioms

(i) $W(0) = 0$
(ii) independent increments
(iii) $W(t) - W(s) \sim N(0,\,t-s)$ for $s<t$  (stationary)
(iv) $t \mapsto W(t)$ continuous a.s.
Use when: the four-item checklist for “short note” questions and for verifying a constructed process.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q2 / Endsem — “short note on the Wiener process”.
Trick: write the four axioms, then add $\operatorname{Cov}=\min(s,t)$ and “continuous but nowhere differentiable” for the extra mark.

5.2  Is $X(t)$ a Wiener Process? — 4-point test

1. $X(0) = 0$ ?
2. $X$ Gaussian (all f.d.d. normal) ?
3. $E[X(t)] = 0$ ?
4. $\operatorname{Cov}(X(s),X(t)) = \min(s,t)$ ?
Use when: T/F questions of the form “$V(t) = \ldots$ is also a Wiener process”. Checking $\min(s,t)$ is usually the deciding step; $X(0)=0$ is the usual trap.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q1(e) — $V(t)=\tfrac1c W(c^2t)$, $c>0$. $V(0)=0$ ✓; $\operatorname{Cov} = \tfrac1{c^2}\min(c^2s,c^2t)=\min(s,t)$ ✓ ⇒ True.

5.3  Is $X(t)$ a Gaussian Process? — weaker test

Yes $\iff$ every finite $\sum a_i X(t_i)$ is normal
$f(t)\,W(g(t))$ with $f,g$ deterministic $\Rightarrow$ always Gaussian
Use when: the question asks Gaussian process, not Wiener. A deterministic scaling and a deterministic time change can never destroy jointly-normal structure — the answer is Yes without computation.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q3, all four sets — $e^{-t}W(e^{2t})$, $e^{-2t}W(e^{2t})$, $e^{-t}W(e^{t})$, $e^{-3t}W(e^{2t})$. Every answer is Yes. Not Wiener though: $X(0) = W(1) \ne 0$.

5.4  The Four Invariances of BM

Scaling: $\tfrac1c W(c^2 t)$
Symmetry: $-W(t)$
Time inversion: $t\,W(1/t)$ for $t>0$, $\;0$ at $t=0$
Time reversal on $[0,T]$: $W(T) - W(T-t)$
Use when: all four are standard BM. Anything else in a T/F question is probably not — run §5.2.
Q: “Is $-W(t)$ a Wiener process? Is $tW(1/t)$?” — both Yes.
Trick: memorize these four outright. A T/F on anything else means you must run the §5.2 checklist.

5.5  Ornstein–Uhlenbeck from BM

$X(t) = e^{-\lambda t}W\!\big(e^{2\lambda t}\big)$
$\operatorname{Cov}(X(s),X(t)) = e^{-\lambda|t-s|}$
Use when: covariance depends on $|t-s|$ only ⇒ stationary Gaussian process (OU), not Wiener. Wiener needs $\min(s,t)$.
Trick: $\operatorname{Cov} = e^{-\lambda(s+t)}\min(e^{2\lambda s},e^{2\lambda t}) = e^{-\lambda(s+t)}e^{2\lambda \min(s,t)} = e^{-\lambda|t-s|}$.

5.6  Lévy’s Martingale Characterization

$X$ continuous, $X(0)=0$, $X_t$ and $X_t^2 - t$ both martingales $\Longrightarrow X$ is standard BM
Use when: you must prove BM-ness without touching distributions. Two martingale checks replace the whole axiom list.
Q: “prove the scaled random-walk limit is Brownian motion”.
Trick: Lévy lets you skip distributions entirely — show continuity, $X_0 = 0$, and that $X_t$ and $X_t^2 - t$ are both martingales.

5.7  Path Properties (short-note ammo)

Continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere (a.s.)
Quadratic variation on $[0,t]$ $= t$, i.e. $(dW)^2 = dt$
Total variation $=\infty$; Markov; strong Markov
Use when: “write a short note”. $(dW)^2 = dt$ is the single fact that powers all of Itô’s formula.
Q: Endsem-style — “explain quadratic variation”.
Trick: say the sentence “$(dW)^2 = dt$ is why Itô’s formula carries the $\tfrac12 f''$ term at all”. That one link is what they are marking.
6.Covariance & Moments — the $\min(s,t)$ machine

6.1  Covariance of BM

$\operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = E[W_s W_t] = \min(s,t)$
Use when: the single most-used identity in the paper. Derivation: for $s<t$, $E[W_sW_t] = E[W_s(W_s + (W_t-W_s))] = s + 0$.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q1(d) — $\operatorname{Var}(W_4)\times\operatorname{Cov}(W_1,W_4) = 4 \times 1 = \mathbf{4}$.

6.2  Linear Combination of BM Values

$\sum_i a_i W_{t_i} \sim N\!\Big(0,\ \sum_i\sum_j a_i a_j \min(t_i,t_j)\Big)$
two terms: $\operatorname{Var}(aW_s + bW_t) = a^2 s + b^2 t + 2ab\min(s,t)$
Use when: “$4W_1 + 6W_3 \sim\ ?$”. Trick: build the $\min$ matrix, then it is one dot product. Mean is always $0$.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q4 — $4W_1+6W_3$: $16(1)+36(3)+2(24)(1) = 16+108+48 = \mathbf{N(0,172)}$. Set-2 $3W_1{+}6W_3\!: \mathbf{153}$; Set-3 $4W_1{+}5W_3\!: \mathbf{131}$; Set-4 $4W_1{+}7W_3\!: \mathbf{219}$.
Midsem’25 Q1(d) — $W_1 - W_2 + 3W_4$: $1+2+36-2(1)+6(1)-6(2) = \mathbf{N(0,31)}$.

6.3  Increment Splitting — the universal trick

$s<t:\quad W_t = W_s + \underbrace{(W_t - W_s)}_{\Delta \,\sim\, N(0,\,t-s),\ \perp\ \mathcal F_s}$
Use when: any mixed moment $E[g(W_s)h(W_t)]$. Substitute, expand, kill odd powers of $\Delta$, use independence. This replaces every 2-D integral.
Q: $E[W_2 W_3^3]$. Put $W_3 = W_2 + \Delta$, expand: $E[W_2^4] + 3E[W_2^2]E[\Delta^2] = 3(4) + 3(2)(1) = \mathbf{18}$.
General: $E[W_sW_t^3] = 3st$ for $s\le t$. Trick: every odd power of $\Delta$ dies.

6.4  Mixed Fourth Moment

$E[W_s^2 W_t^2] = 2s^2 + st = s(2s+t), \qquad s \le t$
Use when: exactly this question. Order matters — $s$ is the smaller index. Derivation: $E[W_s^2(W_s+\Delta)^2] = E[W_s^4] + E[W_s^2]E[\Delta^2] = 3s^2 + s(t-s)$.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q2 — $E(W_2^2W_3^2)=2(4)+6=\mathbf{14}$; $E(W_1^2W_3^2)=2+3=\mathbf{5}$; $E(W_2^2W_4^2)=8+8=\mathbf{16}$; $E(W_1^2W_4^2)=2+4=\mathbf{6}$.

6.5  Covariance of Exponentials

$\operatorname{Cov}\big(e^{W_s}, e^{W_t}\big) = e^{(s+t)/2}\Big(e^{\min(s,t)} - 1\Big)$
Use when: $Y_t := e^{W_t}$, asked for $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_s,Y_t)$. Trick: $W_s+W_t\sim N(0,\,s+t+2\min(s,t))$, then $E[e^{W_s+W_t}] = e^{(s+t+2\min)/2}$, minus $e^{s/2}e^{t/2}$.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q9 — $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)=e^{3/2}(e-1)$; $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_2,Y_3)=e^{5/2}(e^2-1)$; $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_2,Y_4)=e^{3}(e^2-1)\approx\mathbf{948.43}$; $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_4)=e^{5/2}(e-1)\approx\mathbf{20.93}$.

6.6  Correlation of BM

$\operatorname{Corr}(W_s,W_t) = \dfrac{\min(s,t)}{\sqrt{st}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{s}{t}}, \quad s\le t$
Use when: a correlation (not covariance) is requested, or when setting up a bivariate normal for conditioning.
Q: $\operatorname{Corr}(W_1,W_4) = \sqrt{1/4} = \mathbf{0.5}$.
Trick: correlation is $\sqrt{s/t}$ — it decays like a square root, so distant times are far less correlated than the covariance $\min(s,t)$ suggests.
7.Martingales of Brownian Motion & the Drift Test

7.1  Canonical Martingale 1 — Linear

$W(t)$ is a martingale; $\;E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s$
Use when: the base case, and the engine behind every Optional Stopping argument (§10.7).
Paper: Endsem’25 Q1(d) needs exactly one fact: $E[W_T] = 0$ at a stopping time.
Trick: $W_t$ being a martingale is the only input to every OST question in §10.7. Nothing else is required.

7.2  Canonical Martingale 2 — Quadratic

$W(t)^2 - t$ is a martingale; $\;E[W_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s^2 + (t-s)$
Use when: you need $E[\tau]$ for an exit problem, or a T/F on $|W_t|^2 - t$.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q1(f) — “$|W_t|^2 - t$ is NOT a martingale (T/F)”. It is one, so the statement is False. Watch the negation.

7.3  Canonical Martingale 3 — Exponential

$M_\lambda(t) = \exp\!\Big(\lambda W_t - \tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}t\Big)$ is a martingale, every $\lambda\in\mathbb R$
Use when: hitting-time Laplace transforms, Girsanov, and checking GBM martingality. $E[M_\lambda(t)] = 1$ for all $t$.
Q: find $E[e^{-\lambda\tau_a}]$ (§12.7).
Trick: choose the $\lambda$ in the martingale that makes the exponent match the barrier, then apply OST. $E[M_\lambda(t)] = 1$ finishes it.

7.4  Martingale Test for $f(t,W_t)$ — Itô drift $=0$

$df = \Big(\partial_t f + \tfrac12 \partial_{ww} f\Big)dt + \partial_w f\, dW$
martingale $\iff \partial_t f + \tfrac12\partial_{ww} f = 0$
special case $f = e^{\lambda t}g(w)$:   MG $\iff \lambda g + \tfrac12 g'' = 0$
Use when: every “Is $Y_t := \ldots$ a martingale? (Yes/No)”. For $g = \sin$ or $\cos$, $g'' = -g$, so the only working exponent is $\lambda = \tfrac12$.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q5 — $e^{t/2}\sin W_t$ Yes; $e^{t}\cos W_t$ No; $e^{t}\sin W_t$ No; $e^{t/2}\cos W_t$ Yes.
One-second rule: exponent $\tfrac12$ → Yes, anything else → No.

7.5  Itô Integral — Always a Martingale, Always Normal

$\int_0^T f(t)\,dW_t \sim N\!\Big(0,\ \int_0^T f(t)^2\,dt\Big)$
Itô isometry: $E\Big[\big(\int_0^T H_t dW_t\big)^2\Big] = E\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt$
Normality needs $f$ deterministic; the martingale/isometry part holds for adapted $H$.
Use when: “compute the distribution of $\int_0^1 t^k dW_t$”. Mean is $0$ free; variance is a one-line calculus integral.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q1 — $\int_0^1 t^k dW_t \sim N\big(0,\tfrac{1}{2k+1}\big)$: $k{=}1\!\to\!\mathbf{N(0,1/3)}$, $k{=}2\!\to\!\mathbf{1/5}$, $k{=}3\!\to\!\mathbf{1/7}$, $k{=}4\!\to\!\mathbf{1/9}$. CT-2’25 Q7: $\int_0^1 t^2dW_t\sim N(0,1/5)$.

7.6  Conditional Expectations You Can Quote

$E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s$
$E[W_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s^2 + (t-s)$
$E[e^{W_t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = e^{W_s + (t-s)/2}$
$E[e^{\lambda W_t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = e^{\lambda W_s + \lambda^2(t-s)/2}$
Use when: “filtration expectation” questions. All four come from §6.3 + §1.2 in one step.
Q: find $E[W_3^2 \mid \mathcal F_1]$ → $\mathbf{W_1^2 + 2}$.
Trick: all four lines are §6.3 (split the increment) followed by §1.2 (exponential moment). Derive them, don’t memorize them.
8.Density, Transition Kernel & the Diffusion Equation

8.1  Density of $W(t)$

$f_{W(t)}(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,e^{-x^2/(2t)}$
$P(W(t) \le x) = \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{x}{\sqrt t}\right)$
Use when: $W$ alone, no maximum, no barrier. Always divide by $\sqrt t$, never $t$.
Q: $P(W(4) > 3) = 1 - \Phi(3/2) = \mathbf{0.0668}$.
Trick: $\sqrt t = 2$, not $4$. Dividing by $t$ instead of $\sqrt t$ is the single most common lost mark on this paper.

8.2  Transition Density

$p(t;x,y) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(y-x)^2}{2t}\Big)$
Use when: BM started at $x\neq0$. Everything else is unchanged — recentre and reuse every formula.
Q: BM started at $5$; $P(W(1) < 7) = \Phi(2) = \mathbf{0.9772}$.
Trick: subtract the starting point first — then every other formula on this sheet applies unchanged.

8.3  Diffusion (Heat) Equation

$\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \dfrac12\,\dfrac{\partial^2 p}{\partial y^2}$
Use when: “show the density satisfies the heat equation”. Differentiate 8.1 twice; the $\tfrac12$ is the same $\tfrac12$ as in Itô’s formula.
Q: “show the transition density satisfies the heat equation”.
Trick: $\partial_{yy}$ brings down $\big(\tfrac{(y-x)^2}{t^2} - \tfrac1t\big)$; $\partial_t$ brings down exactly half of it. One line each, then compare.

8.4  Chapman–Kolmogorov

$p(t+s;x,z) = \displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R} p(t;x,y)\,p(s;y,z)\,dy$
Use when: justifying the Markov property, or short-note questions on why independent increments force Gaussian transitions.
Q: “why must the increments be Gaussian?”
Trick: Chapman–Kolmogorov plus stationary independent increments plus continuity forces the normal family. That sentence is the short-note answer.
9.Conditioning & the Brownian Bridge

9.1  Which Direction Are You Conditioning?

Forward (know past, want future) → 9.2
Backward (know endpoint, want interior) → 9.4
Use when: decide this first. Forward is trivial (martingale, variance grows). Backward is the bridge (variance shrinks to $0$ at both ends).
Q: “Given $W(1)=2$, find $E[W(3)]$” → $\mathbf{2}$ (martingale). “Given $W(3)=2$, find $E[W(1)]$” → $\tfrac13(2) = \mathbf{2/3}$ (bridge).
Trick: identify which time is known before you write a single symbol.

9.2  Forward Conditional

$W(t)\mid \mathcal F_s \sim N\big(W(s),\ t-s\big), \qquad t>s$
Use when: future given present. Independent increments, nothing more.
Q: $W(5) \mid W(2) = 3 \sim \mathbf{N(3,\,3)}$.
Trick: the mean stays exactly where you left it; the variance is the elapsed time and nothing else.

9.3  Standard Brownian Bridge

$B(s) = W(s) - \dfrac{s}{t}W(t), \quad 0\le s\le t$
$E[B(s)] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(B(u),B(v)) = \min(u,v) - \dfrac{uv}{t}$
$B \perp W(t)$
Use when: the process is pinned at both ends. The $-uv/t$ term is exactly the information removed by knowing the endpoint.
Q: $\operatorname{Var}(B(t/2))$ on $[0,t]$ $= \tfrac t2 - \tfrac{(t/2)^2}{t} = \mathbf{t/4}$.
Trick: bridge variance is zero at both ends and peaks at the midpoint — a 2-second sanity check on any bridge answer.

9.4  Backward Interpolation — the workhorse

$W(s)\mid W(t){=}b \;\sim\; N\!\left(\dfrac{s}{t}\,b,\ \dfrac{s(t-s)}{t}\right), \qquad s<t$
Use when: “given $W(t)=b$, find $P(W(s)<x)$ / $E[W(s)]$ / $\operatorname{Var}$”. Mean is the straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(t,b)$; variance vanishes at both ends and peaks at the midpoint.
Trick: derive it in 10 seconds from §1.9 with $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} s & s\\ s& t\end{pmatrix}$: mean $= \frac st b$, var $= s - \frac{s^2}{t}$.

9.5  Bridge Pinned $a \to b$

$X(s) \sim N\!\left(a + \dfrac{s}{t}(b-a),\ \dfrac{s(t-s)}{t}\right)$
$X(0)=a$, $X(t)=b$.
Use when: the process does not start at $0$. Only the mean line shifts; the variance is untouched.
Q: $W(0)=2$, $W(10)=8$; find the law of $W(4)$. Mean $= 2 + \tfrac{4}{10}(6) = \mathbf{4.4}$, Var $= \tfrac{4(6)}{10} = \mathbf{2.4}$.
Trick: the mean is plain linear interpolation between the two pins.

9.6  Scaled BM ($Y = \sigma W$)

$Y(s)\mid Y(t){=}b \sim N\!\left(\dfrac st b,\ \sigma^2\dfrac{s(t-s)}{t}\right)$
Use when: volatility $\sigma\ne1$. Mean is unchanged (ratios cancel); only variance picks up $\sigma^2$.
Q: $Y = 3W$ with $Y(10) = 12$; find the law of $Y(4)$. Mean $= \tfrac{4}{10}(12) = \mathbf{4.8}$, Var $= 9\cdot\tfrac{4(6)}{10} = \mathbf{21.6}$.
Trick: $\sigma^2$ scales the variance and leaves the mean untouched.
10.BM with Drift — Exit Probabilities & Optimal Stopping

10.1  Definition

$X(t) = \mu t + \sigma W(t) \sim N(\mu t,\ \sigma^2 t)$
$X(t) - X(s) \sim N\big(\mu(t-s),\ \sigma^2(t-s)\big)$, independent increments
Use when: “drift coefficient / drift parameter $\mu$ and variance parameter $\sigma^2$”. Careful: some questions give $\sigma^2$, some give $\sigma$.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q1(a) says “drift coefficient $-1$” → $\mu{=}{-}1$, $\sigma{=}1$. CT-1’26 Q6 says “volatility parameter $\sigma = 0.3$” → that is $\sigma$, not $\sigma^2$.
Trick: check whether they handed you $\sigma$ or $\sigma^2$ — the exit formulas all want $\sigma^2$.

10.2  Which Exit Formula? — decision

$\mu = 0$, two barriers  →  10.4 (distance ratio)
$\mu \ne 0$, two barriers  →  10.3 (exponentials)
$\mu < 0$, one upper barrier, no lower  →  10.5
$\mu \ge 0$, one upper barrier  →  probability $= 1$
Use when: run this fork before writing anything. Most lost marks are from using 10.3 when $\mu=0$ (it is $0/0$).
Paper: Midsem’24 Q1(c) gives $\mu = 0$, so 10.4 applies and 10.3 does not.
Trick: at $\mu = 0$ the exponential formula is literally $0/0$. Check the drift before choosing a formula, every time.

10.3  Two-Barrier Exit — Drift $\ne 0$

$P(\text{hit } A \text{ before } -B \mid X(0){=}0) = \dfrac{1 - e^{-2\mu B/\sigma^2}}{1 - e^{-2\mu(A+B)/\sigma^2}}$
from $X(0)=x$, barriers $A$ (up) and $-B$ (down): replace $B \to x+B$
$A,B>0$, $\mu\ne0$.
Use when: continuous-time gambler’s ruin. It is §3.3 with $(q/p)^i \to e^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2}$.
Q: $\mu = 0.1$, $\sigma = 1$, barriers $+2$ and $-3$ from $0$.
$P = \dfrac{1-e^{-0.6}}{1-e^{-1}} = \dfrac{0.4512}{0.6321} = \mathbf{0.714}$.
Trick: set $r = 2\mu/\sigma^2$ once, then it reads $\frac{1-e^{-rB}}{1-e^{-r(A+B)}}$.

10.4  Two-Barrier Exit — Zero Drift

$P(\text{hit } A \text{ before } -B \mid X(0){=}0) = \dfrac{B}{A+B}$
$P(\text{hit } -B \text{ first}) = \dfrac{A}{A+B}$
Use when: $\mu = 0$. Trick: the probability of hitting a barrier is proportional to the distance to the opposite barrier. $\sigma$ cancels and never appears.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q1(c) — $B(t)$, $\mu{=}0$, $\sigma^2{=}1$, “reaches $-2$ before $1$”. Up-barrier $A{=}1$, down-barrier $B{=}2$. $P(\text{down first}) = \frac{A}{A+B} = \frac{1}{3}$ ⇒ $\mathbf{1/3}$.

10.5  Negative Drift — Maximum Ever Reached

$P\Big(\max_{t\ge0} X(t) \ge a\Big) = e^{2\mu a/\sigma^2} = e^{-2|\mu|a/\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu<0,\ a>0$
$\displaystyle\max_{t\ge0}X(t) \sim \mathrm{Exp}\!\left(\frac{2|\mu|}{\sigma^2}\right)$
Use when: only an upper target, drift pulls down, infinite horizon. If $\mu\ge0$ the probability is $1$ (the process reaches every level a.s.).
Trick: memoryless ⇒ the all-time max is Exponential. That is what makes the optimization in 10.6 a one-line calculus problem.

10.6  Optimal Exercise Threshold (drift $-\mu$)

maximize $\ g(x) = (x-K)\,e^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2}$ over $x \ge K$
$x^{*} = K + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\mu}, \qquad g(x^{*}) = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\mu}\,e^{-2\mu K/\sigma^2 - 1}$
$\sigma = 1$ case: $x^{*} = K + \frac{1}{2\mu}$.
Use when: “option to buy at fixed price $K$; price follows BM with drift coefficient $-\mu$; when, if ever, should we exercise?”
Set-up: payoff $x-K$, probability of ever reaching $x$ is $e^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2}$ (10.5). Differentiate, done.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q1(a) — $K=100$, drift $-1$ (so $\mu{=}1$, $\sigma{=}1$), start $0$. $x^{*} = 100 + \tfrac12 = \mathbf{100.5}$. Exercise the first time the price hits $100.5$.

10.7  Hitting a Sloping Line — via OST

$T = \min\{t: W_t = a + bt\}$
$E[W_T] = 0 \Rightarrow a + b\,E[T] = 0 \Rightarrow E[T] = -\dfrac{a}{b}$
Needs $ab < 0$ (line slopes back toward $0$), else $E[T]=\infty$.
Use when: the barrier moves. Apply OST to the martingale $W_t$ at the stopping time, substitute the barrier equation, solve for $E[T]$.
Paper: Endsem’25 Q1(d) — $T = \min\{t: W_t = 2 - 3t\}$. $0 = 2 - 3E[T] \Rightarrow E[T] = \mathbf{2/3}$.

10.8  Expected Exit Time (two barriers)

$\mu = 0:\quad E[T] = \dfrac{A\,B}{\sigma^2}$   (from $X(0)=0$)
$\mu \ne 0:\quad E[T] = \dfrac{A\,p - B(1-p)}{\mu}$,   $p$ from 10.3
Use when: duration asked as well as probability. $\mu{=}0$ case uses the martingale $X_t^2 - \sigma^2 t$; $\mu\ne0$ uses $X_t - \mu t$ (Wald).
Check: $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$, $A=B=n$ gives $E[T]=n^2$ — the discrete $i(N-i)$ of §3.2 with $i=n,N=2n$.

Midsem’25 Q1(b) — $\mu{=}.01,\sigma{=}.2,Y_0{=}10,t{=}100$: $\mathbf{10e^{3}}$ or $10e^{1}$.

11.3  Filtration Expectation of GBM

(R) $\;E[Y(t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = Y(s)\,e^{(\mu+\sigma^2/2)(t-s)}$
(S) $\;E[Y(t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = Y(s)\,e^{\mu(t-s)}$
Use when: conditional version. Trick: same formula with $Y(0)\to Y(s)$ and $t \to t-s$ — GBM is multiplicatively memoryless.
Q: $E[Y(10)\mid\mathcal F_4]$ with (R), $\mu{=}0.01$, $\sigma{=}0.2$ → $Y(4)\,e^{(0.01+0.02)(6)} = \mathbf{Y(4)e^{0.18}}$.
Trick: same formula as 11.2 with $Y(0)\to Y(s)$ and $t \to t-s$.

11.4  GBM Martingale Condition

(R) MG $\iff \mu = -\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2}$    (S) MG $\iff \mu = 0$
Use when: “is the discounted/undiscounted price a martingale?”. Same $-\sigma^2/2$ as the geometric random walk (§2.3).
Q: “Is $e^{W_t - t/2}$ a martingale?” → Yes: it is (R) with $\mu = -\tfrac12 = -\sigma^2/2$, $\sigma = 1$.
Trick: it is precisely the exponential martingale of §7.3 at $\lambda = 1$. Same object, two names.

11.5  Higher Moments & Variance

(R) $\;E[Y(t)^n] = Y(0)^n\,e^{(n\mu + n^2\sigma^2/2)t}$
$\operatorname{Var}(Y(t)) = E[Y(t)]^2\big(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1\big)$
Use when: variance or second moment requested. Note $\operatorname{Var}$ is convention-free once written in terms of $E[Y(t)]$.
Q: (R) with $\mu{=}0$, $\sigma{=}1$, $Y_0{=}1$, $t{=}1$: $E[Y] = e^{1/2}$, so $\operatorname{Var} = e(e-1) = \mathbf{4.67}$.
Trick: get $E[Y(t)]$ first, square it, multiply by $(e^{\sigma^2 t}-1)$. Convention-free once written that way.

11.6  Median vs Mean

(R) median $= Y(0)e^{\mu t}$,   mean $= Y(0)e^{(\mu+\sigma^2/2)t}$
Use when: conceptual questions on why a GBM can have growing mean but drift to $0$ almost surely: for $\mu < 0 < \mu + \sigma^2/2$, median $\to 0$ while mean $\to\infty$.
Q: (R) with $\mu = -0.1$, $\sigma = 0.6$: does the stock grow? The mean grows ($\mu + \sigma^2/2 = 0.08 > 0$) while the median $\to 0$.
Trick: that gap is the log-normal. Expect a conceptual mark on it.

11.7  Distribution & Probabilities

$\ln\dfrac{Y(t)}{Y(0)} \sim N\big(\mu t,\ \sigma^2 t\big)$  (R)
$P(Y(t) > K) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{\ln(K/Y_0) - \mu t}{\sigma\sqrt t}\right)$
Use when: “probability the price exceeds $K$”. Take logs first, then standardize. This is the $d_2$ of Black–Scholes.
Q: $S_0 = 100$, (R) $\mu = 0.05$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $t = 1$. $P(S_1 > 110) = 1 - \Phi\!\big(\tfrac{\ln 1.1 - 0.05}{0.2}\big) = 1-\Phi(0.227) = \mathbf{0.410}$.
Trick: take logs first, then standardize. That expression is Black–Scholes’ $d_2$.
12.Hitting Times & the Reflection Principle

12.1  Hitting Time & the Key Equivalence

$\tau_a = \inf\{t \ge 0 : W(t) = a\}$
$\{\tau_a \le t\} = \{M(t) \ge a\}$   for $a > 0$
Use when: this equivalence is the bridge between §12 and §13 — every maximum question is a hitting-time question and vice versa. For $a<0$ use $\tau_a \overset{d}{=} \tau_{|a|}$ by symmetry.
Q: $P(M(4) \ge 3)$ and $P(\tau_3 \le 4)$ are the same number.
Trick: whenever you see one, silently rewrite it as the other and use whichever formula you actually remember. They are interchangeable by definition.

12.2  Reflection Principle

$P\big(W(t)\le a-y,\ M(t) > a\big) = P\big(W(t) \ge a+y\big) = 1-\Phi\!\left(\dfrac{a+y}{\sqrt t}\right)$
Requires $a>0$ and $y\ge0$.
Use when: the engine for all of §13–14. Reflect the path after $\tau_a$ across the line $W=a$; the strong Markov property makes the reflected path an equally likely BM.
Why $a>0$: it forces $\tau_a\le t$ automatically, so that condition can be dropped.
Q: $P(W(4) \le 1,\ M(4) > 2)$: read off $a = 2$, $y = 2-1 = 1$ → $1 - \Phi(3/2) = \mathbf{0.0668}$.
Trick: $a$ is the barrier, $y$ is barrier minus the $W$-bound. If $y < 0$ you are in the third branch of §14.1 instead.

12.3  cdf of $\tau_a$

$P(\tau_a \le t) = 2\Big(1-\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big)\Big) = 2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big)$
$a>0$; for general $a$ replace $a$ by $|a|$.
Use when: “find the cdf of $\tau_a$”. Trick: set $y=0$ in 12.2 and double for the symmetric half $\{W(t)>a\}$.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q5(a) — “find the cdf and pdf of $\tau_a$”. Quote 12.3 then differentiate for 12.4.

12.4  pdf of $\tau_a$ (Lévy distribution)

$f_{\tau_a}(t) = \dfrac{|a|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\;t^{3/2}}\,e^{-a^2/(2t)}, \qquad t>0$
Use when: follows from 12.3 by $\frac{d}{dt}$. Trick: differentiate $2\Phi(-a t^{-1/2})$ using the chain rule; the $t^{-3/2}$ comes from $\frac{d}{dt}t^{-1/2}$.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q5(a), second half. $f_{\tau_2}(4) = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,8}e^{-1/2} = \mathbf{0.0605}$.
Trick: differentiate 12.3 — never try to integrate the joint density for this.

12.5  Certain Hitting, Infinite Mean

$P(\tau_a < \infty) = \lim_{t\to\infty} 2\Phi\!\Big(-\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big) = 2\Phi(0) = 1$
$E[\tau_a] = \infty$   (the $t^{-3/2}$ tail is not integrable)
Use when: “$P(T_a < \infty) = ?$” → 1. BM hits every level with certainty, but takes infinitely long on average. Both facts are asked.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q1(f) — “$T_a$ = time to hit $a$; find $P(T_a<\infty)$” ⇒ $\mathbf{1}$.

12.6  Scaling of $\tau_a$

$\tau_a \overset{d}{=} a^2\,\tau_1$
Use when: comparing hitting times of different levels, or sanity-checking: doubling the barrier quadruples the typical time.
Q: “$E[\tau_1] = \infty$ — what about $\tau_{10}$?” Also $\infty$, and typically $100\times$ longer.
Trick: $\tau_a \overset{d}{=} a^2\tau_1$ is the same $\sqrt t$ scaling as §4.1, seen from the other side.

12.7  Laplace Transform of $\tau_a$

$E\big[e^{-\lambda \tau_a}\big] = e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}, \qquad \lambda>0,\ a>0$
Use when: a discounted payoff at a hitting time. Derived by OST on the exponential martingale (§7.3) with $\lambda_{\text{mg}}=\sqrt{2\lambda}$.
Q: value of Rs 1 paid the instant the price first hits $a$, discounted at rate $\lambda$ → $\mathbf{e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}}$.
Trick: apply OST to $\exp(\theta W_t - \tfrac{\theta^2}{2}t)$ with $\theta = \sqrt{2\lambda}$, so the $t$-term becomes exactly the discount factor.
13.The Running Maximum $M(t)$ — alone

13.1  Definition & Hard Constraints

$M(t) = \max_{0\le u \le t} W(u)$
$M(t) \ge W(t)$  and  $M(t) \ge 0$  always
Use when: check these two first. Any event forcing $W(t) > M(t)$, or $M(t) < 0$, has probability zero — write “$0$” and move on.
Q: $P(2 < W(4) < 3,\ 0 < M(4) < 1) = \mathbf{0}$.
Trick: it demands $W > M$, which is impossible. Spot it, write $0$, move on — this is a deliberate trap and free marks.

13.2  Distribution of $M(t)$ — half-normal

$M(t) \overset{d}{=} |W(t)|$
$P(M(t) \ge a) = 2\Big(1 - \Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big)\Big), \qquad a\ge0$
$P(M(t) \le a) = 2\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big) - 1$
Use when: the question involves $M$ but not $W$. One line, no reflection needed.
Interval: $P(c < M(t) \le d) = 2\big[\Phi(d/\sqrt t) - \Phi(c/\sqrt t)\big]$. For $t{=}4$, $(c,d)=(2,3)$: $2[\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(1)] = 2[.9332-.8413] = \mathbf{0.1837}$.

13.3  Density of $M(t)$

$f_{M(t)}(a) = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,e^{-a^2/(2t)}, \qquad a\ge0$
Use when: an expectation over $M$ is needed. It is twice the $N(0,t)$ density on the positive half-line.
Q: “compute $E[M(4)]$ by integrating the density”.
Trick: don’t — quote §13.4. Reach for the density only when the functional is non-standard (e.g. $E[e^{M(t)}]$).

13.4  Moments of $M(t)$

$E[M(t)] = \sqrt{\dfrac{2t}{\pi}}, \qquad E[M(t)^2] = t$
$\operatorname{Var}(M(t)) = t\Big(1 - \dfrac{2}{\pi}\Big)$
Use when: “expected maximum”. Note $E[M(t)^2]=E[W(t)^2]=t$ because $M\overset{d}{=}|W|$.
Q: “expected highest level reached in 4 units of time” $= \sqrt{8/\pi} = \mathbf{1.596}$.
Trick: $E[M(t)] = \sqrt{2t/\pi} \approx 0.8\sqrt t$ — useful for checking an answer’s order of magnitude.

13.5  Running Minimum

$m(t) = \min_{0\le u\le t}W(u) \overset{d}{=} -M(t)$
$P(m(t) \le -a) = 2\Big(1-\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt t}\Big)\Big), \quad a \ge 0$
Use when: a minimum or a lower barrier appears. Flip signs everywhere ($W\to-W$) and reuse §13–14 verbatim.
Q: $P\big(\min_{u\le4} W(u) \le -3\big) = 2(1-\Phi(1.5)) = \mathbf{0.1336}$.
Trick: reflect the entire problem ($W \to -W$) and reuse §13.2 with $+3$. Never derive a separate minimum formula.
14.Joint Law of $\big(W(t),M(t)\big)$ — the master recipe

14.1  ⚠ The Guard Everyone Gets Wrong

$G(b,c) := P\big(W(t)\le b,\ M(t)\ge c\big)$
$\boxed{\;G(b,c)=\begin{cases} \Phi\!\big(\tfrac{b}{\sqrt t}\big) & c \le 0\\[6pt] 1 - \Phi\!\big(\tfrac{2c-b}{\sqrt t}\big) & 0 < c,\ b \le c\\[6pt] 1 + \Phi\!\big(\tfrac{b}{\sqrt t}\big) - 2\Phi\!\big(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt t}\big) & 0 < c < b\end{cases}}$
The $b>c$ case is NOT zero. The reflection formula $1-\Phi(\frac{2c-b}{\sqrt t})$ is only valid while $b\le c$. If $b > c$, split at $c$: once $W(t)>c$ the constraint $M(t)\ge c$ is automatic, so
$G = \underbrace{[1-\Phi(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt t})]}_{W\le c} + \underbrace{[\Phi(\tfrac{b}{\sqrt t})-\Phi(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt t})]}_{c<W\le b}$.
Sanity checks: at $b=c$ both branches give $1-\Phi(c/\sqrt t)$ ✓. As $b\to\infty$: $G\to 2(1-\Phi(c/\sqrt t)) = P(M\ge c)$ ✓ (§13.2).
Q: this guard alone decides Midsem’25 Q5(b) — see 14.4.
Trick: before evaluating any $G$, write down the comparison of $b$ with $c$ on your script. $b \le c$ → reflection formula; $b > c$ → the three-term form.

14.2  Joint Density

$f_{W,M}(w,m) = \dfrac{2(2m-w)}{t\sqrt{2\pi t}}\;\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{(2m-w)^2}{2t}\right)$
Support: $m \ge \max(0, w)$. Zero elsewhere.
Use when: the region is not a rectangle, or an expectation like $E[M(t)W(t)]$ is asked. For rectangles use 14.3 — it is far faster.
Q: $E[M(4)W(4)]$, or any non-rectangular region.
Trick: the substitution $u = 2m - w$ turns every integral of this density into a standard normal one — that substitution is exactly how §14.1 is derived.

14.3  ⭐ Master Recipe — any rectangle, any position

$P\big(a < W(t) \le b,\ c < M(t)\le d\big)$
$=\big[G(b,c) - G(a,c)\big] - \big[G(b,d) - G(a,d)\big]$
$G$ from 14.1. Strict vs non-strict inequalities are irrelevant (continuous laws).
Use when: always. This one line replaces the whole “is $I$ above / below / overlapping $J$” case tree — the branching lives inside $G$, so you never classify anything.
Four evaluations of $G$, one subtraction. That is the entire problem.
Trick: tabulate $G(b,c), G(a,c), G(b,d), G(a,d)$ in a $2\times2$ box, then (top-left − bottom-left) − (top-right − bottom-right).

14.4  ⭐ Worked: Midsem’25 Q5(b)

$P\big(1 < W(4) < 3,\ 2 < M(4) < 3\big)$
$t=4,\ \sqrt t = 2;\quad a{=}1,\ b{=}3,\ c{=}2,\ d{=}3$
$G(3,2)$: $0<c{=}2<b{=}3$ → branch 3: $1+\Phi(1.5)-2\Phi(1) = 1+.9332-1.6827 = .2505$
$G(1,2)$: $b{=}1\le c{=}2$ → branch 2: $1-\Phi(\tfrac{4-1}{2}) = 1-\Phi(1.5) = .0668$
$G(3,3)$: $b{=}3\le c{=}3$ → branch 2: $1-\Phi(\tfrac{6-3}{2}) = 1-\Phi(1.5) = .0668$
$G(1,3)$: $b{=}1\le c{=}3$ → branch 2: $1-\Phi(\tfrac{6-1}{2}) = 1-\Phi(2.5) = .0062$

$\big[.2505-.0668\big] - \big[.0668-.0062\big] = .1837 - .0606 = \mathbf{0.1231}$
Note: $G(3,2)$ hits the $b>c$ branch. Using the naive reflection formula there gives $1-\Phi(\tfrac{4-3}{2})=1-\Phi(.5)=.3085$ and a wrong final answer of $0.1811$. This single guard is the whole question.

14.5  Shortcut — $I$ entirely below $J$

$b \le c,\ c>0:$
$P = \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{2d-b}{\sqrt t}\Big) - \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{2c-b}{\sqrt t}\Big) + \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{2c-a}{\sqrt t}\Big) - \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{2d-a}{\sqrt t}\Big)$
Use when: $I=(a,b]$ lies entirely below $J=(c,d]$. This is 14.3 with all four $G$’s on branch 2 — write it directly if you spot $b\le c$.
Q: $P(1 < W(4) \le 2,\ 2 < M(4) \le 3)$: here $b{=}2 \le c{=}2$ ✓, so
$\Phi(2)-\Phi(1)+\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(2.5) = \mathbf{0.0753}$.
Trick: this is exactly the lower half of Midsem’25 Q5(b); the upper half $(2,3)$ needs the $b>c$ branch.

14.6  Shortcut — $I$ entirely above $J$

$a \ge d \;\Longrightarrow\; P\big(W(t)\in I,\ M(t)\in J\big) = 0$
Use when: would need $W(t) > M(t)$ — impossible (§13.1). Also $d\le 0 \Rightarrow 0$, since $M \ge 0$. Spot these before computing anything.
Q: $P(3 < W(4) < 4,\ 1 < M(4) < 2) = \mathbf{0}$, since $a{=}3 \ge d{=}2$.
Trick: test $a \ge d$ and $d \le 0$ first — two comparisons that can retire the whole question instantly.

14.7  Useful Special Cases of $G$

$P(W(t)\le b,\ M(t)\ge c)$ with $b=c$: $\;1-\Phi\!\big(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt t}\big)$
$P(M(t)\ge c) = G(\infty,c) = 2\big(1-\Phi(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt t})\big)$
$P(W(t)\le b,\ M(t)< c) = \Phi\!\big(\tfrac{b}{\sqrt t}\big) - G(b,c)$
Use when: the question gives a half-line instead of an interval — set the missing endpoint to $\pm\infty$ and use $\Phi(\pm\infty) = 1$ or $0$.
Q: $P(W(4) \le 1,\ M(4) < 2) = \Phi(0.5) - G(1,2) = 0.6915 - 0.0668 = \mathbf{0.6247}$.
Trick: “the max stayed below $c$” is the marginal of $W$ minus $G$. Half-lines are just $\pm\infty$ endpoints.

14.8  Barrier-Option Translation

knock-out / knock-in payoffs $=$ rectangles in $(W,M)$
$P(\text{never hit } c,\ W(t)\in I) = P(W(t)\in I) - G(\cdot,c)$ terms
Use when: a question is dressed as a barrier option. Translate “price never exceeded $c$” into $\{M(t) < c\}$ and run 14.3.
Q: up-and-out call, barrier $3$, $S = e^{W}$ → $P\big(M(4) < \ln 3,\ W(4) \in I\big)$.
Trick: “the price never touched the barrier” $\iff \{M(t) < c\}$. Translate, then run §14.3 verbatim.

I1.The Itô Integral

I1.1  Definition — left endpoint

$\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t)\,dW_t = \lim_{\text{mesh}\to0}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(t_{i-1})\big(W(t_i)-W(t_{i-1})\big)$
Use when: the left endpoint $t_{i-1}$ is the whole definition. It makes the integrand predictable — you commit the stake before seeing the increment (§M7.2). A midpoint rule would give a different answer (Stratonovich).
Trick: for ordinary Riemann integrals the evaluation point does not matter in the limit. Here it does, because $\Delta W \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}$ is too big for the difference to vanish. Say that sentence in any “define the Itô integral” question.

I1.2  Pathwise Formula (deterministic $f$)

$\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t)\,dW_t = f(b)W(b) - f(a)W(a) - \int_a^b W(t)f'(t)\,dt$
$f$ non-random with continuous derivative.
Use when: integration by parts, and note no $dW$ remains on the right — it is ordinary calculus path by path. Only works because $f$ is deterministic.
Q: $\displaystyle\int_0^1 t\,dW_t = W(1) - \int_0^1 W(t)\,dt$.
Trick: $f\equiv1$ gives $\int_a^b dW = W(b)-W(a)$ — the sum telescopes.

I1.3  Mean Zero & Martingale

$E\Big[\displaystyle\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\Big] = 0$
$\Big(\displaystyle\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\Big)_{t\ge0}$ is a martingale  ($H$ adapted, square-integrable)
Use when: every expectation computation in Part III starts by killing the $dW$ term. Each summand has $E[f(t_{i-1})\Delta W_i] = f(t_{i-1})\cdot 0 = 0$ by independence.
Trick: “an Itô integral is a martingale” is the continuous-time version of “you cannot beat a fair game” (§M7.4). Predictable integrand against martingale increments — identical statement.

I1.4  ⭐ Itô Isometry

$E\Big[\Big(\displaystyle\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s\Big)^{2}\Big] = \displaystyle\int_0^T E\big[H_s^{2}\big]\,ds$
Use when: any variance of a stochastic integral. It turns a stochastic second moment into an ordinary integral. Why it works: increments over disjoint intervals are independent, so all cross terms vanish and variances just add.
Q: Endsem’25 Q1(a) — $\operatorname{Var}\big(\int_0^1 t^4dW_t\big) = \int_0^1 t^{8}dt = \mathbf{1/9}$.
Trick: square the integrand, integrate $dt$, done. No stochastic work at all.

I1.5  ⭐ Distribution (deterministic integrand)

$\displaystyle\int_0^T f(t)\,dW_t \;\sim\; N\Big(0,\ \int_0^T f(t)^{2}\,dt\Big)$
$f$ must be non-random. Sums of independent normals are normal, and $L^2$ limits of normals are normal.
Use when: “compute the distribution of $\int_0^1 t^k dW_t$”. Mean $0$ is free; variance is a one-line calculus integral. Same card as §7.5.
Paper: $\int_0^1 t^k dW_t \sim N\big(0,\tfrac{1}{2k+1}\big)$.
CT-1’26: $k{=}1\to\mathbf{N(0,\tfrac13)}$, $k{=}2\to\mathbf{\tfrac15}$, $k{=}3\to\mathbf{\tfrac17}$, $k{=}4\to\mathbf{\tfrac19}$. CT-2’25 Q7: $\mathbf{N(0,\tfrac15)}$. Endsem’25 Q1(a): $\mathbf{\tfrac19}$.

I1.6  ⚠ Random Integrand ⇒ NOT Normal

$\displaystyle\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s$ is not $N\big(0,\int_0^t W_s^2\,ds\big)$
That “variance” would itself be a random variable. Normality in I1.5 needs a deterministic integrand. The isometry (I1.4) survives, normality does not.
Use when: check before writing “$\sim N$”: does the integrand contain $W$ or $X$? If yes, you may still take mean and variance, but not the distribution.
Q: what is it? By I1.7, $\int_0^t W\,dW = \tfrac12W_t^2 - \tfrac t2$, so it is a shifted and scaled $\chi^2_1$ — visibly skewed, not normal.

I1.7  ⭐ The Correction Term

$\displaystyle\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \tfrac12 W_t^{2} - \tfrac12 t$
Use when: the signature result of the subject. Ordinary calculus would give $\tfrac12W_t^2$; the extra $-\tfrac t2$ is there because squared increments do not vanish.
Derivation: $a(b-a) = \tfrac12(b^2-a^2) - \tfrac12(b-a)^2$; sum, telescope, and use $\sum(\Delta W)^2 \to t$.
Check: $E[\tfrac12W_t^2 - \tfrac t2] = \tfrac t2 - \tfrac t2 = 0$ ✓ — as an Itô integral must have. Without the $-\tfrac t2$ the mean would be $\tfrac t2 \ne 0$.

I1.8  Quadratic Variation

$\displaystyle\sum_{j}(\Delta W_j)^2 \;\longrightarrow\; t$   (in $L^2$, by the LLN)
$[W]_t = t$,   i.e. $(dW)^2 = dt$
Use when: the one fact that generates all of Itô calculus. Proof: $\Delta W_j \overset{d}{=}\sqrt{t/n}\,Z_j$, so the sum is $t\cdot\tfrac1n\sum Z_j^2 \to t\,E[Z^2] = t$.
Numeric feel: $t{=}1$, $n{=}4$, $Z = 1.2,-0.5,0.8,-1.1$: $\tfrac{1.44+0.25+0.64+1.21}{4} = 0.885 \approx 1$ ✓
Trick: BM has infinite total variation but finite quadratic variation. That is exactly why the second-order Taylor term survives.
I2.Itô Processes & the Multiplication Table

I2.1  Itô Process

$X_t = X_0 + \displaystyle\int_0^t a_s\,ds + \int_0^t b_s\,dW_s$
shorthand:   $dX_t = a_t\,dt + b_t\,dW_t$
$a$ (drift) and $b$ (diffusion) adapted; the differential is notation for the integral equation, nothing more.
Use when: identify $a$ and $b$ first, always. Every subsequent formula is stated in terms of them.
Q: $X = W$: $a{=}0$, $b{=}1$.   $X_t = \int_0^t s\,dW_s$: $a{=}0$, $b_s{=}s$.
From I1.7: $W_t^2 = \int_0^t 1\,ds + \int_0^t 2W_s\,dW_s$, so $d(W_t^2) = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t$ — $a{=}1$, $b{=}2W_t$.

I2.2  ⭐ Multiplication Table

$dt\cdot dt = 0$
$dt\cdot dW = 0$
$dW\cdot dW = dt$
Use when: memorize these three lines and Itô’s formula is just Taylor. Reason: $\Delta W$ has size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so $(\Delta W)^2 \sim \Delta t$ survives while $(\Delta t)^2$ and $\Delta t^{3/2}$ die.
Orders: $\sum(\Delta t)^2 = t^2/n \to 0$;   $\sum\Delta t\,\Delta W \approx \tfrac tn W_t \to 0$;   $\sum(\Delta W)^2 \to t$ ✓
Trick: third order and beyond all vanish, which is why the Taylor expansion stops at two terms.

I2.3  Square of an Itô Process

$(dX_t)^2 = (a\,dt + b\,dW)^2 = b_t^{2}\,dt$
Use when: the plug-in for the correction term. The drift never enters $(dX)^2$ — only the diffusion coefficient does, and it gets squared.
Q: $dX = dt + 2\sqrt{X}\,dW \Rightarrow (dX)^2 = 4X\,dt$.   $dN = rN\,dt + \alpha N\,dW \Rightarrow (dN)^2 = \alpha^2N^2\,dt$.
I3.Itô’s Formula — the three forms

I3.1  ⭐ The Recipe

1. Taylor-expand to second order
2. apply $dt\,dt = 0$,   $dt\,dW = 0$,   $dW\,dW = dt$
3. collect $dt$ terms (drift) and $dW$ terms (diffusion)
Use when: every Itô question. You do not need to memorize the three forms below — they are all this recipe. Memorize the recipe and the table.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q2(b) asks you to derive Itô’s formula [2 marks].
Trick: state the Taylor expansion, state the table with the $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ reason, note that third order vanishes. That is the derivation.

I3.2  Form 1 — $f(X_t)$

$df(X_t) = f'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac12 f''(X_t)\,b_t^{2}\,dt$
for $X = W$:   $df(W_t) = f'(W_t)\,dW_t + \tfrac12 f''(W_t)\,dt$
Use when: no explicit $t$ in $f$. The second line is the one you use most — the whole dictionary in §I4 comes from it.
Q: $f(x) = x^3$, $X = W$: $d(W_t^3) = 3W_t^2\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt$.
Trick: compare with ordinary calculus, which would give only $3W^2dW$. The extra $\tfrac12 f''$ term is Itô calculus.

I3.3  ⭐ Form 2 — $f(t, X_t)$

$df = \partial_t f\,dt + \partial_x f\,dX_t + \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx} f\,b_t^{2}\,dt$
for $X = W$:   $df = \Big(\partial_t f + \tfrac12\partial_{xx}f\Big)dt + \partial_x f\,dW_t$
Use when: the exam’s standard form — almost every question has an $e^{\lambda t}$ out front. Note there is no $\partial_{tt}$ term: only $x$ carries the $dW$.
Q: $f = x^2 - t$, $X = W$: $dY = -dt + 2W\,dW + dt = 2W_t\,dW_t$. Zero drift ✓ consistent with $W_t^2 - t$ being a martingale (§7.2).

I3.4  Form 3 — Product Rule

$d(X_tY_t) = X_t\,dY_t + Y_t\,dX_t + dX_t\,dY_t$
$dX\,dY = b_1b_2\,dt$   (the Itô correction)
Use when: the product of two processes. It is the ordinary product rule plus one cross term. If either factor is deterministic, $b_1b_2 = 0$ and the correction disappears.
Q: $d(tW_t) = W_t\,dt + t\,dW_t$ — no correction, since $t$ is deterministic.
Q: $X{=}Y{=}W$: $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW + dt$ ✓ recovers I2.1.

I3.5  Form 4 — Quotient Rule

$d\Big(\dfrac{X_t}{Y_t}\Big) = \dfrac{dX_t}{Y_t} - \dfrac{X_t}{Y_t^{2}}\,dY_t + \dfrac{X_t}{Y_t^{3}}(dY_t)^{2} - \dfrac{1}{Y_t^{2}}\,dX_t\,dY_t$
Use when: rarely asked directly. Usually faster to write $X/Y = X\cdot Y^{-1}$ and use I3.2 on $Y^{-1}$ then the product rule.
Q: $X{=}1$, $Y = e^{W_t}$: $d(e^{-W_t}) = \tfrac12e^{-W_t}\,dt - e^{-W_t}\,dW_t$.
Check: that is GBM (§I4) with $\mu{=}0$, $\sigma{=}-1$ ✓

I3.6  General 2-Variable Form

$df(X,Y) = \partial_x f\,dX + \partial_y f\,dY + \tfrac12\big[\partial_{xx}f\,(dX)^2 + 2\partial_{xy}f\,dX\,dY + \partial_{yy}f\,(dY)^2\big]$
Use when: I3.4 and I3.5 are this with $f = xy$ and $f = x/y$. Derive them rather than memorizing, if you prefer — it is two derivatives each.
Check: $f = xy$ gives $\partial_{xy}f = 1$, $\partial_{xx}f = \partial_{yy}f = 0$, so the bracket is $2\,dX\,dY$, halved to $dX\,dY$ ✓ exactly I3.4.
I4.⭐ The Differential Dictionary — memorize these

I4.1  Powers of $W$

$d(W_t^{2}) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt$
$d(W_t^{3}) = 3W_t^{2}\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt$
$d(W_t^{n}) = nW_t^{n-1}dW_t + \tfrac12 n(n-1)W_t^{n-2}\,dt$
Use when: $f = x^n$ in I3.2. The $dt$ coefficient is $\tfrac12 f''$, nothing more.
Bonus — moment recursion: taking $E$ kills the $dW$ term (I1.3), leaving
$E[W_t^n] = \tfrac12 n(n-1)\int_0^t E[W_s^{n-2}]\,ds$.
$n{=}2 \to t$; $n{=}4 \to 6\int_0^t s\,ds = \mathbf{3t^2}$; $n{=}6 \to 15\int_0^t 3s^2ds = \mathbf{15t^3}$ ✓ (§1.4)

I4.2  Exponential of $W$

$d(e^{W_t}) = e^{W_t}\,dW_t + \tfrac12 e^{W_t}\,dt$
$d\big(e^{\sigma W_t}\big) = \sigma e^{\sigma W_t}\,dW_t + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}e^{\sigma W_t}\,dt$
$d\big(e^{\sigma W_t - \sigma^2t/2}\big) = \sigma\,e^{\sigma W_t - \sigma^2t/2}\,dW_t$  (zero drift)
Use when: the third line is the exponential martingale of §7.3 — the $-\sigma^2t/2$ exists precisely to cancel the Itô correction.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q4(b) — $X_t = e^{W_t - t/2}$. $\partial_t f = -\tfrac12 f$, $\partial_x f = \partial_{xx}f = f$, so
$dX = -\tfrac12X\,dt + X\,dW + \tfrac12X\,dt = \mathbf{X_t\,dW_t}$. Zero drift ⇒ Yes, a martingale.

I4.3  ⭐ Sine and Cosine

$d\big(\sin W_t\big) = \cos W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\sin W_t\,dt$
$d\big(\cos W_t\big) = -\sin W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\cos W_t\,dt$
Use when: both have $f'' = -f$, so the drift is always $-\tfrac12 f$. That single fact answers every trig question on this paper.
Paper: CT-2’25 Q8 — “compute $d\sin(W_t)$” → $\mathbf{\cos W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\sin W_t\,dt}$ ✓
Midsem’24 Q1(e) — “compute $d\cos(W_t)$” → $\mathbf{-\sin W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\cos W_t\,dt}$ ✓

I4.4  ⭐ The $e^{\lambda t}$ Family

$d\big(e^{\lambda t}g(W_t)\big) = e^{\lambda t}\Big(\lambda g + \tfrac12 g''\Big)dt \;+\; e^{\lambda t}g'(W_t)\,dW_t$
martingale $\iff \lambda g + \tfrac12 g'' = 0$
Use when: the exam’s favourite shape. For $g = \sin$ or $\cos$ ($g'' = -g$) the condition is $\lambda = \tfrac12$ — exponent $\tfrac12$ ⇒ martingale, anything else ⇒ not.
Paper: CT-1’26 Q5 — $e^{t/2}\sin W_t$ Yes; $e^{t}\cos W_t$ No; $e^{t}\sin W_t$ No; $e^{t/2}\cos W_t$ Yes.
Midsem’25 Q4(b)(i) — $X_t = e^{t/2}\cos W_t$: $dX_t = \mathbf{-e^{t/2}\sin W_t\,dW_t}$, drift zero.

I4.5  Deterministic Factors (no correction)

$d\big(g(t)X_t\big) = g'(t)X_t\,dt + g(t)\,dX_t$
$d(tW_t) = W_t\,dt + t\,dW_t$
$d(e^{t}W_t) = e^{t}W_t\,dt + e^{t}\,dW_t$
Use when: $\partial_{xx}f = 0$, so no Itô correction — it is the ordinary product rule. This is also the integrating factor of §I7.1.
Paper: Endsem’25 Q1(b) — “compute $d(e^{t}W_t)$” → $\mathbf{e^{t}W_t\,dt + e^{t}\,dW_t}$ ✓
Endsem’25 Q1(c) — “is $Y_t = e^{t}W_t$ a martingale?” Drift $= e^tW_t \ne 0$ ⇒ No.

I4.6  Geometric Brownian Motion (both directions)

$Z_t = e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t} \;\Rightarrow\; dZ_t = \Big(\mu + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)Z_t\,dt + \sigma Z_t\,dW_t$
$dN_t = rN_t\,dt + \alpha N_t\,dW_t \;\Rightarrow\; N_t = N_0\exp\Big\{\Big(r - \tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}\Big)t + \alpha W_t\Big\}$
Use when: this $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}$ shuttling back and forth is the two-convention problem of §11.1. Read the direction the question gives you before writing anything.
Derivation of line 2: Itô with $f = \log x$: $d(\log N) = \tfrac{dN}{N} - \tfrac{(dN)^2}{2N^2} = \big(r - \tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}\big)dt + \alpha\,dW$, then exponentiate.
Check: $E[N_t] = N_0e^{(r-\alpha^2/2)t}\,e^{\alpha^2t/2} = \mathbf{N_0e^{rt}}$ ✓ and median $= N_0e^{(r-\alpha^2/2)t}$.

I4.7  Mixed Products

$d\big(W_te^{W_t}\big) = \big(1+\tfrac12W_t\big)e^{W_t}\,dt + (1+W_t)e^{W_t}\,dW_t$
$d\big(e^{-W_t}\big) = \tfrac12 e^{-W_t}\,dt - e^{-W_t}\,dW_t$
Use when: two routes agree — the product rule I3.4, or I3.2 with $f(x) = xe^x$ ($f' = (1{+}x)e^x$, $f'' = (2{+}x)e^x$). Use one and check with the other.
Trick: for $f(x) = xe^x$ the drift is $\tfrac12 f'' = \tfrac12(2+W)e^{W} = (1 + \tfrac12 W)e^{W}$ ✓ Same answer, fewer steps.
I5.Martingale Test & Computing Expectations

I5.1  ⭐ Zero Drift = Martingale

$dX_t = a_t\,dt + b_t\,dW_t$ is a martingale $\iff a_t \equiv 0$
Strictly, zero drift gives a local martingale; a true martingale also needs integrability, which holds in every exam case.
Use when: every “Is $Y_t$ a martingale? (Yes/No)”. Apply Itô, read the $dt$ coefficient, done. You never need the definition.
Paper: Endsem’25 Q1(c) — $Y = e^tW_t$, drift $e^tW_t \ne 0$ ⇒ No.
Midsem’24 Q4(b) — $X = e^{W_t-t/2}$, drift $0$ ⇒ Yes.
Midsem’25 Q1(f) — $|W_t|^2 - t$ has drift $0$, so “is NOT a MG” is False.

I5.2  ⭐ Mean of a Solution — the drift ODE

take $E$ of the integral form; the $dW$ term dies (I1.3):
$m(t) := E[X_t] = x_0 + \displaystyle\int_0^t E\big[a(s,X_s)\big]\,ds$
$a$ linear in $x$ $\Rightarrow$   $m'(t) = a\big(t, m(t)\big)$,   $m(0) = x_0$
Use when: “find $E(X_t)$” for any SDE with linear drift — you never need the solution. Differentiate the integral form and solve an ordinary ODE.
Q: $a = 1$ → $m' = 1$, $m = \mathbf t$.   $a = -x$ → $m' = -m$, $m = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t}}$.   $a = \kappa(b-x)$ → $m = \mathbf{b + e^{-\kappa t}(x_0-b)}$.
Trick: the diffusion coefficient $b$ never appears in the mean.

I5.3  ⭐ Variance of a Solution — Itô on $X^2$

$d(X_t^{2}) = 2X_t\,dX_t + (dX_t)^{2} = \big(2X_ta_t + b_t^{2}\big)dt + 2X_tb_t\,dW_t$
$\dfrac{d}{dt}E[X_t^{2}] = E\big[2X_ta_t + b_t^{2}\big]$,   then $\operatorname{Var} = E[X_t^2] - m(t)^2$
Use when: “also find $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$” — the part they circle. Same method as I5.2, applied to $X^2$ instead of $X$.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q4(a) — $a{=}1$, $b{=}2\sqrt X$, $x_0{=}0$.
$\tfrac{d}{dt}E[X^2] = E[2X + 4X] = 6E[X] = 6t$, so $E[X_t^2] = 3t^2$ and $\operatorname{Var} = 3t^2 - t^2 = \mathbf{2t^2}$ ✓
Cross-check: $X_t = W_t^2$, so $\operatorname{Var} = E[W^4]-t^2 = 3t^2-t^2 = 2t^2$ ✓

I5.4  ⭐ $E[f(W_t)]$ via a Martingale

find $\lambda$ with $\lambda f + \tfrac12 f'' = 0$; then $e^{\lambda t}f(W_t)$ is a MG (I4.4)
$\Rightarrow e^{\lambda t}E[f(W_t)] = f(0) \Rightarrow E[f(W_t)] = f(0)\,e^{-\lambda t}$
Use when: “using part (i) or otherwise, evaluate $E[f(W_t)]$”. Constant mean of a martingale turns a hard expectation into one division.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q4(b)(ii) — $E[\cos W_t]$. Part (i) gave $e^{t/2}\cos W_t$ a MG, so
$e^{t/2}E[\cos W_t] = \cos 0 = 1 \Rightarrow E[\cos W_t] = \mathbf{e^{-t/2}}$ ✓
Check: $E[e^{iW_t}] = e^{-t/2}$, and taking real parts gives the same ✓   Also $E[\sin W_t] = 0$ by symmetry.

I5.5  Reading the Distribution Off an SDE

$b$ deterministic (no $X$ in the noise) $\Rightarrow X_t$ is Gaussian
$b$ contains $X$ $\Rightarrow$ generally not Gaussian
Use when: decide this before claiming a distribution. Gaussian: BM with drift, OU, Vasicek. Not Gaussian: GBM (log-normal), CIR, $dX = dt + 2\sqrt X\,dW$.
Trick: it is I1.5 restated — only a deterministic integrand keeps the Itô integral normal. Look at the $dW$ coefficient and nothing else.
I6.Stochastic Differential Equations — setup & verification

I6.1  Itô Process vs SDE

Itô process: $dX_t = a_t\,dt + b_t\,dW_t$ — $a_t,b_t$ given, nothing to solve
SDE: $dX_t = a(t,X_t)\,dt + b(t,X_t)\,dW_t$, $X_0 = x_0$ — coefficients built from the unknown
Use when: tell them apart before starting. $dX = W_t\,dt + dW_t$ is an Itô process (just integrate); $dX = X_t^2\,dt + dW_t$ is a genuine equation.
Trick: most SDEs have no closed form. The exam only asks the handful in §I7 — or hands you the answer and asks you to verify it (I6.3).

I6.2  What “Solves” Means

$X_t = x_0 + \displaystyle\int_0^t a(s,X_s)\,ds + \int_0^t b(s,X_s)\,dW_s \quad \forall t \in [0,T]$
Use when: a solution is an Itô process satisfying the integral identity. The differential form is shorthand and can never be manipulated as a fraction.
Q: $dX = dt + 2\,dW$, $x_0 = 3$: RHS $= 3 + t + 2W_t$, so $X_t = 3 + t + 2W_t$ ✓
Q: $dX = X\,dt$, $x_0 = 1$: RHS $= 1 + \int_0^t X_s\,ds$; candidate $e^t$ gives $1 + (e^t-1) = e^t$ ✓

I6.3  ⭐ Verification Recipe — “show $X_t = \ldots$ is a solution”

1. apply Itô to the proposed $X_t$
2. rewrite the result in terms of $X_t$ itself
3. compare with the given SDE, and check $X_0 = x_0$
Use when: the exam asks this far more often than it asks you to derive a solution. It is a one-way check — never try to solve.
Paper: Midsem’25 Q4(a) — show $X_t = W_t^2$ solves $dX_t = dt + 2\sqrt{X_t}\,dW_t$, $X_0 = 0$.
Itô: $d(W_t^2) = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t$. Since $\sqrt{X_t} = |W_t|$, this matches ✓ and $X_0 = W_0^2 = 0$ ✓
Then $E[X_t] = E[W_t^2] = \mathbf t$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = 3t^2 - t^2 = \mathbf{2t^2}$ (I5.3).

I6.4  Express the Solution as a Stochastic Integral

$dY_t = h_t\,dt + k_t\,dW_t \iff Y_t = Y_0 + \displaystyle\int_0^t h_s\,ds + \int_0^t k_s\,dW_s$
Use when: the question says “express $X_t$ as a stochastic integral with respect to $W_t$”. Translate differentials into integrals — that is the answer, no evaluation needed.
Paper: Midsem’24 Q4(a) — $dX_t = -X_t\,dt + dW_t$, $X_0 = x_0$.
Answer: $X_t = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t} + \int_0^t e^{-(t-s)}\,dW_s}$ (derived in I7.3).
I7.The Solvable SDEs

I7.1  ⭐ Integrating Factor (linear, additive noise)

$dX_t = \big(\alpha(t) - rX_t\big)dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t$
multiply by $e^{rt}$:   $d\big(e^{rt}X_t\big) = re^{rt}X_t\,dt + e^{rt}dX_t$  (no correction, I4.5)
$\Rightarrow d\big(e^{rt}X_t\big) = e^{rt}\alpha(t)\,dt + e^{rt}\sigma(t)\,dW_t$  the $X$ cancels
Use when: the only general solving technique on this syllabus. Integrate, then divide by $e^{rt}$. Works exactly when the noise does not contain $X$.
Trick: the whole method is “choose $f$ so that Itô’s formula deletes the unknown”. If the noise contains $X$ (as in CIR) nothing cancels — see I7.5.

I7.2  Brownian Motion with Drift

$dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$,   $X_0 = x_0$
$X_t = x_0 + \mu t + \sigma W_t \sim N\big(x_0 + \mu t,\ \sigma^2 t\big)$
Use when: the trivial case — both coefficients constant, so you just integrate. All of §10 is built on it.
Trick: constant coefficients $\Rightarrow$ no Itô correction anywhere, because there is no $f$ to differentiate twice.

I7.3  ⭐ Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

$dX_t = -rX_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$,   $X_0 = x_0$,   $r,\sigma>0$
$X_t = x_0e^{-rt} + \sigma\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{-r(t-s)}\,dW_s$
$X_t \sim N\Big(x_0e^{-rt},\ \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2r}\big(1-e^{-2rt}\big)\Big)$
Use when: drift proportional to $-X$ = mean reversion: the pull toward $0$ strengthens with distance. Gaussian, because the noise is deterministic (I5.5).
Paper: Midsem’24 Q4(a) — $dX_t = -X_t\,dt + dW_t$ ($r{=}\sigma{=}1$). “Whether it is an OU process? Explain.” Yes.
$E[X_t] = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t}}$,   $\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \mathbf{\tfrac12(1-e^{-2t})}$.
Variance derivation: isometry gives $\int_0^t e^{-2r(t-s)}ds = \tfrac{1-e^{-2rt}}{2r}$ ✓

I7.4  OU: Stationary Limit

$X_t \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N\Big(0,\ \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2r}\Big)$ as $t\to\infty$
Use when: the contrast with BM is the marked point — OU variance stays bounded; BM variance grows like $t$. The starting value is forgotten exponentially fast.
Cross-ref: §5.5 — $e^{-\lambda t}W(e^{2\lambda t})$ is the same process, built by time-changing BM. Its covariance $e^{-\lambda|t-s|}$ depends only on $|t-s|$: stationary, and therefore not a Wiener process.

I7.5  Vasicek & CIR (interest rates)

Vasicek: $dr_t = \kappa(b-r_t)dt + \sigma\,dW_t$
$r_t \sim N\Big(b + e^{-\kappa t}(r_0-b),\ \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\big(1-e^{-2\kappa t}\big)\Big)$
CIR: $dr_t = \kappa(b-r_t)dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t$ — no closed form
same mean $E[r_t] = b + e^{-\kappa t}(r_0-b)$, but not normal
Use when: Vasicek is OU shifted — substitute $X_t = r_t - b$ and it becomes I7.3 exactly. CIR keeps $r_t \ge 0$ (noise vanishes at $0$), strictly positive if $2\kappa b \ge \sigma^2$ (Feller).
Trick: Vasicek’s flaw is that Gaussian rates can go negative, $P(r_t < 0) > 0$. That is precisely the motivation for CIR — a standard short-note answer.
Note: the two share a mean because they share a drift (I5.2: the diffusion never enters the mean).

I7.6  Geometric Brownian Motion

$dN_t = rN_t\,dt + \alpha N_t\,dW_t$,   $N_0 > 0$
$N_t = N_0\exp\Big\{\Big(r-\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}\Big)t + \alpha W_t\Big\}$  —  log-normal
$E[N_t] = N_0e^{rt}$,   median $= N_0e^{(r-\alpha^2/2)t}$
Use when: multiplicative noise. Solve by Itô on $\log N$ — the only place taking a logarithm is the right move, and it works because $N_t > 0$.
Cross-ref: all numerical GBM questions are §11.2, including the two-convention trap of §11.1. This card is where that $\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}$ comes from.
Rigour note: $\log N_t$ presumes $N_t > 0$; close the gap by verifying the candidate with I6.3 instead.

I7.7  Summary Table

$\mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$  →  Normal, variance $\sigma^2t$
$-rX\,dt + \sigma\,dW$  →  Normal, variance bounded (OU)
$rN\,dt + \alpha N\,dW$  →  log-Normal (GBM)
$\kappa(b-r)dt + \sigma\sqrt r\,dW$  →  no closed form (CIR)
Use when: identify which row you are in before doing anything. Noise depends on $X$? If no → Gaussian, integrating factor works. If yes → either GBM (log) or nothing.
Trick: the mean always comes from the drift alone (I5.2), so you can answer “find $E[X_t]$” for every row — even CIR, where no solution exists.
I8.Worked Past-Paper Walkthroughs

I8.1  ⭐ Midsem’25 Q4(a)

$dX_t = dt + 2\sqrt{X_t}\,dW_t$,   $X_0 = 0$. Show $X_t = W_t^2$; find $E(X_t)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$.
Show: Itô with $f(x) = x^2$ on $X{=}W$ gives $d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt$.
With $X_t = W_t^2$ we have $\sqrt{X_t} = |W_t|$, so $2\sqrt{X_t}\,dW_t = 2W_t\,dW_t$ ✓, and $X_0 = 0$ ✓

Mean (I5.2): $a \equiv 1$, so $m' = 1$, $m(0)=0$ ⇒ $E[X_t] = \mathbf t$.  (Check: $E[W_t^2] = t$ ✓)

Variance (I5.3): $\tfrac{d}{dt}E[X^2] = E[2X_ta_t + b_t^2] = E[2X_t + 4X_t] = 6t$
$\Rightarrow E[X_t^2] = 3t^2 \Rightarrow \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = 3t^2 - t^2 = \mathbf{2t^2}$
Check: $\operatorname{Var}(W_t^2) = E[W^4] - t^2 = 3t^2 - t^2 = 2t^2$ ✓
Note: $\operatorname{Var}$ is the part they circled on the paper. Do it both ways — the cross-check costs one line.

I8.2  ⭐ Midsem’25 Q4(b)

$X_t := e^{t/2}\cos(W_t)$. (i) find $dX_t$. (ii) evaluate $E[\cos W_t]$.
(i) $f(t,x) = e^{t/2}\cos x$:   $\partial_t f = \tfrac12 f$,   $\partial_x f = -e^{t/2}\sin x$,   $\partial_{xx}f = -f$.
$dX_t = \tfrac12 X_t\,dt - e^{t/2}\sin W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12 X_t\,dt = \mathbf{-e^{t/2}\sin(W_t)\,dW_t}$

(ii) Drift is zero ⇒ $X_t$ is a martingale ⇒ $E[X_t] = X_0 = e^0\cos 0 = 1$.
$e^{t/2}E[\cos W_t] = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; E[\cos W_t] = \mathbf{e^{-t/2}}$
Check: $E[e^{iW_t}] = e^{-t/2}$; real part gives the same ✓  ($E[\sin W_t] = 0$ by symmetry.)
Trick: “using part (i) or otherwise” is the examiner telling you the martingale is the method. This is I5.4.

I8.3  ⭐ Midsem’24 Q4(a)

$dX_t = -X_t\,dt + dW_t$, $X_0 = x_0$. Express as a stochastic integral. OU? Find $E(X_t)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$.
Solve (I7.1): $d(e^{t}X_t) = e^{t}X_t\,dt + e^{t}dX_t = e^{t}dW_t$ — the $X$ cancels.
Integrate: $e^{t}X_t = x_0 + \int_0^t e^{s}dW_s$, so
$X_t = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t} + \displaystyle\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)}\,dW_s}$

OU? Yes — drift $-rX$ with $r=1$, constant noise $\sigma=1$: mean-reverting to $0$.

Mean: the Itô integral has mean $0$, so $E[X_t] = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t}}$.
Variance (isometry): $\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{-2(t-s)}ds = \mathbf{\tfrac12\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)}$
Distribution: noise is deterministic ⇒ $X_t \sim N\big(x_0e^{-t},\ \tfrac12(1-e^{-2t})\big)$.
Trick: as $t\to\infty$, $X_t \Rightarrow N(0,\tfrac12)$ — add that line, it is the point of OU.

$X_t = e^{W_t - t/2}$: MG?  |  $d(e^{t}W_t) = ?$  |  is $e^{t}W_t$ a MG?
$X_t = e^{W_t-t/2}$: $f = e^{x-t/2}$, so $\partial_t f = -\tfrac12 f$, $\partial_x f = \partial_{xx}f = f$.
$dX = -\tfrac12X\,dt + X\,dW + \tfrac12X\,dt = \mathbf{X_t\,dW_t}$ ⇒ zero drift ⇒ Yes, a martingale.

$d(e^{t}W_t)$: $\partial_{xx}f = 0$, so no correction (I4.5):
$d(e^{t}W_t) = \mathbf{e^{t}W_t\,dt + e^{t}\,dW_t}$

Is $e^{t}W_t$ a MG? Drift $= e^{t}W_t \ne 0$ ⇒ No.
Trick: both questions are one Itô application each. Compute the differential, then read the $dt$ coefficient — that single number answers the Yes/No.

I8.5  The Short Differentials (1-mark answers)

$d\sin W_t = \cos W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\sin W_t\,dt$  [CT-2’25 Q8]
$d\cos W_t = -\sin W_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\cos W_t\,dt$  [Midsem’24 Q1(e)]
$\operatorname{Var}\big(\int_0^1 t^4dW_t\big) = \tfrac19$  [Endsem’25 Q1(a)]
$\int_0^1 t^2dW_t \sim N(0,\tfrac15)$  [CT-2’25 Q7]
Use when: these appear as single-mark table entries. Write the answer, show nothing. Each takes ten seconds with I4.3 and I1.5.
Trick: for any $f(W_t)$ the drift is always $\tfrac12 f''(W_t)$ and the diffusion always $f'(W_t)$. Two derivatives and you are done — that covers every entry above.

A1.Arbitrage & the Risk-Neutral Measure

A1.1  Arbitrage — definition

A scheme with cost $\le 0$ at $t=0$ and payoff $\ge 0$ in every outcome, $>0$ in some.
Risk-free profit. “Every outcome” means with probability 1 — not on average. No probability appears in the definition.
Use when: any question saying “sure win”, “risk-free profit”, “for which price is there no arbitrage”. The answer is always: build a portfolio whose time-1 value is the same number in all states, then compare with its cost.
Trick: if a certain gain is positive for one scale $x$, it is unbounded — scale $x$ up. That is why an arbitrage price cannot survive in a market.

A1.2  ⭐ No-arbitrage price by hedging (one period, two states)

Buy $x$ stock, $y$ options. Force $V_1$ equal in both states $\Rightarrow$ $y$ in terms of $x$, then
$\text{gain} = V_1 - (1+r)\,\text{cost} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; c^{*}$
Use when: two possible time-1 prices and an option quoted at $c$. Works with no probabilities at all — only the two possible values matter.
Paper: $S_0=100 \to \{200,50\}$, $K=150$, $r=0$. $200x+50y = 50x \Rightarrow y=-3x$; $V_1 = 50x$, cost $=100x-3xc$, gain $= x(3c-50)$. So $c^{*} = \mathbf{50/3}$; $c=20$ (buy stock, write 3 calls) or $c=15$ (short stock, buy 3 calls) both print money.

A1.3  ⭐ Risk-neutral measure

$S_0 = \dfrac{E^{*}[S_1]}{1+r}, \qquad p^{*} = \dfrac{(1+r)S_0 - S_d}{S_u - S_d} = \dfrac{1+r-d}{u-d}$
Equivalently: every wager has zero expected return under $p^{*}$; the discounted price is a martingale (§M3.4).
Use when: any binomial pricing question. Need $d < 1+r < u$, else $p^{*}\notin(0,1)$ and the market itself is an arbitrage.
Paper: $100\to\{130,80\}$, $r=0.05$: $105 = 80 + 50p^{*} \Rightarrow p^{*} = \mathbf{0.5}$ — unrelated to the real $p=0.4$.

A1.4  ⭐ Pricing formula for any claim

$C_0 = \dfrac{E^{*}\big[g(S_1)\big]}{1+r}, \qquad \text{call: } g(s) = (s-K)^+$
Use when: “price the call / put / claim”. Two steps, never more: (1) $p^{*}$ from the stock, (2) expectation of the payoff. Never use the real-world $p$.
Paper: $100\to\{130,80\}$, $K=110$, $r=0.05$, $p^{*}=0.5$: $C_0 = \frac{20(0.5)+0(0.5)}{1.05} = \mathbf{9.52}$.

A1.5  ⭐ Replicating portfolio $(\Delta, B)$

$\Delta = \dfrac{C_u - C_d}{S_u - S_d}, \qquad B = \dfrac{C_u - \Delta S_u}{1+r}, \qquad C_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$
$\Delta$ = shares held, $B$ = cash lent ($B<0$ means borrowing). $C_0$ from replication always equals $C_0$ from §A1.4 — two routes, one price.
Use when: “construct the hedge”, “show the claim is attainable”, or when you must exhibit the arbitrage rather than merely assert it.
Paper: $\Delta = \frac{20-0}{130-80} = 0.4$, $B = \frac{20-52}{1.05} = -30.48$; $C_0 = 40-30.48 = \mathbf{9.52}$. Down state check: $0.4(80)-32 = 0$ ✓.

A1.6  ⚠ The actuarial trap

$\dfrac{E_p[\text{payoff}]}{1+r} \ne C_0$   when $p$ is the real-world probability.
Why it fails: the payoff is replicable. Two portfolios with identical cash flows in every state must have identical prices, whatever anyone believes about likelihoods. Insurance risk is diversifiable but not hedgeable, which is why the actuarial method is right there and wrong here.
Paper: $p=0.4$ gives $\frac{20(0.4)}{1.05} = 7.62$, but $C_0 = 9.52$. At $7.62$: buy the call, short $0.4$ shares $(+40)$, lend $30.48$ ⇒ net $+1.90$ today, payoffs cancel at $T$. Certain $1.90$ per contract.
A2.Contingent Claims, the Arbitrage Theorem & Incompleteness

A2.1  Payoff dictionary

Forward (long): $S_T - K$  —  an obligation, can be negative
European call: $(S_T-K)^+$  |  European put: $(K-S_T)^+$
A derivative security / contingent claim is any $X = g(S_T)$ paid at $T$. $x^+ := \max(x,0)$.
Use when: writing down the payoff before anything else. Getting $(\cdot)^+$ vs no $(\cdot)^+$ right is the whole difference between an option and a forward.
Trick: $K=150$, $S_T\in\{200,50\}$ → forward $+50/-100$, call $50/0$, put $0/100$. The call is the forward with the loss leg deleted — which is exactly what the premium buys. Futures = forwards, but exchange-traded and marked to market daily.

A2.2  American vs European

$\text{price} = \sup_{\tau \le T} E^{*}\big[(1+r)^{-\tau}(S_\tau - K)^+\big]$,   $\tau$ a stopping time
$(S_\tau-K)^+$ is the payoff, not the price. $\{\tau \le t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ is compulsory: “the time $S$ peaks” is not admissible, since at $t$ you cannot know the future (§M5).
Use when: “short note: American vs European”. No dividends and $r\ge0$ ⇒ early exercise of a call is never optimal, so the two prices are equal; for a put early exercise can be optimal and the American is strictly dearer.
Trick: one-line reason for the call: exercising early throws away the interest on $K$ and the remaining insurance value. See §M7.6.

A2.3  ⭐ Arbitrage theorem (dichotomy)

Wagers $i=1..n$, outcomes $j=1..m$, return $r_i(j)$ per unit staked. Exactly one holds:
(i) $\exists\,\underline p$ probability vector with $\displaystyle\sum_{j} p_j r_i(j) = 0 \;\forall i$
(ii) $\exists\,\underline x$ with $\displaystyle\sum_{i} x_i r_i(j) > 0 \;\forall j$
No arbitrage $\iff$ a risk-neutral measure exists. Return $=$ payoff $-$ cost; $x_i<0$ allowed (short).
Use when: asked to state or prove it. The “not both” half is three lines: $\sum_j p_j R(j) = \sum_i x_i\sum_j p_j r_i(j) = 0$, yet $R(j)>0 \,\forall j$ and $\sum_j p_j = 1$ force it $>0$. Contradiction.
Trick: the “at least one” half is a separating-hyperplane (Farkas) argument — say so and move on; it is never asked in full.

A2.4  Three states ⇒ incomplete market

$m$ states, $n$ assets with $n < m$: $\;p^{*}$ is not unique $\Rightarrow$ an interval of arbitrage-free prices.
Use when: the tree has three or more branches from one node. Do not hunt for a single price — there is none, and the claim cannot be replicated.
Paper: $100 \to \{300,200,50\}$, $K=150$, $r=0$. Stock: $250p_1 + 150p_2 = 50 \Rightarrow p_2 = \frac{1-5p_1}{3}$; option: $c = 150p_1 + 50p_2 = \frac{200p_1+50}{3}$. Positivity gives $p_1\in(0,\tfrac15)$, hence $c \in \big(\mathbf{\tfrac{50}{3}},\,\mathbf{30}\big)$.
