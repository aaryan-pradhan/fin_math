Q1(a) — No-sure-win option price [1]

Question. A stock is presently selling at Rs 75 per share. After one period its price (in present-value terms) will be either Rs 200 or Rs 50. An option to purchase $y$ units at time 1 for the (present value) price $120y$ costs $cy$. What should $c$ be for there to be no sure win?
Formula: §M4.5, §A1.3
Answer. $c = \mathbf{13.33}$ $(=40/3)$.

Method (§M4.5, §A1.3). Prices are in present value, so $r=0$: $$75 = 200p^{*} + 50(1-p^{*}) \;\Longrightarrow\; p^{*} = \tfrac16 .$$ Payoff $(S_1-120)^+$ is $80$ (up) and $0$ (down), so $c = \tfrac16\cdot 80 = 13.33$. Any other $c$ admits a sure win (hedge: $x$ shares, $y=-\tfrac{150}{80}x$ options).

Q1(b) — Random walk as a martingale [1]

Question. $X_0=0$, $X_n = Y_1+\dots+Y_n$ with $Y_j$ i.i.d., $E|Y_1|<\infty$. $\{X_n\}$ is a martingale if $E(Y_1) = \;?$
Formula: §M2.5
Answer. $E(Y_1) = \mathbf{0}$.

Method (§M2.5). $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n] = X_n + E[Y_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n] = X_n + E[Y_1]$ by independence, and this equals $X_n$ iff $E[Y_1]=0$.

Q1(c) — Two-barrier exit, zero drift [1]

Question. $B(t)$ is Brownian motion from $0$ with $\mu=0$, $\sigma^{2}=1$. Find $P(B \text{ reaches } -2 \text{ before } 1)$.
Formula: §10.4
Answer. $\mathbf{1/3 = 0.33}$.

Method (§10.4). With zero drift, $B$ is a martingale; by OST $0 = -2\,p + 1\,(1-p)$, so $p = \dfrac{1}{1+2} = \dfrac13$. The nearer barrier (1) is the likelier one.

Q2(a) — Short note: call options [2]

Formula: §A2.1, §A2.2

Answer (§A2.1, §A2.2). A call gives the holder the right, not the obligation, to buy one unit of the underlying at strike $K$ at (European) or up to (American) maturity $T$. Payoff $(S_T-K)^{+}$: worthless if $S_T\le K$, unbounded upside. The buyer pays a premium $C$ today; the writer takes the obligation. No-arbitrage price in the binomial model: $C = (1+r)^{-T}E^{*}[(S_T-K)^{+}]$; in continuous time, Black–Scholes. Bounds: $\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},0)\le C\le S_0$. Used to bet on a rise with limited loss, or to cap the cost of a future purchase. On a non-dividend stock, American call = European call (early exercise never optimal).

Q2(b) — Short note: put options [2]

Formula: §M4.6

Answer. A put gives the right, not the obligation, to sell one unit at strike $K$. Payoff $(K-S_T)^{+}$, bounded above by $K$. Price $P = (1+r)^{-T}E^{*}[(K-S_T)^{+}]$; bounds $\max(Ke^{-rT}-S_0,0)\le P\le Ke^{-rT}$. Used as insurance on a held stock (protective put: floor at $K$). Linked to the call by put–call parity (§M4.6): $C - P = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. Unlike the call, an American put can be worth strictly more than the European one: deep in the money, taking $K$ now and earning interest beats waiting.

Q2(c) — Short note: no-arbitrage principle [2]

Formula: §A1.1, §A2.3

Answer (§A1.1, §A2.3). An arbitrage is a self-financing strategy with $V_0 \le 0$, $V_T\ge0$ almost surely and $P(V_T>0)>0$: no cost, no risk of loss, positive chance of gain. The principle: in equilibrium no arbitrage exists, because traders exploiting it would move prices until it vanished. Consequences:
  • Law of one price: two portfolios with the same payoff in every state have the same price today. Hence price of a claim = cost of its replicating portfolio.
  • Fundamental theorem (arbitrage theorem): no arbitrage $\iff$ there is a risk-neutral measure $P^{*}$ under which discounted prices are martingales. In the one-period binomial model this reads $d < 1+r < u$.
  • Price of any claim $X$: $V_0 = E^{*}[X]/(1+r)^{T}$ — the real-world probabilities do not appear.

Q3(a) — Dynamic programming and the optimality equation [3]

Question. Explain dynamic programming in Markov decision processes. Explain the optimality equation and derive it using the total probability formula.
Formula: not on the sheet — derived in the solution
Answer. An MDP has states $x$, actions $a\in A(x)$, one-step rewards $r(x,a)$ and transition law $P(y\mid x,a)$ that depends only on the current state and action (Markov). A policy picks an action at each stage; the goal is to maximise expected total reward over $n$ stages. Dynamic programming solves this backwards: whatever the first decision, the remaining decisions must be optimal for the state they start from (Bellman's principle of optimality). So the $n$-stage problem reduces to one decision plus an already-solved $(n-1)$-stage problem.

Derivation. Let $V_n(x)$ be the maximal expected reward with $n$ stages left from state $x$, $V_0$ the terminal reward. Fix a first action $a$. By total probability over the next state $y$, $$E[\text{total}\mid x,a] = r(x,a) + \sum_{y}P(y\mid x,a)\,E[\text{remaining}\mid y].$$ The remaining reward from $y$ is at most $V_{n-1}(y)$, with equality when playing optimally from $y$ (the Markov property says the history before $y$ is irrelevant). Maximising over $a$: $$V_n(x) = \max_{a\in A(x)}\Big\{ r(x,a) + \sum_{y}P(y\mid x,a)\,V_{n-1}(y)\Big\}.$$ This is the optimality equation. Solve $V_1, V_2, \dots$ in turn; the maximising $a$ at each $(x,n)$ gives an optimal policy. Worked example: Midsem 2025 Q3(b).

Q3(b) — Random walk converges to BM with drift [3]

Question. In each $\Delta t$ the walk moves $+\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with probability $p = \tfrac12(1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t})$ and $-\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with probability $1-p$. Argue that as $\Delta t\to0$ the limit is Brownian motion with drift $\mu$.
Formula: §4.2
Answer (§4.2). Let $X_i = \pm1$ be the step signs, so $X(t) = \sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i$ with $n = t/\Delta t$ steps. Per step, $$E[X_i] = 2p-1 = \mu\sqrt{\Delta t},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = 1-(2p-1)^{2} = 1-\mu^{2}\Delta t.$$ Hence $$E[X(t)] = \sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\frac{t}{\Delta t}\cdot\mu\sqrt{\Delta t} = \mu t,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X(t)) = \Delta t\cdot\frac{t}{\Delta t}\,(1-\mu^{2}\Delta t)\to t.$$ As $\Delta t\to0$, $X(t)$ is a sum of $n\to\infty$ i.i.d. terms, so by the CLT $X(t)\to N(\mu t,\ t)$. Steps in disjoint time intervals are disjoint sets of independent $X_i$, so increments are independent; their law depends only on the interval length, so they are stationary; and $X(0)=0$. These are exactly the properties of Brownian motion with drift $\mu$ and $\sigma^{2}=1$ (paths become continuous since the jump size $\sqrt{\Delta t}\to0$).
The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ inside $p$ is essential: it makes the per-step bias $O(\sqrt{\Delta t})$, which after $t/\Delta t$ steps of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ gives a finite drift $\mu t$. A bias of order 1 would blow up.

Q4(a) — OU SDE as a stochastic integral [3]

Question. Solve $dX_t = -X_t\,dt + dW_t$, $X_0 = x_0$, as a stochastic integral. Is it an OU process? Find $E(X_t)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$.
Formula: §I7.1, §I7.3, §I1.5, §I1.4
Answer (§I7.1, §I7.3). Integrating factor $e^{t}$: by the product rule (no correction, $e^{t}$ is deterministic), $$d(e^{t}X_t) = e^{t}X_t\,dt + e^{t}dX_t = e^{t}dW_t .$$ Integrate from $0$ to $t$: $$X_t = x_0e^{-t} + \int_0^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\,dW_s .$$ Yes, OU: it is $dX = \theta(\mu - X)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ with $\theta=1$, $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$ — mean-reverting to $0$. The integrand is deterministic, so $X_t$ is Gaussian (§I1.5). $$E(X_t) = \mathbf{x_0e^{-t}},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \int_0^{t}e^{-2(t-s)}ds = \mathbf{\tfrac12\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)}$$ by the Itô isometry (§I1.4). As $t\to\infty$, $X_t\to N(0,\tfrac12)$.

Question. Derive and explain the single-period binomial market.
Formula: §M3, §M4, §A1
Answer (§M3, §M4, §A1). Two assets, two dates, $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\omega_2\}$: a bond $B_0 = 1$, $B_1 = 1+r$; a stock $S_0$, with $S_1 = uS_0$ on $\omega_1$ and $dS_0$ on $\omega_2$, $d

  • Risk-neutral probability. Solve $S_0 = \frac{1}{1+r}\big[q\,uS_0 + (1-q)\,dS_0\big]$: $$q = \frac{(1+r)-d}{u-d}\in(0,1)\iff d<1+r
  • Replication. For a claim $X$ with values $X_u, X_d$, hold $\Delta$ shares and $\beta$ in bonds so that $\Delta S_1 + \beta(1+r) = X$ in both states: $$\Delta = \frac{X_u - X_d}{(u-d)S_0},\qquad \beta = \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{(u-d)(1+r)}.$$
