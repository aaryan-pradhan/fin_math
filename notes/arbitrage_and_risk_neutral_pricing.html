Arbitrage and Risk-Neutral Pricing

In a market with no arbitrage, a derivative's price is its expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure, discounted at the risk-free rate — and the real-world probabilities never appear.

Step 1: Discounting

With continuously-compounded rate $\alpha$, an amount $v$ received at time $t$ is worth $ve^{-\alpha t}$ today. $e^{-\alpha t}$ is the discount factor. In discrete time with periodic rate $r$, the risk-free asset is the bond $B_t=(1+r)^tB_0$, and the discount factor is $(1+r)^{-t}$.
$\alpha=0.05$, $t=1$, $v=100$: today's worth is $100e^{-0.05}\approx 95.12$.
The first worked example below sets $\alpha=0$, so every discount factor is $1$. That is an assumption of the example, not a theorem. Step 9 restores $r>0$.

Step 2: The one-period binomial market

One stock, two dates. $S_0=100$ known. $S_1$ is a random variable on $\Omega=\{u,d\}$ with $$S_1=\begin{cases}200 & \text{on } u\\ 50 & \text{on } d\end{cases}$$ No probabilities are attached yet — deliberately.

Step 3: Payoff of a European call

A European call with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ gives the right, not the obligation, to buy one unit of the asset at price $K$ at time $T$. Its value at $T$ is $$X=(S_T-K)^+=\max(S_T-K,0).$$ The $\max$ is forced: if $S_T<K$ the holder declines and buys on the market, so the right is worth $0$ and never negative.
$K=150$. If $S_1=200$: pay 150 for a share worth 200, payoff $50$. If $S_1=50$: walk away, payoff $0$.

Step 4: The portfolio

At time 0 buy $x$ units of stock and $y$ units of the call, the call costing $c$ per unit. Both $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, and negative is allowed: $x<0$ is short-selling the stock, $y<0$ is writing (selling) calls. $$V_1=\begin{cases}200x+50y & \text{on } u\\ 50x+0\cdot y & \text{on } d\end{cases}\qquad \text{cost}=100x+cy.$$ The coefficients of $y$ are just the two call payoffs from Step 3.

Step 5: Killing the randomness

A portfolio is riskless if $V_1$ takes the same value in every outcome, i.e. $V_1$ is a constant, not a random variable.
Impose it: $$200x+50y=50x\;\Longrightarrow\;150x+50y=0\;\Longrightarrow\; y=-3x,$$ and then $V_1=50x$ with certainty.
$x=1,\,y=-3$: buy 1 share, write 3 calls. Up state $200-150=50$; down state $50-0=50$. Same number, so no probability is needed to value it.

Step 6: The gain and the definition of arbitrage

Cost at time 0 is $100x-3xc$; certain value at time 1 is $50x$. With $\alpha=0$, $$\text{gain}=50x-(100x-3xc)=x(3c-50).$$ This is a known number at time 0, not an expectation.
An arbitrage is a betting scheme requiring no net investment (or producing a net inflow) whose payoff is, with probability 1, non-negative in every outcome and positive somewhere. Risk-free profit.
Three cases:
  • $3c>50$: take $x>0$, gain positive.
  • $3c<50$: take $x<0$, gain positive.
  • $3c=50$: gain $=0$ for every $x$. No arbitrage.
So exactly one price survives: $c=\dfrac{50}{3}\approx16.67$.
$c=20$: $3c-50=10$, gain $=10x$. Buy 1 share (pay 100), write 3 calls (receive 60), net cost 40; holding is worth 50 in both states; certain profit 10. Scaling to $x=1000$ prints 10 000 risk-free.
$c=15$: $3c-50=-5$, so $x=-1,\,y=3$. Short 1 share (receive 100), buy 3 calls (pay 45), net inflow 55; holding at time 1 is $50x=-50$; certain profit $55-50=5$.
The price was pinned down using only the two possible values of $S_1$. How likely they are never entered the argument.

Step 7: The same price via a probability

Attach $\mathbb{P}(S_1=200)=p$, $\mathbb{P}(S_1=50)=1-p$, and treat each tradable asset as a wager whose return per unit is payoff minus cost. Impose $E[\text{return}]=0$ for every wager.
Stock: return is $100$ on $u$ and $-50$ on $d$, so $$100p-50(1-p)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;100=200p+50(1-p)\;\Longrightarrow\;p=\tfrac13.$$
Option: return is $50-c$ on $u$ and $-c$ on $d$, so $$(50-c)\tfrac13-c\cdot\tfrac23=0\;\Longrightarrow\;c=\tfrac{50}{3},$$ matching Step 6.
$p=\tfrac13$ is the risk-neutral (martingale) measure: the unique probability under which every wager has zero expected return. Equivalently $$c=E_p\big[(S_1-K)^+\big].$$ Price equals expected payoff under $p$. This is the engine of all option pricing; Black–Scholes is this formula with a continuum of outcomes.
$p$ is not the market's actual chance of a rise. The true probability could be $0.9$ and nothing changes, because $0.9$ never entered an equation. "No arbitrage" is a property of the prices; $p$ is the certificate of it.

Step 8: The arbitrage theorem

Outcomes $S=\{1,\dots,m\}$, exactly one occurs. There are $n$ wagers, and $r_i(j)$ is the return per unit staked on wager $i$ when outcome $j$ occurs. A betting scheme is $\underline x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (negative entries allowed), with total return in outcome $j$ $$R(j)=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,r_i(j).$$
Arbitrage theorem. Exactly one of the following holds.
  1. There is a probability vector $\underline p=(p_1,\dots,p_m)$, $p_j\ge0$, $\sum_j p_j=1$, with $\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^m p_j\,r_i(j)=0$ for every $i=1,\dots,n$.
  2. There is a betting scheme $\underline x$ with $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,r_i(j)>0$ for every $j=1,\dots,m$.
Slogan: no arbitrage $\iff$ a risk-neutral measure exists.
Proof that both cannot hold. Assume (i) and (ii). Then $$\sum_j p_jR(j)=\sum_j p_j\sum_i x_ir_i(j)=\sum_i x_i\underbrace{\sum_j p_jr_i(j)}_{=0}=0,$$ but $R(j)>0$ for all $j$ and $\sum_jp_j=1$ forces some $p_j>0$, giving $\sum_jp_jR(j)>0$. Contradiction.
The other half — that at least one of (i), (ii) holds — is a separating-hyperplane (Farkas duality) argument, not reproduced here.

Step 9: Three outcomes — incomplete markets

Now $S_1\in\{300,200,50\}$ with probabilities $(p_1,p_2,1-p_1-p_2)$, $S_0=100$, $K=150$, so payoffs are $150,50,0$. Fairness of the two wagers gives $$100=300p_1+200p_2+50(1-p_1-p_2),\qquad c=150p_1+50p_2.$$
First equation: $250p_1+150p_2=50$, so $p_2=\dfrac{1-5p_1}{3}$. Substituting, $$c=150p_1+\frac{50(1-5p_1)}{3}=\frac{200p_1+50}{3}.$$ Positivity: $p_2>0\Rightarrow p_1<\tfrac15$, and $p_3=\tfrac{2+2p_1}{3}>0$ always. Hence $p_1\in(0,\tfrac15)$ and $$c\in\left(\tfrac{50}{3},\,30\right).$$
Two equations, three unknowns: an interval of arbitrage-free prices, not one. With three outcomes and only two assets the option cannot be hedged, so no single price is forced. Such a market is incomplete.

Step 10: The instrument zoo

Forward. An agreement at time 0 to trade one unit at fixed price $K$ at time $T$. It is an obligation on both sides: long is obliged to buy, short to sell. Payoff to the long: $$S_T-K,$$ linear, and negative when $S_T<K$ — no $(\cdot)^+$, since no one may walk away.
"Forwards give the right to buy or sell" is wrong. The right-without-obligation is exactly what makes an instrument an option. A future is the same economics as a forward but exchange-traded, standardised and marked to market daily; a forward is a private bilateral agreement.
European call: $X=(S_T-K)^+$, exercisable only at the single date $T$.
American call: exercisable at any $\tau\in[0,T]$, paying $(S_\tau-K)^+$.
European put: right to sell at $K$, $X=(K-S_T)^+$.
$K=150$, $S_T\in\{200,50\}$: forward pays $+50$ or $-100$; call pays $50$ or $0$; put pays $0$ or $100$. The call is the forward with the $-100$ stripped off, which is precisely why it costs money upfront and the forward does not.
$\tau$ must be a stopping time: $\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t$. "The time at which $S_t$ attains its maximum on $[0,T]$" is not admissible, because at $t$ one cannot know whether the level will be exceeded later. Without this restriction the American price would be $\max_t(S_t-K)^+$, which is not attainable. See stopping_times.
The notes' line "price of American call $=(S_\tau-K)^+$" confuses payoff with price. The price is $\sup_\tau E_{p}\big[(1+r)^{-\tau}(S_\tau-K)^+\big]$ over stopping times $\tau\le T$. For a non-dividend-paying stock with $r\ge0$, early exercise of a call is never optimal, so its price equals the European call's; for a put it can be optimal and the prices differ.
Derivative security (= contingent claim) with maturity $T$: a random variable $X=X(\omega)=g\big(S_T(\omega)\big)\ge0$ paid to the holder at $T$. "Contingent" because its value depends on which $\omega$ is realised. Call: $g(s)=(s-K)^+$. Put: $g(s)=(K-s)^+$.

Step 11: Why the actuarial price is an arbitrage

Data: $S_0=100$; $S_1=130$ with real-world probability $0.4$, $S_1=80$ with $0.6$; European call, $K=110$, $T=1$; risk-free rate $r=0.05$ for both lending and borrowing. Payoff $X=(S_1-110)^+$ is $20$ up, $0$ down.
The classical (actuarial) valuation discounts the expectation under the true measure: $$E_p[X]=20(0.4)=8,\qquad C_0=\frac{8}{1.05}=7.62.$$
The risk-neutral valuation instead solves $100=\dfrac{1}{1.05}\big(130p^*+80(1-p^*)\big)$: $$105=80+50p^*\;\Longrightarrow\;p^*=0.5,\qquad C_0=\frac{20(0.5)}{1.05}=\frac{10}{1.05}=9.52.$$
Replication proves $9.52$ is the only possible price. Hold $\Delta$ shares and borrow $B$: $$\Delta=\frac{20-0}{130-80}=0.4,\qquad 0.4(130)-1.05B=20\Rightarrow B=\frac{32}{1.05}=30.48,$$ and the down state checks out: $0.4(80)-32=0$. This portfolio is the call in every state, and it costs $40-30.48=9.52$.
At a quoted price of $7.62$: buy the call ($-7.62$), short $0.4$ shares ($+40$), lend $30.48$ ($-30.48$). Net inflow today $9.52-7.62=1.90$; at $T$ the call's payoff cancels the short position in both states. Certain profit $1.90$ per contract, scalable without limit.
The actuarial method is correct for insurance, where risk is diversifiable but not hedgeable. It fails for derivatives because the payoff is replicable: the market already contains a portfolio with identical cash flows, and two identical cash-flow streams must carry the same price.
