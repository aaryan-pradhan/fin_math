1. Filtrations and martingales

1Filtration, adapted, measurable: what are they and how do I answer "is $Y$ $\mathcal F_n$-measurable?"
Formula
Filtration: $\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_t$ for $s\le t$. Natural filtration: $\mathcal F_n=\sigma(X_1,\dots,X_n)$. Adapted: $X_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable for every $t$.
Use when
"Is $Y$ $\mathcal F_n$-measurable?", "Is the process adapted?", or as condition (i) of a martingale.
How to plug
  1. Write $Y$ using the $X_k$'s.
  2. Only $X_1,\dots,X_n$ appear → Yes. Any $X_{n+1}$ or later appears → No.
Caveats
  • Any function of adapted things is adapted ($S_n^2$, $e^{S_n}$, $\max_{k\le n}S_k$).
  • A future increment ($\xi_{n+1}$, $W_{t+h}-W_t$) is never $\mathcal F_n$- / $\mathcal F_t$-measurable.
Example (practice)$S_n=\xi_1+\dots+\xi_n$. Is $S_{n+1}$ $\mathcal F_n$-measurable?
Step 1: $S_{n+1}=S_n+\xi_{n+1}$. Step 2: $\xi_{n+1}$ is a future step → No. Is $S_n^2$? It uses only $\xi_1..\xi_n$ → Yes.
2Martingale: the definition, sub and super, and what follows from it.
Formula
(i) adapted, (ii) $E|X_t|<\infty$, (iii) $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]=X_t$.
Sub-MG: $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]\ge X_t$ (goes up). Super-MG: $\le$ (goes down).
Consequences: $E[X_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_t]=X_t$ and $E[X_t]=E[X_0]$ for all $t$.
Use when
"Is $X_n$ a martingale? (Yes/No)", "Which statement is TRUE?", "sub or super?".
How to plug
  1. State (i) and (ii) in one line each.
  2. Compute $E[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]$ (card 3) and compare with $X_t$: equal → MG, bigger → sub, smaller → super.
Caveats
  • Constant mean is necessary, NOT sufficient: $E X_t=E X_0$ does not prove MG.
  • "Super" goes DOWN (the casino's game). Names are counter-intuitive.
Example — CT-1'26 Q8 (all sets)"Which statement is TRUE?" → pick the option "the mean of a martingale process is constant with respect to time", because $E[X_t]=E[X_0]$.
3How to compute $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]$ in one line.
Formula
If $A$ is known at time $n$ and $B$ is independent of $\mathcal F_n$: $\;E[A\cdot B\mid\mathcal F_n]=A\cdot E[B]$, $\;E[A+B\mid\mathcal F_n]=A+E[B]$.
Use when
Every discrete "is it a martingale?" question.
How to plug
  1. Write $X_{n+1}$ = (something known at $n$) combined with (the new step).
  2. Pull the known part out.
  3. Replace the new step by its mean.
  4. Compare with $X_n$.
Caveats
Step 3 needs the new step to be independent of the past (iid steps: fine).
Example — CT-2'25 Q3$X_n=2^nU_1\cdots U_n$, $U_i\sim U(0,1)$ iid. MG?
Step 1: $X_{n+1}=X_n\cdot 2U_{n+1}$. Step 2: $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n\,E[2U_{n+1}]$. Step 3: $E[2U]=2\cdot\frac12=1$. Step 4: $=X_n$ → Yes, MG.

2. Brownian motion and related processes

5Wiener process: axioms, and the 4-point test "is $X(t)$ a Wiener process?".
Formula
Axioms: $W(0)=0$; independent increments; $W(t)-W(s)\sim N(0,t-s)$; continuous paths.
Test: (1) $X(0)=0$, (2) Gaussian, (3) $E X(t)=0$, (4) $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\min(s,t)$.
Use when
"Short note on the Wiener process"; T/F "$V(t)=\dots$ is a Wiener process".
How to plug
  1. Check $X(0)=0$ first (quickest fail).
  2. Gaussian? (card 6: $f(t)W(g(t))$ always is.)
  3. Compute the covariance and compare with $\min(s,t)$.
Caveats
Covariance $e^{-\lambda|t-s|}$ (depends on $|t-s|$ only) means stationary OU, NOT Wiener.
Example — Midsem'25 Q2"Short note on the Wiener process" → write the 4 axioms, then add $\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t)$ and "paths continuous but nowhere differentiable".
6⭐ Master formula: $X(t)=f(t)\,W(g(t))$ with $f,g$ deterministic. Gaussian? Covariance? Wiener?
Formula
Always Gaussian, mean $0$, $\quad\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=f(s)f(t)\min\big(g(s),g(t)\big)$.
Wiener $\iff$ that equals $\min(s,t)$ and $X(0)=0$.
Use when
"Is $X(t)=e^{-t}W(e^{2t})$ Gaussian?", "Is $\frac1cW(c^2t)$ / $tW(1/t)$ a Wiener process?", "Cov of $X$?".
How to plug
  1. Read off $f$ and $g$.
  2. Gaussian question → Yes immediately.
  3. Wiener question → compute $f(s)f(t)\min(g(s),g(t))$ and $X(0)=f(0)W(g(0))$.
$X(t)$$f$, $g$CovWiener?
$\frac1cW(c^2t)$$\frac1c$, $c^2t$$\min(s,t)$Yes
$tW(1/t)$$t$, $\frac1t$$\frac{st}{\max(s,t)}=\min(s,t)$Yes
$e^{-\lambda t}W(e^{2\lambda t})$$e^{-\lambda t}$, $e^{2\lambda t}$$e^{-\lambda|t-s|}$No (OU)
Caveats
  • $f$ and $g$ must be non-random (no $W$ inside them), and $g\ge0$.
  • Gaussian ≠ Wiener: $e^{-t}W(e^{2t})$ has $X(0)=W(1)\neq0$.
Example — CT-1'26 Q3"$X(t)=e^{-t}W(e^{2t})$ is a Gaussian process (Yes/No)" → Step 1: $f=e^{-t}$, $g=e^{2t}$, both deterministic. Step 2: → Yes, no computation.
7The 4 transforms of BM that are again standard BM.
Formula
$\frac1cW(c^2t)$ (scaling), $\;-W(t)$ (symmetry), $\;tW(1/t)$ with $0$ at $t=0$ (time inversion), $\;W(T)-W(T-t)$ on $[0,T]$ (time reversal).
Use when
T/F "… is also a Wiener process".
How to plug
  1. Match the expression to a row → True.
  2. No match → run card 6 / card 5 test.
Caveats
$cW(t)$ with $c\ne\pm1$ is NOT standard BM (variance $c^2t$). Scaling needs the $\frac1c$ outside AND $c^2$ inside.
Example — Midsem'25 Q1(e)"$V(t)=\frac1cW(c^2t)$, $c>0$, is a Wiener process (T/F)" → scaling row → True. Check: $V(0)=0$, $\operatorname{Cov}=\frac1{c^2}\min(c^2s,c^2t)=\min(s,t)$ ✓.
8Lévy's characterization and the path facts (short notes).
Formula
Lévy: $X$ continuous, $X_0=0$, $X_t$ and $X_t^2-t$ both MGs $\Rightarrow$ standard BM.
Paths: continuous, nowhere differentiable; quadratic variation $\sum(\Delta W)^2\to t$, i.e. $(dW)^2=dt$; total variation $=\infty$; Markov.
Use when
"Short note", "explain quadratic variation", "prove the limit is BM".
How to plug
  1. Write the facts as sentences.
  2. Add the reason: $\Delta W\sim\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so $(\Delta W)^2\sim\Delta t$ survives.
Caveats
Quadratic variation is finite ($=t$) while total variation is infinite: don't mix them up.
Example — Endsem-style"Explain quadratic variation of BM" → $\sum_j(W_{t_j}-W_{t_{j-1}})^2\to t$ as the mesh $\to0$, so $(dW)^2=dt$; this is why Itô's formula has the extra $\frac12f''\,dt$ term.
9⭐ Distribution of any linear combination $\sum a_iW_{t_i}$.
Formula
$$\textstyle\sum_i a_iW_{t_i}\sim N\Big(0,\ \sum_i a_i^2t_i+2\sum_{i<j}a_ia_j\min(t_i,t_j)\Big)$$ Two terms: $\operatorname{Var}(aW_s+bW_t)=a^2s+b^2t+2ab\min(s,t)$. $\operatorname{Corr}(W_s,W_t)=\sqrt{s/t}$, $s\le t$.
Use when
"$4W_1+6W_3\sim$ ?", "$\operatorname{Var}(\dots)$", "$\operatorname{Cov}(W_1,W_4)$".
How to plug
  1. List coefficients $a_i$ and times $t_i$.
  2. Square terms: $a_i^2t_i$.
  3. Cross terms: $2a_ia_j\times$(smaller time).
  4. Add → variance; mean is $0$.
Caveats
Mean $0$ only for standard BM from $0$. With drift $\mu t$ or start $x_0$, add those to the mean (variance unchanged).
Example — CT-1'26 Q4$4W_1+6W_3\sim$? → Step 1: $a=(4,6)$, $t=(1,3)$. Step 2: $16(1)+36(3)=124$. Step 3: $2(4)(6)\min(1,3)=48$. Step 4: $N(0,172)$.
Midsem'25 Q1(d): $W_1-W_2+3W_4$ → squares $1+2+36=39$; cross $2[(-1)(1)+(3)(1)+(-3)(2)]=-8$ → $N(0,31)$.
10⭐ Any mixed moment $E[g(W_s)h(W_t)]$, $s<t$: increment splitting.
Formula
$W_t=W_s+\Delta$, $\;\Delta\sim N(0,t-s)$ independent of $W_s$.
Even powers of a normal (double factorial): if $X\sim N(0,v)$, $$E[X^{2k}]=(2k-1)!!\,v^k,\qquad (2k-1)!!=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k-1)=\frac{(2k)!}{2^k\,k!},\qquad E[X^{2k+1}]=0$$
$k$$E[Z^{2k}]$, $Z\sim N(0,1)$$E[W_t^{2k}]$$E[\Delta^{2k}]$, $\Delta\sim N(0,t-s)$
$1$$1!!=1$$t$$t-s$
$2$$3!!=1\cdot3=3$$3t^2$$3(t-s)^2$
$3$$5!!=1\cdot3\cdot5=15$$15t^3$$15(t-s)^3$
$4$$7!!=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=105$$105t^4$$105(t-s)^4$
Results: $E[W_sW_t]=\min(s,t)$, $\;E[W_s^2W_t^2]=s(2s+t)$ for $s\le t$.
Use when
"Compute $E(W_2^2W_3^2)$", "$E[W_2W_3^3]$", any product of BM at two times.
How to plug
  1. Let $s$ = the smaller time. Write $W_t=W_s+\Delta$.
  2. Expand the product.
  3. Delete terms with an odd power of $\Delta$ or of $W_s$.
  4. Use independence: $E[W_s^k\Delta^m]=E[W_s^k]E[\Delta^m]$.
Caveats
$s$ must be the SMALLER time; $\Delta$ is "later minus earlier".
Example — CT-1'26 Q2$E(W_2^2W_3^2)$ → $s=2$, $\Delta=W_3-W_2\sim N(0,1)$.
$E[W_2^2(W_2+\Delta)^2]=E W_2^4+2E[W_2^3]E\Delta+E W_2^2\,E\Delta^2=3(4)+0+2(1)=$ $14$. Shortcut: $s(2s+t)=2(7)=14$ ✓.
11⭐ Exponential of a normal: $E[e^X]$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(e^{W_s},e^{W_t})$.
Formula
$X\sim N(m,v)\Rightarrow E[e^X]=e^{m+v/2}$, $\;\operatorname{Var}(e^X)=e^{2m+v}(e^v-1)$.
$$\operatorname{Cov}(e^{W_s},e^{W_t})=e^{(s+t)/2}\big(e^{\min(s,t)}-1\big)$$
Use when
Any $E[e^{\dots}]$ with BM inside; "$Y_t=e^{W_t}$, find $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)$".
How to plug
  1. Write the exponent as a normal: find its mean $m$ and variance $v$ (card 9).
  2. $E[e^X]=e^{m+v/2}$.
  3. For Cov: $E[e^{W_s+W_t}]-E[e^{W_s}]E[e^{W_t}]$.
Caveats
Exponent must be normal (a linear combination of $W$'s). $E[e^{W_t^2}]$ is NOT this formula.
Example — CT-1'26 Q9$Y_t=e^{W_t}$, $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)$ → $s=1$, $t=2$: $e^{(1+2)/2}(e^{1}-1)=$ $e^{3/2}(e-1)$.
Rebuild: $W_1+W_2\sim N(0,1+2+2)=N(0,5)$, so $E[e^{W_1+W_2}]=e^{5/2}$; minus $e^{1/2}e^{1}=e^{3/2}$; $e^{5/2}-e^{3/2}=e^{3/2}(e-1)$ ✓.
12⭐ Is $f(t,W_t)$ a martingale? The one test.
Formula
$$f(t,W_t)\text{ is a MG}\iff\partial_tf+\tfrac12\partial_{ww}f=0$$ Family $e^{\lambda t}g(W_t)$: MG $\iff\lambda g+\frac12g''=0$. For $\sin$, $\cos$: $\lambda=\frac12$ exactly.

$e^{W_t}$, $e^{t}W_t$, $e^{t}\cos W_t$$\ne0$No
Use when
"Is $Y_t=\dots$ a martingale? (Yes/No)" with $Y$ a function of $t$ and $W_t$.
How to plug
  1. Compute $\partial_tf$ and $\partial_{ww}f$.
  2. Form $\partial_tf+\frac12\partial_{ww}f$.
  3. Zero → Yes. Not zero → No.

  • Only for functions of $(t,W_t)$. For a general Itô process use card 25 (drift of $dX$).

Example — CT-1'26 Q5"Is $Y_t=e^{t/2}\sin W_t$ a MG?" → Step 1: $\partial_tf=\frac12f$, $\partial_{ww}f=-f$. Step 2: $\frac12f-\frac12f=0$. Step 3: Yes. (Set with $e^t\cos W_t$: $f-\frac12f\ne0$ → No.)

14⭐ Distribution of $\int_0^Tf(t)\,dW_t$, and the Itô isometry.
Formula
$$f\text{ deterministic}:\quad\int_0^Tf(t)\,dW_t\sim N\Big(0,\ \int_0^Tf(t)^2\,dt\Big)$$ Isometry (any adapted $H$): $E\big[(\int_0^TH\,dW)^2\big]=\int_0^TE[H_t^2]\,dt$. Mean always $0$. Shortcut: $\int_0^1t^kdW_t\sim N(0,\frac{1}{2k+1})$.
Use when
"Compute the distribution of $\int_0^1t\,dW_t$", "$\operatorname{Var}(\int\dots dW)$".
How to plug
  1. Check the integrand has no $W$ in it.
  2. Square it, integrate $dt$ → the variance.
  3. Answer $N(0,\text{that})$.
Caveats
Integrand containing $W$ (e.g. $\int W\,dW$): mean $0$ and isometry still hold, but it is NOT normal ($\int_0^tW\,dW=\frac12W_t^2-\frac t2$).
Example — CT-1'26 Q1 / CT-2'25 Q7$\int_0^1t\,dW_t$ → $f=t$ deterministic; $\int_0^1t^2dt=\frac13$ → $N(0,\frac13)$. CT-2'25: $\int_0^1t^2dW_t$ → $\int_0^1t^4dt=\frac15$ → $N(0,\frac15)$.
15Probabilities for $W_t$ (and BM started at $x_0$); the heat equation.
Formula
$P(W_t\le x)=\Phi\big(\frac{x}{\sqrt t}\big)$; density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-x^2/(2t)}$. General $X\sim N(m,v)$: $P(X\le x)=\Phi\big(\frac{x-m}{\sqrt v}\big)$.
Transition density $p(t;x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-(y-x)^2/(2t)}$ solves $\partial_tp=\frac12\partial_{yy}p$.
Use when
"$P(W(4)>3)$", "BM started at 5, $P(W(1)<7)$", "show the density solves the heat equation".
How to plug
  1. Find the mean $m$ (start point, drift) and variance $v$ ($t$, or $\sigma^2t$).
  2. Standardize: $\frac{x-m}{\sqrt v}$.
  3. Look up $\Phi$.
Caveats
Divide by $\sqrt t$, NEVER by $t$. This is the most common lost mark.
Example (sheet)$P(W(4)>3)$ → $m=0$, $\sqrt v=2$: $1-\Phi(\frac32)=$ $0.0668$. BM from $5$: $P(W(1)<7)=\Phi(\frac{7-5}{1})=\Phi(2)=$ $0.9772$.
16⭐ Conditioning on a value of BM: forward, backward (bridge), pinned.
Formula
$(X,Y)$ jointly normal: $X\mid Y{=}y\sim N\Big(\mu_X+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}}{\operatorname{Var}Y}(y-\mu_Y),\ \operatorname{Var}X-\frac{\operatorname{Cov}^2}{\operatorname{Var}Y}\Big)$
GivenLaw
forward: $W_t\mid W_s{=}a$, $s<t$$N(a,\ t-s)$
backward: $W_s\mid W_t{=}b$, $s<t$$N\big(\frac stb,\ \frac{s(t-s)}t\big)$
pinned $W_0{=}a$, $W_t{=}b$$N\big(a+\frac st(b-a),\ \frac{s(t-s)}t\big)$

⭐ Most general: $Y_t=x_0+\mu t+\sigma W_t$, two known values, $t$ in between
$s_1<t<s_2$, $\;Y_{s_1}=a_1$, $\;Y_{s_2}=a_2$: $$Y_t\mid(Y_{s_1}{=}a_1,\,Y_{s_2}{=}a_2)\ \sim\ N\Big(\underbrace{\tfrac{s_2-t}{s_2-s_1}}_{\text{weight on }a_1}a_1+\underbrace{\tfrac{t-s_1}{s_2-s_1}}_{\text{weight on }a_2}a_2,\ \ \sigma^2\,\frac{(t-s_1)(s_2-t)}{s_2-s_1}\Big)$$ The drift $\mu$ and the start $x_0$ do NOT appear: once both ends are pinned, the drift is already inside $a_1$ and $a_2$. Plain $W$ is $x_0=0$, $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$.
What is knownLaw of $Y_t$

Intuition: a weighted average. The mean is an average of the two known values, and the two weights add to $1$. Each value gets the weight of the distance to the OTHER end. So the closer $t$ is to $s_1$, the more $a_1$ counts. At $t=s_1$ the weights are $(1,0)$ and you get $a_1$; at $t=s_2$ you get $a_2$; at the midpoint it's $\frac{a_1+a_2}2$. On a graph, it's the straight line joining $(s_1,a_1)$ and $(s_2,a_2)$, read at $t$.
Variance = $\sigma^2$ × (distance to the left end) × (distance to the right end) ÷ (gap). It is $0$ at both ends (you know the value there) and largest at the midpoint, where it is $\sigma^2\frac{s_2-s_1}4$.
Only the nearest known values matter (Markov): any value known before $s_1$ or after $s_2$ is irrelevant. Every row of the table above is this formula: backward is $s_1=0$, $a_1=0$; pinned is $s_1=0$.
  • $t$ must lie BETWEEN the two known times. If $t>s_2$, only the latest value counts: forward row $N\big(a_2+\mu(t-s_2),\ \sigma^2(t-s_2)\big)$.
  • Between two known values, drop $\mu$; going forward past the last known value, keep $\mu$.
  • Check whether they gave $\sigma$ or $\sigma^2$.
  • Not for GBM: there, take logs first ($\ln Y$ is BM with drift), apply this to $\ln Y$, then convert back.
Example (practice)$W_1=2$, $W_5=10$; law of $W_2$? → $s_1=1$, $s_2=5$, gap $4$. Weights: on $a_1$: $\frac{5-2}{4}=\frac34$; on $a_2$: $\frac{2-1}4=\frac14$. Mean $\frac34(2)+\frac14(10)=1.5+2.5=$ $4$. Variance $\frac{(1)(3)}4=$ $0.75$. So $W_2\sim N(4,\,0.75)$.
Check with the straight line: slope $\frac{10-2}{4}=2$ per unit time, so at $t=2$: $2+2(1)=4$ ✓.
With drift and volatility: $Y_t=2t+3W_t$, $Y_1=5$, $Y_5=13$, law of $Y_2$? → same weights $\frac34,\frac14$: mean $3.75+3.25=$ $7$ (the drift $2$ is not used); variance $9\cdot\frac{1\cdot3}4=$ $6.75$.
Sheet: $Y=3W$, $Y_{10}=12$, law of $Y_4$ → $s_1=0$, $a_1=0$: mean $\frac4{10}(12)=$ $4.8$, variance $9\cdot\frac{4\cdot6}{10}=$ $21.6$.
Bridge $B(s)=W(s)-\frac stW(t)$: $\operatorname{Cov}(B_u,B_v)=\min(u,v)-\frac{uv}t$. Use when
"Given $W(3)=2$, find $E[W(1)]$ / $P(W(1)<x)$", "Brownian bridge".
How to plug
  1. Which time is known: earlier (forward) or later (backward)?
  2. Pick the row; plug $s$, $t$, the known value.
  3. For a probability, standardize with card 15.
Caveats
Forward: mean stays, variance grows. Backward: mean is the straight line, variance is $0$ at both ends. Don't swap them.
Example (sheet)"Given $W(3)=2$, find $E[W(1)]$" → backward, $s=1$, $t=3$, $b=2$: mean $\frac13(2)=$ $\frac23$, variance $\frac{1\cdot2}{3}=\frac23$. "Given $W(1)=2$, $E[W(3)]$" → forward → $2$.
17⭐ BM with drift $X_t=\mu t+\sigma W_t$: which barrier first, max ever, exit time, best exercise level.
Formula

3. Itô's formula

21⭐ Itô's formula (general) and the multiplication table.
Formula
$dt\cdot dt=0,\quad dt\cdot dW=0,\quad dW\cdot dW=dt$. For $dX=a\,dt+b\,dW$: $(dX)^2=b^2\,dt$. $$df(t,X_t)=\partial_tf\,dt+\partial_xf\,dX_t+\tfrac12\partial_{xx}f\,b^2\,dt$$ For $X=W$: $\;df=\big(\partial_tf+\frac12\partial_{xx}f\big)dt+\partial_xf\,dW$.
Product: $d(XY)=X\,dY+Y\,dX+b_Xb_Y\,dt$.
Use when
"Compute $dY_t$", "derive Itô's formula", every SDE question.
How to plug
  1. Identify $a$ and $b$ of $dX$ (for $W$: $a=0$, $b=1$).
  2. Compute $\partial_tf$, $\partial_xf$, $\partial_{xx}f$.
  3. Plug; collect the $dt$ part (drift) and the $dW$ part (diffusion).
Caveats
  • No $\partial_{tt}$ term. Only the $x$-variable gets the $\frac12$ correction.
  • If $f$ is linear in $x$ (e.g. $g(t)W_t$), the correction is $0$.
Example — Midsem'25 Q2(b)"Derive Itô's formula" [2 marks] → Taylor: $df=f_t\,dt+f_x\,dX+\frac12f_{xx}(dX)^2+\dots$; table: $(dW)^2=dt$ because $\Delta W\sim\sqrt{\Delta t}$; $dt^2$, $dt\,dW$ and higher orders vanish → the formula.

24⭐ Mean and variance of a solution of $dX=a(t,X)\,dt+b(t,X)\,dW$ without solving it.
Formula

  3. Second moment ODE: plug $a$, $b$, replace $E[X]$ by $m(t)$; integrate.
  4. Subtract $m^2$.

33⭐ Multi-period binomial: backward induction, hedge at each node, self-financing.
Formula
At node $S_{t-1}=s$: $$V_{t-1}[s]=\frac{p^*V_t[us]+(1-p^*)V_t[ds]}{1+r},\qquad V_T=g(S_T)$$ $\Delta_t(s)=\dfrac{V_t[us]-V_t[ds]}{us-ds}$, $\;b_t(s)=\dfrac{uV_t[ds]-dV_t[us]}{(1+r)^t(u-d)}$ (number of bonds, bond price $(1+r)^t$).
Self-financing: $\Delta_tS_t+b_t(1+r)^t=\Delta_{t+1}S_t+b_{t+1}(1+r)^t$.
Direct: $V_0=\frac{1}{(1+r)^T}\sum_{j=0}^T\binom Tj(p^*)^j(1-p^*)^{T-j}g(u^jd^{T-j}S_0)$.
Use when
"$T=2$ or $3$ periods, price the option and give the strategy at each node", "verify self-financing".
How to plug
  1. Draw the tree; leaves $S_0u^jd^{T-j}$.
  2. Payoffs at the leaves.
  3. Step back one period at a time with the $V_{t-1}$ formula.
  4. At each node: $\Delta_t$, $b_t$.
Caveats
  • $b_t$ counts bonds, its cash value is $b_t(1+r)^t$.
  • Path-dependent payoffs: don't recombine the tree.
Example (sheet)$S_0=100$, $u=2$, $d=\frac12$, $r=\frac14$, call $K=100$, $T=2$.
$p^*=\frac{1.25-0.5}{1.5}=\frac12$. Leaves $400,100,25$ → payoffs $300,0,0$. $V_1[200]=\frac{\frac12(300)}{1.25}=120$, $V_1[50]=0$. $V_0=\frac{\frac12(120)}{1.25}=$ $48$. Direct: $\frac{\frac14(300)}{1.5625}=48$ ✓.
34⭐ Finite single-period market: EMM, Theorems 2, 3, 4.
Formula
EMM: all $p_k^*>0$, $\sum p_k^*=1$, and for each stock $i$: $\sum_kp_k^*S_1^i(\omega_k)=(1+r)S_0^i$.
ThmStatement
2NA $\iff$ an EMM exists $\iff S_0\in D_0$ (strictly inside the hull of discounted payoffs)
3$E^*[\frac{V_1}{1+r}]=V_0$ for any strategy; attainable $X$: price $X^*=E^*[\frac{X}{1+r}]$, same for every EMM
4complete $\iff$ EMM unique (needs states $N$ = assets $n+1$, $\det A\ne0$)
Use when
"Find all EMMs", "is the market arbitrage-free / complete?", "price the claim", 3 or more branches from one node.
How to plug
  1. Unknowns $p_1^*..p_N^*$: one equation per stock + sum $=1$.
  2. Solve; keep only all-positive solutions. None → arbitrage. One → complete. A line → incomplete.
  3. Price $X$: compute $E^*[\frac{X}{1+r}]$ with the free parameter left in. It cancels → attainable, one price; it doesn't → a price interval.
Caveats
Incomplete market: no single price for a non-attainable claim, only an interval.
Example (sheet)$100\to\{300,200,50\}$, $K=150$, $r=0$ → stock: $300p_1+200p_2+50p_3=100$, sum $=1$ → $p_2=\frac{1-5p_1}3$, $p_1\in(0,\frac15)$ (a line: incomplete). Call: $c=150p_1+50p_2=\frac{200p_1+50}3$ → $c\in$ $(\frac{50}3,30)$.

7. Martingales and claim pricing

35⭐ The three facts of claim pricing, and Black–Scholes.
Formula
(1) EMM with discounted price a MG $\Rightarrow$ NA. (2) Attainable $X$: $P_t(X)=E^*\big[(1+r)^{t-T}X\mid\mathcal F_t\big]$ (continuous: $e^{-r(T-t)}$). (3) EMM unique $\Rightarrow$ complete.
Black–Scholes: under $P^*$ the drift $\mu\to r$, $S_t=S_0e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma W_t^*}$. $$C=S_0\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2),\quad d_1=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r+\frac{\sigma^2}2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\quad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T$$
Use when
"Short note on martingales and claim pricing", "is the BS market arbitrage-free / complete?", "price a call with BS".
How to plug
  1. Find the EMM ($p^*$, or $\mu\to r$).
  2. Price = discounted $E^*$ of the payoff.
  3. BS: $d_1$, $d_2$, two $\Phi$ look-ups; put by parity.
Caveats
  • The real drift $\mu$ never appears in a price.
  • Binomial discounts with $(1+r)^{-T}$, BS with $e^{-rT}$. Don't mix.
Example (sheet)$S_0=K=100$, $r=0.05$, $\sigma=0.2$, $T=1$ → $d_1=\frac{0+0.07}{0.2}=0.35$, $d_2=0.15$; $C=100(0.6368)-95.12(0.5596)=$ $10.45$; put $=10.45-100+95.12=$ $5.57$.
