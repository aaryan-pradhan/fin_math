Binomial Market and Black–Scholes Problems

Elements of Stochastic Modelling, §13.10 Problems 1–11. Each problem is followed by its solution. Sources: Assign Financial Maths problem.pdf (problems) and Solution Financial Maths problem.pdf (solutions).

Notation: the single-period binomial market has $S_1\in\{dS_0,\,uS_0\}$ and bond $B_0=1$, $B_1=1+r$. No arbitrage (NA) $\iff d<1+r

Problem 1 — Call price as a function of strike

Problem. For a single-period binomial market, sketch the graph of the call price $C=C(K)$ as a function of the strike $K$. Which features of this function do you think will be common to the call prices in more general markets as well?
Solution. $C(K)$ (Example 13.3) is piecewise linear in $K$:
  • $C(0)=S_0$;
  • slope $-1/(1+r)$ for $K
  • slope $-p^*/(1+r)>-1/(1+r)$ for $dS_0
  • slope $0$ for $K>uS_0$, where $C(K)=0$.
Features common to all calls:
  1. $C(0)=S_0$. A zero strike means you just receive the share at maturity, so it costs the current share price.
  2. $C(K)$ is decreasing in $K$. A larger strike gives a smaller payoff, so it costs less.
  3. $C(K)\to0$ as $K\to\infty$, since the payoff vanishes.

Problem 2 — Straddle payoff

Problem. A straddle is a portfolio of a call and a put on the same underlying stock, with the same strike and the same expiry. Plot the payoff function of the straddle, as a function of the terminal stock price, on one share of the stock.
Solution. The payoff is the sum of the call and put payoffs, (13.5) and (13.6): $$(s-K)^+ + (s-K)^- = |s-K|.$$ It is a V-shape with its vertex at $s=K$: slope $-1$ to the left, slope $+1$ to the right.

Problem 3 — Bull spread

Problem. A bull spread buys a call with strike $K_1$ and sells a call with strike $K_2>K_1$, both on the same stock with the same expiry $T$. It is a contingent claim.
  1. Plot the payoff function of a bull spread.
  2. Which is cheaper: a bull spread or a call with strike $K_1$ (same stock, same expiry)? Explain.
  3. In a single-period binomial market with $S_0=5$, $S_1\in\{4,6\}$, $r=0.1$, $K_1=3$ and $K_2=5$, price the bull spread with the risk-neutral ("fair price") formula (13.11).
  4. Under the assumptions of (iii), construct a replicating portfolio for the bull spread and verify that it replicates the claim.
Solution.
  1. $g(s)=(s-K_1)^+-(s-K_2)^+$. It is piecewise linear: $0$ for $s\le K_1$, slope $1$ on $[K_1,K_2]$, and flat at $K_2-K_1$ for $s\ge K_2$. So $g(0)=0$ and $g(\infty)=K_2-K_1$.
  2. The spread is cheaper. Its payoff equals the call's for $s\le K_2$ and is strictly smaller for $s>K_2$.
  3. $d=\tfrac45$, $u=\tfrac65$, and $d<1+r=1.1
  4. $$\Delta=\frac{g(6)-g(4)}{6-4}=\frac12,\qquad b=\frac{1.2\,g(4)-0.8\,g(6)}{1.1\times0.4}=-\frac{10}{11}.$$ Check: $V_1(u)=\tfrac12\cdot6-\tfrac{10}{11}\cdot1.1=3-1=2=g(6)$ and $V_1(d)=\tfrac12\cdot4-1=1=g(4)$. It replicates.

Problem 4 — Arbitrage-free price and replication

Problem. A single-period binomial market has $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\omega_2\}$, $S_0=5$, $B_0=1$, $S_1(\omega_1)=20/3$, $S_1(\omega_2)=40/9$, and $r=1/9$.
  1. Show that the market is arbitrage-free.
  2. A contingent claim $X$ has $X(\omega_1)=7$ and $X(\omega_2)=2$. Find its arbitrage-free value at $t=0$.
  3. Construct a replicating portfolio for $X$ and verify that its values at $t=0$ and $t=1$ equal the price from (ii) and the claim $X$.
Solution.
  1. Verify (13.2): $d=S_1(\omega_2)/S_0=\tfrac89$ and $u=S_1(\omega_1)/S_0=\tfrac43=\tfrac{12}9$, so $d<1+r=\tfrac{10}9
  2. $$p^*=\frac{10/9-8/9}{12/9-8/9}=\frac12,\qquad X^*=\frac{1}{1+r}E^*X=\frac9{10}\left[\tfrac12\cdot7+\tfrac12\cdot2\right]=\frac{81}{20}=\mathbf{4.05}.$$
  3. $$\Delta=\frac{7-2}{20/3-40/9}=\frac94,\qquad b=\frac{(12/9)\cdot2-(8/9)\cdot7}{(10/9)(4/9)}=-\frac{36}5=-7.2 .$$ At $t=0$: $V_0=\tfrac94\cdot5-\tfrac{36}5=\tfrac{81}{20}$, which agrees with (ii). At $t=1$: $$V_1=\Delta S_1+b(1+r)=\begin{cases}\tfrac94\cdot\tfrac{20}3-\tfrac{36}5\cdot\tfrac{10}9=7 & \omega=\omega_1,\\[2pt] \tfrac94\cdot\tfrac{40}9-\tfrac{36}5\cdot\tfrac{10}9=2 & \omega=\omega_2.\end{cases}$$

Problem 5 — Put price and put–call parity

Problem. For a single-period binomial market:
  1. sketch the graph of the put price $P=P(K)$ as a function of the strike $K$;
  2. price a put with strike $K=3.8$ on one share with $S_0=4$, if $r=5\%$ and $S_1\in\{3.6,\,4.6\}$;
  3. find the $t=0$ value of the call with the same strike.
Solution.
  1. The price was computed in Example 13.4. Like the call price in Problem 1, it is piecewise linear, with kinks at $dS_0$ and $uS_0$. Alternatively, put–call parity (13.15) expresses it through the call price: flip the Problem 1 plot upside down.
  2. $d=0.9$, $u=1.15$, $d<1.05
  3. By put–call parity (13.15): $C=S_0+P-\dfrac{K}{1+r}=4+\dfrac8{105}-\dfrac{3.8}{1.05}=\dfrac{16}{35}\approx\mathbf{0.457}$.

Problem 6 — Single-period trinomial market

Problem. A single-period trinomial market has $\Omega=\{d,m,u\}$, with $S_1(d)=dS_0$, $S_1(m)=mS_0$, $S_1(u)=uS_0$ where $0<d<m<u$. Bond prices are $B_0=1$ and $B_1=1+r$, with $d<1+r<u$.
  1. Use Theorem 13.2 to verify (or disprove) that the market is arbitrage-free.
  2. Let $X$ be a contingent claim with values $X_d$, $X_m$, $X_u$, and assume $X_d<X_m<X_u$. Draw the set of all hedges $(\Delta,b)$ for the claim (as in Fig. 13.3 for the binomial market). Is there a perfect hedge (a replicating portfolio) for $X$?
Solution.
  1. NA is equivalent to the existence of $p_j^*>0$ with $\sum_{j=1}^3p_j^*=1$ and $S_0=\frac1{1+r}(p_1^*dS_0+p_2^*mS_0+p_3^*uS_0)$, i.e. $p_1^*d+p_2^*m+p_3^*u=1+r$. Divide by $p_1^*+p_3^*=1-p_2^*$: $$\underbrace{\frac{p_1^*}{p_1^*+p_3^*}}_{=:p}d+\underbrace{\frac{p_3^*}{p_1^*+p_3^*}}_{=1-p}u=\frac{1+r}{1-p_2^*}-\frac{p_2^*}{1-p_2^*}m.$$ For $p_2^*>0$ small enough the right-hand side is arbitrarily close to $1+r$, so it still lies in $(d,u)$. As in the binomial case, there is then a $p\in(0,1)$ with $pd+(1-p)u$ equal to the right-hand side. So an EMM $(p_j^*)$ exists and the market is arbitrage-free.
  2. The set of hedges is the intersection of three half-planes in the $(\Delta,b)$-plane, each bounded below by the line $\Delta kS_0+b(1+r)=X_k$, $k=d,m,u$. A perfect hedge would be a point common to all three lines. In general the three lines do not meet at one point, so no perfect hedge exists: the market is incomplete.

Problem 7 — Two stocks, three states

Problem. A single-period trinomial market has two stocks and one bond with $r=0$. $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3\}$, $S_0^1=4$, $S_0^2=5$, and at $t=1$: $$S_1^1(\omega_1)=6,\ S_1^2(\omega_1)=6;\qquad S_1^1(\omega_2)=4,\ S_1^2(\omega_2)=4;\qquad S_1^1(\omega_3)=2,\ S_1^2(\omega_3)=7.$$
  1. Plot the $t=0$ and $t=1$ stock prices as points in the $(S^1,S^2)$-plane.
  2. Use Theorem 13.2 to prove or disprove that the market is arbitrage-free.
  3. Find the EMM $\mathbf P^*$ and use the Completeness Theorem 13.4 to prove or disprove that the market is complete.
  4. Price the call on one share of stock 1 with expiry $T=1$ and strike $K=5$.
  5. Find the strategy replicating the call from (iv). Do we really need stock 2 to replicate the call on stock 1?
Solution.
  1. The $t=1$ points are $(6,6)$, $(4,4)$ and $(2,7)$; the $t=0$ point is $(4,5)$.
  2. NA holds iff an EMM $\mathbf P^*$ exists. Equivalently, the point $\mathbf S_0=(4,5)$ lies inside the triangle $\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf S_1(\omega_1),\mathbf S_1(\omega_2),\mathbf S_1(\omega_3)\}$, which the plot in (i) confirms. Arbitrage-free.
  3. Solve $\mathbf S_0=\sum_jp_j^*\mathbf S_1(\omega_j)$ componentwise: $$\begin{cases}4=6p_1^*+4p_2^*+2p_3^*\\ 5=6p_1^*+4p_2^*+7p_3^*\\ 1=p_1^*+p_2^*+p_3^*\end{cases}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_1^*=0.2,\ p_2^*=0.6,\ p_3^*=0.2 .$$ The EMM is unique, so the market is complete.
  4. For $X=(S_1^1-5)^+$: $$X^*=E^*X=0.2(6-5)^++0.6(4-5)^++0.2(2-5)^+=\mathbf{0.2}.$$
  5. Find $(\Delta^1,\Delta^2,b)$ with $\Delta^1S_1^1(\omega)+\Delta^2S_1^2(\omega)+b(1+r)=X(\omega)$ for every $\omega$: $$\begin{cases}6\Delta^1+6\Delta^2+b=1\\ 4\Delta^1+4\Delta^2+b=0\\ 2\Delta^1+7\Delta^2+b=0\end{cases}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(\Delta^1,\Delta^2,b)=(0.3,\,0.2,\,-2).$$ Yes, stock 2 is needed. This is the unique solution of the replication system, and it has $\Delta^2\neq0$.

Problem 9 — Four-period binomial call

Problem. A four-period binomial market has $u=4/3$, $d=2/3$ and $r=0$ (prices already discounted). With $S_0=81$, use the diagram method to price a call with maturity $T=4$ and strike $K=120$.
Solution. $p^*=\dfrac{1-2/3}{4/3-2/3}=0.5$. The five terminal prices are $16, 32, 64, 128, 256$, with call payoffs $(S_4-120)^+=0,0,0,8,136$. Working backwards (equivalently, binomial weights $\binom4k/16$): $$C=\frac{4\cdot8+1\cdot136}{16}=\mathbf{10.5}.$$

Problem 10 — BHP call and implied volatility

Problem. On 28 July 2000 ($t=0$), the BHP stock price on the ASX was $S_0=18.50$.
  1. With continuously compounded rate $r=0.062$ and volatility $\sigma=0.2$, use the Black–Scholes formula to price the call with maturity one month later ($T=1/12$ year) and strike $K=18.50$.
  2. The real-life buy/sell prices of the option were $0.57/0.63$. Taking their mean as the market price, find the volatility for which the Black–Scholes formula comes closest to the market price. This value is the implied volatility.
Solution.
  1. $$d_1=\frac{\ln1+(0.062+0.02)/12}{0.2\sqrt{1/12}}=0.1184,\qquad d_2=d_1-0.2\sqrt{1/12}=0.0606,$$ $$C=18.5\,\Phi(0.1184)-18.5\,e^{-0.062/12}\,\Phi(0.0606)\approx\mathbf{0.474}.$$
  2. Solve $C(\sigma)=0.60$ numerically (for example by bisection): $\sigma_{\text{imp}}\approx\mathbf{0.2594}$.
The source solution prints "$C\approx0.1184$", but that number is $d_1$. The call price is $0.474$. As a check, the implied volatility $0.2594$ it quotes is only consistent with $C(0.2)=0.474<0.60$.

Problem 11 — Call and put at two maturities

Problem. The current stock price is $S_0=100$, with volatility $40\%$ and continuously compounded rate $r=4\%$ (time unit one year). Use the Black–Scholes formula and put–call parity to price the call and put with strike $K=110$ and time to maturity (i) 18 months and (ii) one month. Comment on your findings, in particular on the prices in (ii).
Solution. $P=C-S_0+Ke^{-rT}$.
  1. $T=1.5$: $C\approx\mathbf{17.95}$, $P\approx\mathbf{21.54}$.
  2. $T=1/12$: $C\approx\mathbf{1.46}$, $P\approx\mathbf{11.09}$.
Both put prices are close to $(S_0-K)^-=10$, but the call is much cheaper in (ii). With one month left, the stock is unlikely to move much. If $S_T$ stays near $100$ the call is nearly worthless, while the put pays close to $(100-110)^-=10$. The call keeps some value only because $S_T>110$ is still possible.
