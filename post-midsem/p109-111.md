# p.109–111: Thm 3, the arbitrage pricing theorem

## Notation check

Running example, same as before: $n = 2$ stocks, $N = 3$ outcomes, $r = 0.25$, and the unique EMM $p^* = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25)$ we found on p.107. Rows are time and outcome (4), columns are the securities (3):

| | bond | stock 1 | stock 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_1$ | $1.25$ | $2$ | $1$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_2$ | $1.25$ | $0.5$ | $2$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_3$ | $1.25$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |

## The statement (p.109)

**Theorem 3 (the arbitrage pricing theorem).** In a single-period arbitrage-free market, the discounted value process $\{V_t (1+r)^{-t}\}_{t=0,1}$ of any trading strategy $(\Delta, b)$ is a MG under any EMM $p^*$:

$$E^*\left(\frac{V_1}{1+r}\right) = V_0 \qquad (1)$$

In particular, if $X$ is an attainable claim in the market, then its time $t = 0$ value is given by (p.110)

$$X^* := E^*\left(\frac{X}{1+r}\right) \qquad (2)$$

where the RHS does not depend on the choice of the EMM $p^*$.

So Thm 2 said each discounted stock is a fair game under $P^*$. Thm 3 says every portfolio built from them is a fair game too, and that gives us a price for any payoff we can build.

## Attainable claim (p.110)

A claim is considered attainable if there exists a self-financing strategy that matches the claim capital at the maturity. The strategy is called a hedging strategy.

In one period, "self-financing" just means: I pick $(\Delta, b)$ at $t = 0$, hold it to $t = 1$, and put no money in or take none out in between. So $X$ is attainable when some $(\Delta, b)$ has $V_1(\omega) = X(\omega)$ for every $\omega$.

## Proof of (1) (p.110)

Take any strategy $(\Delta, b)$. Start from the left side of (1) and substitute $V_1 = \Delta \cdot S_1 + b(1+r)$:

$$E^*\left(\frac{V_1}{1+r}\right) = E^*\left(\frac{\Delta \cdot S_1 + b(1+r)}{1+r}\right)$$

Split the fraction. The bond part is $\frac{b(1+r)}{1+r} = b$, a fixed number, and the expectation of a fixed number is itself (the $p_k^*$ add to $1$):

$$= E^*\left(\frac{\Delta \cdot S_1}{1+r}\right) + b$$

$\Delta$ is a fixed vector, so I move it outside the expectation:

$$= \Delta \cdot E^*\left(\frac{S_1}{1+r}\right) + b$$

Now use Thm 2. Under an EMM, $E^*\left(\frac{S_1}{1+r}\right) = S_0$:

$$= \Delta \cdot S_0 + b$$

And that is exactly the value at $t = 0$:

$$= V_0 \qquad (3)$$

$\therefore$ (1) holds.

Numbers. Take $\Delta = (1, 1)$ and $b = 1$: one of each stock and one bond. Rows are time and outcome (4), columns are the securities plus the sum (4). Each cell is holding $\times$ price:

| | bond | stock 1 | stock 2 | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $3$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_1$ | $1 \times 1.25 = 1.25$ | $1 \times 2 = 2$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $4.25$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_2$ | $1 \times 1.25 = 1.25$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $1 \times 2 = 2$ | $3.75$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_3$ | $1 \times 1.25 = 1.25$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $2.75$ |

So $V_0 = 3$. Now the left side of (1): $E^*(V_1) = 0.5 \times 4.25 + 0.25 \times 3.75 + 0.25 \times 2.75 = 2.125 + 0.9375 + 0.6875 = 3.75$, and $\frac{3.75}{1.25} = 3 = V_0$.

Check, line (3) instead: $\Delta \cdot S_0 + b = 1 \times 1 + 1 \times 1 + 1 = 3$. Same.

## Proof of (2) (p.110–111)

Now suppose $X$ is an attainable claim, i.e., for some portfolio $(\Delta, b)$ one has

$$V_1(\omega) = X(\omega), \quad \omega \in \Omega \qquad (4)$$

(1) & (4) imply that

$$V_0 = E^*\left(\frac{V_1}{1+r}\right) = E^*\left(\frac{X}{1+r}\right) = X^* \qquad (5)$$

The first equality is (1). The second substitutes $V_1 = X$ from (4), outcome by outcome. The notes write "(1) & (2)" here, but it's (1) and (4) that are used: (2) is what we're proving.

Which proves (2). Why is $V_0$ the right price? If $X$ were sold at $t = 0$ for anything other than $V_0$, I could sell the dearer one of $X$ and the hedge, buy the cheaper, and pocket the gap at $t = 0$. At $t = 1$ the two pay the same in every outcome and cancel. That's an arbitrage, so the price must be $V_0$.

Numbers. Take a call on stock 1 with strike $1$: $X = (S_1^1 - 1)^+$, so $X = (1, 0, 0)$ on $(\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3)$. First find the hedge. One row per outcome (3), unknowns $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$, $b$:

| | stock 1 | stock 2 | bond | $= X$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\omega_1$ | $2\Delta_1$ | $+ 1\Delta_2$ | $+ 1.25b$ | $= 1$ |
| $\omega_2$ | $0.5\Delta_1$ | $+ 2\Delta_2$ | $+ 1.25b$ | $= 0$ |
| $\omega_3$ | $0.5\Delta_1$ | $+ 1\Delta_2$ | $+ 1.25b$ | $= 0$ |

Subtract row $\omega_3$ from row $\omega_2$: $\Delta_2 = 0$. Subtract row $\omega_3$ from row $\omega_1$: $1.5\Delta_1 = 1$, so $\Delta_1 = \frac{2}{3}$. Put both into row $\omega_3$: $\frac{1}{3} + 1.25b = 0$, so $b = -\frac{4}{15}$.

The hedge, rows time and outcome (4), columns securities plus sum (4):

| | bond | stock 1 | stock 2 | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $-\frac{4}{15} \times 1 = -\frac{4}{15}$ | $\frac{2}{3} \times 1 = \frac{2}{3}$ | $0 \times 1 = 0$ | $\frac{6}{15} = 0.4$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_1$ | $-\frac{4}{15} \times 1.25 = -\frac{1}{3}$ | $\frac{2}{3} \times 2 = \frac{4}{3}$ | $0 \times 1 = 0$ | $1$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_2$ | $-\frac{4}{15} \times 1.25 = -\frac{1}{3}$ | $\frac{2}{3} \times 0.5 = \frac{1}{3}$ | $0 \times 2 = 0$ | $0$ |
| $t = 1$, $\omega_3$ | $-\frac{4}{15} \times 1.25 = -\frac{1}{3}$ | $\frac{2}{3} \times 0.5 = \frac{1}{3}$ | $0 \times 1 = 0$ | $0$ |

The $t = 1$ sums are $(1, 0, 0) = X$, so (4) holds, and $V_0 = 0.4$.

Check, by (2) directly: $X^* = E^*\left(\frac{X}{1.25}\right) = 0.5 \times \frac{1}{1.25} + 0.25 \times 0 + 0.25 \times 0 = 0.4$. Same.

## Why $X^*$ doesn't depend on the EMM (p.111)

The value of $X^*$ does not depend on the choice of EMM $p^*$. This follows from (5), as the LHS in that relation is one and the same for any EMM. $V_0 = \Delta \cdot S_0 + b$ is built from today's prices and the hedge only. No $p^*$ appears in it. So every EMM must give the same right side.

Numbers. Our running market has only one EMM, so let's make one with many. Drop stock 2: one stock with $S_0 = 1$ and payoffs $(2, 0.5, 0.5)$, same bond. An EMM needs $p_1^* \times 1.6 + (p_2^* + p_3^*) \times 0.4 = 1$ and $p_1^* + p_2^* + p_3^* = 1$. Substitute $p_2^* + p_3^* = 1 - p_1^*$: $1.2 p_1^* + 0.4 = 1$, so $p_1^* = 0.5$, and $p_2^* + p_3^* = 0.5$ split any way with both $> 0$. Two of them: $p^* = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25)$ and $q^* = (0.5, 0.1, 0.4)$.

The call $X = (1, 0, 0)$ is still attainable here: the hedge above used $\Delta_2 = 0$. Under $p^*$: $0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.4$. Under $q^*$: $0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.4$. Same, because $X$ is $0$ wherever the two EMMs disagree.

## Not every claim is attainable (p.111)

Thm 3 provides one with the arbitrage-free price for any attainable claim. It turns out that, in the general case, not all claims are attainable in a financial market.

Numbers. Same one-stock market. Take $X = (0, 1, 0)$: pays $1$ only in $\omega_2$. The stock pays $0.5$ in both $\omega_2$ and $\omega_3$, and the bond pays $1.25$ in both. So any $(\Delta_1, b)$ pays the same in $\omega_2$ and $\omega_3$, and can't produce $1$ in one and $0$ in the other. Not attainable. And now (2) gives no single answer: under $p^*$, $0.25 \times 0.8 = 0.2$; under $q^*$, $0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.08$. Different. That's why Thm 3 only prices attainable claims.

## Complete market (p.111)

A market with the property that any claim is attainable in it is said to be complete.

Single-period NA binomial market is complete. Let's see why. Any claim is a pair $(X(u), X(d))$. To hedge it I need

$$\Delta S_1(u) + b(1+r) = X(u)$$

$$\Delta S_1(d) + b(1+r) = X(d)$$

Subtract the second from the first, and $b$ drops out:

$$\Delta = \frac{X(u) - X(d)}{S_1(u) - S_1(d)}$$

This works for any claim, since $S_1(u) \ne S_1(d)$. Then $b$ comes from either equation.

Numbers, from p.91: $S_0 = 1$, $S_1(u) = 1.75$, $S_1(d) = 0.5$, $r = 0.25$. Take $X = (1, 0)$. $\Delta = \frac{1 - 0}{1.75 - 0.5} = 0.8$. From the down equation: $0.8 \times 0.5 + 1.25b = 0$, so $b = -0.32$. $V_0 = 0.8 - 0.32 = 0.48$.

Check by (2) with $p^* = 0.6$: $X^* = \frac{0.6 \times 1 + 0.4 \times 0}{1.25} = 0.48$. Same.

So: two outcomes and one stock, always complete. Three outcomes and one stock, not complete. Thm 4, next, gives the exact rule.
