# p.86–87: General discrete model, self-financing strategies and no-arbitrage

## The securities (p.86)

Now we go from one stock to many. Let's write the prices at time $n$ as a vector:

$$S_n = (S_n^0, S_n^1, \dots, S_n^d)$$

This is the development of $d$ equity securities. Here $S_n^i$ is the price of stock number $i$ at time $n$.

Security number $0$ is special. It is the bond:

$$S_n^0 = (1+r)^n$$

Numbers. Take $d = 1$, $r = 0.05$, and one stock with $S_0^1 = 100$, $S_1^1 = 130$. Then

$$S_0 = (1, 100), \quad S_1 = (1.05, 130)$$

Let's lay all the prices out as a table. Each row is a time $n$. Each column is a security $i$, so there are $d + 1$ columns: the bond ($i = 0$) and the $d$ stocks.

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ | $\dots$ | $i = d$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n = 0$ | $S_0^0$ | $S_0^1$ | $\dots$ | $S_0^d$ |
| $n = 1$ | $S_1^0$ | $S_1^1$ | $\dots$ | $S_1^d$ |
| $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | | $\vdots$ |

Row $n$ of this table is the vector $S_n$. Column $i$ is the price history of security $i$.

This is the layout for every table in these notes: rows are time, columns are securities. When a cell needs two numbers (shares and price), both go inside the cell as shares $\times$ price $=$ value, and a sum column on the right adds across the row.

Our numbers, with $d = 1$ (so 2 columns), and one more time step where the stock goes to $150$ (so 3 rows):

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) |
|---|---|---|
| $n = 0$ | $1$ | $100$ |
| $n = 1$ | $1.05$ | $130$ |
| $n = 2$ | $1.1025$ | $150$ |

## Portfolio and trading strategy (p.86)

A portfolio describes the proportions of the different securities in it.

A trading strategy

$$\Phi = (\Phi_n) = (\Phi_n^0, \dots, \Phi_n^d)$$

describes the development of the portfolio over time. Here $\Phi_n^i$ is the number of shares of security number $i$ at time $n$.

Numbers. Take the duplicating portfolio from p.66: $0.4$ shares and a bond amount of $-30.48$. Since $S_0^0 = 1$, that's $-30.48$ units of bond. So

$$\Phi_1 = (-30.48, \ 0.4)$$

The strategy is a table of the same shape as the prices. Each row is a time $n$. Each column is a security $i$ ($d + 1$ columns). The entry is how many shares I hold.

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ | $\dots$ | $i = d$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$ | $\Phi_1^0$ | $\Phi_1^1$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_1^d$ |
| $n = 2$ | $\Phi_2^0$ | $\Phi_2^1$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_2^d$ |
| $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | | $\vdots$ |

Row $n$ is the vector $\Phi_n$. Our numbers (2 columns; $\Phi_2$ comes from the self-financing step below):

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) |
|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$ | $-30.48$ | $0.4$ |
| $n = 2$ | $-18.10$ | $0.3$ |

## Value of the portfolio (p.86)

The value of the portfolio at time $n$ is: for each security, shares times price, then add up. That's a dot product:

$$V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_n \cdot S_n$$

$$V_n(\Phi) = \sum_{i=0}^{d} \Phi_n^i S_n^i$$

In the tables: take row $n$ of the strategy table and row $n$ of the price table. Multiply them column by column. Add across.

Now a cell has to hold three things: the shares, the price, and their product. That's the extra dimension. So each cell reads shares $\times$ price $=$ value, and the sum column gives $V_n(\Phi)$. Rows are times, columns are the $d + 1$ securities, plus the sum column:

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ | $\dots$ | $i = d$ | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | $\Phi_n^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\Phi_n^1 \times S_n^1$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_n^d \times S_n^d$ | $V_n(\Phi)$ |

Numbers. Value $\Phi_1$ at the time-1 prices $S_1 = (1.05, 130)$. One row ($n = 1$), 2 security columns, plus the sum:

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$ | $-30.48 \times 1.05 = -32$ | $0.4 \times 130 = 52$ | $20$ |

$$\Phi_1 \cdot S_1 = (-30.48)(1.05) + (0.4)(130) = -32 + 52 = 20$$

That's the call payoff from p.67, as it should be.

## Self-financing (p.86)

$\Phi$ is called self-financing if

$$\Phi_n \cdot S_n = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$$

Let's read this. At time $n$ the prices are $S_n$. On the left, I value my old holding $\Phi_n$ at these prices. On the right, I value my new holding $\Phi_{n+1}$, the one I switch to now, at the same prices. They must be equal.

So changes to the portfolio result only from regroupings. No additional capital is required for the change. No withdrawal of value takes place.

Numbers. At time $1$, prices $S_1 = (1.05, 130)$, I hold $\Phi_1 = (-30.48, 0.4)$, worth $20$. Now I regroup. I sell $0.1$ share, which brings in $0.1 \times 130 = 13$. I put that $13$ into the bond, which buys $13 / 1.05 = 12.38$ units. So

$$\Phi_2 = (-30.48 + 12.38, \ 0.4 - 0.1) = (-18.10, \ 0.3)$$

Check self-financing. Time $n$ now needs two rows: one before I trade and one after. Both rows use the same prices $S_n$; only the holding changes. Columns are the securities, plus the sum:

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $\dots$ | $i = d$ | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n$, before trading | $\Phi_n^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_n^d \times S_n^d$ | $\Phi_n \cdot S_n$ |
| $n$, after trading | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times S_n^d$ | $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$ |

Self-financing says the two numbers in the sum column are equal. With our numbers (2 rows, 2 security columns):

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$, before trading | $-30.48 \times 1.05 = -32$ | $0.4 \times 130 = 52$ | $20$ |
| $n = 1$, after trading | $-18.10 \times 1.05 = -19$ | $0.3 \times 130 = 39$ | $20$ |

The second row, written out:

$$\Phi_2 \cdot S_1 = (-18.10)(1.05) + (0.3)(130) = -19 + 39 = 20$$

Same $20$. Nothing added, nothing taken out.

## Chronology: what happens when (p.86)

Let's fix the timeline, using days. Time $n$ is the end of day $n$. The subscript on $\Phi$ tells you which day you hold it for: $\Phi_n$ is held during day $n$. I choose it at the end of day $n - 1$.

What does "trading" mean here? It means changing how many units of each security I hold: buying some, selling some. That is, changing the vector of holdings from $\Phi_n$ to $\Phi_{n+1}$.

When does it happen? Only at the instants $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. The model sees prices only at these instants, one price per security, $S_n^i$. It says nothing about prices between $n$ and $n + 1$. So the "day" is just the gap between two snapshots: I can't trade in it, and I don't look at prices in it. Every trade at time $n$ happens at the snapshot prices $S_n$.

So at the end of day $n$, I do two things in order. First, the prices $S_n$ come out and I value what I held all day, $\Phi_n$. Then, at those same prices $S_n$, I trade out of $\Phi_n$ into $\Phi_{n+1}$, and I hold that through day $n + 1$.

Here is the general timeline. Same layout as every table: each row is a moment in time (5 rows), each column is a security, plus the sum. Each cell is holding $\times$ price. During a day nobody trades, so those rows only show what I hold:

| moment | $i = 0$ (bond) | $\dots$ | $i = d$ | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| during day $n$ | hold $\Phi_n^0$ | $\dots$ | hold $\Phi_n^d$ | no trading; next prices are $S_n$ |
| time $n$, before trading | $\Phi_n^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_n^d \times S_n^d$ | $\Phi_n \cdot S_n = V_n(\Phi)$ |
| time $n$, after trading | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times S_n^d$ | $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$ |
| during day $n + 1$ | hold $\Phi_{n+1}^0$ | $\dots$ | hold $\Phi_{n+1}^d$ | no trading; next prices are $S_{n+1}$ |
| time $n + 1$, before trading | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times S_{n+1}^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times S_{n+1}^d$ | $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_{n+1} = V_{n+1}(\Phi)$ |

Now read the definitions off this table:

- $V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_n \cdot S_n$ is row 2: the day-$n$ holding at the day-$n$ closing prices.
- Self-financing, $\Phi_n \cdot S_n = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$, says row 2 sum $=$ row 3 sum. The trade at the end of day $n$ only regroups. Same prices, same total.
- The prices change only between row 3 and row 5, while I sit still holding $\Phi_{n+1}$. That's why the gain over day $n + 1$ will be $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot (S_{n+1} - S_n)$, as we see on p.87.

Numbers, with our running example ($n = 1$; 5 rows, 2 security columns, plus the sum):

| moment | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| during day $1$ | hold $-30.48$ | hold $0.4$ | no trading; next prices are $(1.05, 130)$ |
| time $1$, before trading | $-30.48 \times 1.05 = -32$ | $0.4 \times 130 = 52$ | $20$ |
| time $1$, after trading | $-18.10 \times 1.05 = -19$ | $0.3 \times 130 = 39$ | $20$ |
| during day $2$ | hold $-18.10$ | hold $0.3$ | no trading; next prices are $(1.1025, 150)$ |
| time $2$, before trading | $-18.10 \times 1.1025 = -19.95$ | $0.3 \times 150 = 45$ | $25.05$ |

Rows 2 and 3 both say $20$: self-financing. From row 3 to row 5 the value goes $20 \to 25.05$, a gain of $5.05$, only because prices moved.

## Increase in value (p.87)

The increase in value from $n$ to $n+1$ is

$$\Delta V_n(\Phi) = V_{n+1}(\Phi) - V_n(\Phi)$$

Now substitute $V = \Phi \cdot S$ at each time:

$$\Delta V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_{n+1} - \Phi_n \cdot S_n$$

Now, if $\Phi$ is self-financing, I replace $\Phi_n \cdot S_n$ by $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$:

$$\Delta V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_{n+1} - \Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$$

Now take $\Phi_{n+1}$ out as a common factor, and write $\Delta S_n = S_{n+1} - S_n$:

$$\Delta V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot \Delta S_n$$

So for a self-financing strategy, the value changes only because prices move. It equals my holding times the price change. On the timeline: $V_{n+1} - V_n$ is row 5 minus row 2. Self-financing lets me swap row 2 for row 3, and between rows 3 and 5 the holding is fixed at $\Phi_{n+1}$.

Numbers. Continue the example. Say at time $2$ the stock is $150$, so $S_2 = (1.05^2, 150) = (1.1025, 150)$. Then

$$\Delta S_1 = S_2 - S_1 = (0.0525, \ 20)$$

Now apply the formula with $\Phi_2 = (-18.10, 0.3)$. Same layout: rows are times, columns are securities, plus the sum. Rows 3 and 5 of the timeline, then their difference (3 rows):

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $\dots$ | $i = d$ | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n$, after trading | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times S_n^d$ | $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$ |
| $n + 1$, before trading | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times S_{n+1}^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times S_{n+1}^d$ | $V_{n+1}(\Phi)$ |
| change over day $n + 1$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^0 \times \Delta S_n^0$ | $\dots$ | $\Phi_{n+1}^d \times \Delta S_n^d$ | $\Delta V_n(\Phi)$ |

The holding is the same in every row of a column, so the change row is holding $\times$ price change. With our numbers:

| | $i = 0$ (bond) | $i = 1$ (stock) | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$, after trading | $-18.10 \times 1.05 = -19$ | $0.3 \times 130 = 39$ | $20$ |
| $n = 2$, before trading | $-18.10 \times 1.1025 = -19.95$ | $0.3 \times 150 = 45$ | $25.05$ |
| change over day $2$ | $-18.10 \times 0.0525 = -0.95$ | $0.3 \times 20 = 6$ | $5.05$ |

$$\Delta V_1 = (-18.10)(0.0525) + (0.3)(20) = -0.95 + 6 = 5.05$$

Check it directly. $V_2 = (-18.10)(1.1025) + (0.3)(150) = -19.95 + 45 = 25.05$. And $V_1 = 20$. So $V_2 - V_1 = 5.05$. Same.

## No-arbitrage principle (p.87)

The no-arbitrage principle says there is no arbitrage strategy $\Phi$. That is, there is no strategy $\Phi$ such that

$$V_0(\Phi) = 0, \quad V_n(\Phi) \ge 0 \quad \text{and} \quad P(V_n(\Phi) > 0) > 0$$

Let's read the three parts. I start with nothing. I can never end up below zero. And there is some chance I end up above zero. That's free money with no risk.

Or, equivalently,

$$V_0(\Phi) \le 0, \quad V_n(\Phi) \ge 0 \quad \text{and} \quad P(V_n(\Phi) > 0) > 0$$

Why is this the same? If $V_0(\Phi) < 0$, I am paid $-V_0$ to start. I put that money in the bond. Now my starting value is $0$, and my final value is even bigger. So I'm back in the first form.

Numbers. Take Example 1 from p.60, where $r = 0$ and $C = 20$. Buy 1 share, sell 3 options: cost $40$. Borrow that $40$ in the bond, so $V_0 = 40 - 40 = 0$. At time $1$ the holding is worth $50$ and I repay $40$, so $V_1 = 10$ in both cases. Then $V_1 \ge 0$ and $P(V_1 > 0) = 1 > 0$. That's an arbitrage strategy. The principle says the price $C = 20$ can't happen.

## Extras from the slides (p.97)

The slide states the no-arbitrage principle a little more precisely. There is no **self-financing** strategy $\Phi$ that creates a riskless profit from nothing. So the strategies ruled out are self-financing ones: money can't be added or taken out along the way.

Why does this matter? Without self-financing, "profit" is meaningless. If I could add $5$ at time $1$, then $V_1$ would rise by $5$ with no risk, but that's my own money, not a profit. Numbers: in our example, $V_0 = 9.52$ grew to $V_1 = 20$ and then $V_2 = 25.05$, with every step a pure regrouping, so all of the growth came from prices.

## Doubts

**Q:** In what order do things happen? Is $\Phi_n$ the position I end day $n$ with, which I then trade?

**A:** Almost. $\Phi_n$ is the position I hold during day $n$; I chose it at the end of day $n - 1$. At the end of day $n$, the prices $S_n$ come out, and I value $\Phi_n$ at them: $V_n = \Phi_n \cdot S_n$. Then I trade, at the same $S_n$, into $\Phi_{n+1}$, which I hold through day $n + 1$. Self-financing means this trade doesn't change the total. Numbers: at the end of day $1$, $\Phi_1 = (-30.48, 0.4)$ is worth $20$ at $S_1 = (1.05, 130)$. I switch to $\Phi_2 = (-18.10, 0.3)$, which is also worth $20$ at $S_1$, and hold it through day $2$. See the timeline tables in "Chronology: what happens when".

**Q:** What is "trading" here, and when does it happen? Does the price stay the same all day?

**A:** Trading means changing the holding vector from $\Phi_n$ to $\Phi_{n+1}$: buying some units, selling others. It happens only at the instants $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, at the snapshot prices $S_n$. The model has no price between $n$ and $n + 1$. It isn't saying the price is flat all day; it just never looks there, and you can't trade there. Numbers: at time $1$, I sell $0.1$ stock at $S_1^1 = 130$, getting $13$, and buy $13 / 1.05 = 12.38$ bond units at $S_1^0 = 1.05$. Both trades use time-1 prices. The next time I can trade, or see a price, is time $2$, at $S_2 = (1.1025, 150)$.

**Q:** Is $\Delta V_n(\Phi)$ the increase in value over the next day, day $n + 1$?

**A:** Yes. $\Delta V_n = V_{n+1} - V_n$ runs from the snapshot at time $n$ to the snapshot at time $n + 1$, which is day $n + 1$. The subscript is the start time. The holding that earns it is $\Phi_{n+1}$, the one held during day $n + 1$, which is why $\Delta V_n = \Phi_{n+1} \cdot \Delta S_n$. Numbers: $\Delta V_1 = 25.05 - 20 = 5.05$ is the gain over day $2$, earned by $\Phi_2 = (-18.10, 0.3)$.

**Q:** Is $V_0(\Phi)$ the value of the portfolio at time $0$, just as $V_n(\Phi)$ is the value at the end of day $n$?

**A:** Yes. $V_n(\Phi) = \Phi_n \cdot S_n$ is the value at the time-$n$ snapshot, before trading; by self-financing it is the same after trading, $\Phi_{n+1} \cdot S_n$. Time $0$ is the start: $V_0(\Phi) = \Phi_0 \cdot S_0 = \Phi_1 \cdot S_0$ is what it costs to set up the first holding $\Phi_1$. That's why the no-arbitrage condition starts from $V_0(\Phi) = 0$: I put in nothing. Numbers: setting up $\Phi_1 = (-30.48, 0.4)$ at $S_0 = (1, 100)$ costs $V_0 = -30.48 \times 1 + 0.4 \times 100 = 9.52$, which is the call price from p.67.
