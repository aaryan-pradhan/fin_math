# p.130–133: From the binomial call price to Black–Scholes

## Notation check

Same symbols as p.125–129. For the small example we keep $S_0 = 4$, $u = 2$, $d = 0.5$, $r = 0.25$, $p^* = 0.5$, $K = 4$, now with $T = 3$. From p.125–129 we already know this call costs $2.56$.

## The European call in the binomial model (p.130)

<!-- anim: binomial_tree T=3 S0=4 u=2 d=0.5 K=4 say="With $K=4$, only the end nodes $32$ and $8$ are in the money." -->

The call pays $g(S_T) = (S_T - K)^+$. Put this $g$ into the general formula. At $t = 0$ the price of the European call is

$$C = \frac{1}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=0}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j} (u^j d^{T-j} S_0 - K)^+$$

The $(\cdot)^+$ is $0$ when the call ends out of the money. So only the paths with enough ups count, and for those the $^+$ can be dropped:

$$C = \frac{1}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=m}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j} (u^j d^{T-j} S_0 - K)$$

## How many ups are enough: $m$ (p.130)

$m$ is the smallest number of ups with $u^j d^{T-j} S_0 > K$. Take logs:

$$\ln S_0 + j \ln u + (T - j) \ln d > \ln K$$

Move everything without $j$ to the right:

$$j(\ln u - \ln d) > \ln K - \ln S_0 - T \ln d$$

So $j$ must be bigger than the right side divided by $\ln u - \ln d$, and the smallest whole such $j$ is

$$m := \left\lfloor \frac{\ln K - \ln S_0 - T \ln d}{\ln u - \ln d} \right\rfloor + 1$$

Numbers. $\frac{0 - 3 \ln 0.5}{\ln 2 - \ln 0.5} = \frac{2.08}{1.39} = 1.5$, so $m = 1 + 1 = 2$. Indeed $2$ ups give $8 > 4$, but $1$ up gives $2 < 4$.

## Splitting into two binomial probabilities (p.130)

Split $(u^j d^{T-j} S_0 - K)$ into two sums. In the first, move $u^j d^{T-j}$ next to the probabilities:

$$C = S_0 \sum_{j=m}^{T} \binom{T}{j} \left(\frac{u p^*}{1+r}\right)^j \left(\frac{d (1 - p^*)}{1+r}\right)^{T-j} - \frac{K}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=m}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j}$$

The notes write $\frac{d p^*}{1+r}$ in the second bracket; it should be $\frac{d(1-p^*)}{1+r}$, since the downs come with $1 - p^*$.

Now call $\hat{p} = \frac{u p^*}{1+r}$. Then $\frac{d(1-p^*)}{1+r} = 1 - \hat{p}$, because $u p^* + d(1 - p^*) = 1 + r$ (p.127). So both sums are binomial tail probabilities:

$$C = S_0 \, P(\hat{U} \ge m) - \frac{K}{(1+r)^T} P(U^* \ge m) \qquad (\star)$$

with $\hat{U} \sim B(T, \hat{p})$ and $U^* \sim B(T, p^*)$. (The notes write $B(n, \cdot)$; here the number of steps is $T$, and the slides write $\text{Bin}(T, \cdot)$.)

In words: the call is worth "stock times a probability" minus "discounted strike times a probability". The second probability is just $P^*(\text{call ends in the money})$.

Numbers. $\hat{p} = \frac{2 \times 0.5}{1.25} = 0.8$. $P(\hat{U} \ge 2) = 3(0.8)^2(0.2) + 0.8^3 = 0.896$ and $P(U^* \ge 2) = 0.5$. So $C = 4 \times 0.896 - \frac{4}{1.953} \times 0.5 = 3.584 - 1.024 = 2.56$. Same as before.

## Many small steps (p.130–131)

Now fix the maturity $T > 0$ and split $[0, T]$ into $n$ small periods of length

$$\delta_n = \frac{T}{n}, \quad n \to \infty$$

so trading happens at $t = 0, \delta_n, 2\delta_n, \dots, n\delta_n = T$. In each small period the binomial model moves:

$$S_{j\delta_n} = u_n S_{(j-1)\delta_n} \quad \text{or} \quad S_{j\delta_n} = d_n S_{(j-1)\delta_n}, \quad j = 1, \dots, n$$

with $u$, $d$, $r$ chosen to shrink with the step:

$$u_n = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}} \simeq 1 + \sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}, \quad d_n = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}} \simeq 1 - \sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}, \quad r_n = e^{r\delta_n} - 1 \simeq r\delta_n$$

In words: each tiny step moves the price up or down by about $\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}$, and the bond earns $r\delta_n$. Over the $n$ steps the bond grows by $(1 + r_n)^n = e^{r\delta_n n} = e^{rT}$.

As on p.128, the price is $S_0$ times $e$ to a random walk:

$$S_n = S_0 e^{\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} = S_0 e^{Z_n}$$

with IID jumps

$$Y_{n,k} = \begin{cases} \ln u_n = \sigma\sqrt{T/n} & \text{w.p. } p_n^* \\ \ln d_n = -\sigma\sqrt{T/n} & \text{w.p. } 1 - p_n^* \end{cases}$$

and

$$p_n^* = \frac{1 + r_n - d_n}{u_n - d_n} = \frac{e^{r\delta_n} - e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}}}{e^{\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}} - e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\delta_n}}}$$

As $n \to \infty$, the call price at $t = 0$ is still given by $(\star)$.

Numbers. $\sigma = 0.2$, $T = 1$, $n = 100$: $\delta_n = 0.01$, $u_n = e^{0.02} = 1.0202$, $d_n = 0.9802$. Each step is about $\pm 2\%$.

## The limit of $p_n^*$ (p.132)

Use $e^x \simeq 1 + x$ in $p_n^*$:

$$p_n^* \simeq \frac{1 + r\frac{T}{n} - 1 + \sigma\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}}}{1 + \sigma\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}} - 1 + \sigma\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}}} = \frac{r\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}} + \sigma}{2\sigma} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{r}{2\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}}$$

The notes write $\frac{r}{\sigma}$ at the end; dividing $r\sqrt{T/n}$ by $2\sigma$ gives $\frac{r}{2\sigma}$, which is also what the slides have. So $p_n^* \to \frac{1}{2}$: with tiny steps, up and down become almost equally likely. Also

$$\hat{p}_n = \frac{u_n p_n^*}{1 + r_n}$$

One small remark. If we keep one more term, $e^{-x} \simeq 1 - x + \frac{x^2}{2}$, the $r$ above becomes $r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$. That extra $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ is where the $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ in the Black–Scholes formula comes from.

Numbers. $r = 0.05$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $n = 100$: $p_n^* \simeq 0.5 + \frac{0.05}{0.4} \times 0.1 = 0.5125$.

## The CLT turns $(\star)$ into normals (p.132)

<!-- anim: crr_clt r=0.05 sigma=0.2 T=1 say="The log-price walk under $p_n^*$ for more and more steps becomes normal." -->

$U^*$ and $\hat{U}$ are binomials with $n$ huge, so by the CLT they are nearly normal. $P(U \ge m) = 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{m - \text{mean}}{\text{sd}}\right)$, and $1 - \Phi(x) = \Phi(-x)$. So

$$C = S_0 \left(1 - \Phi\left(\frac{m - n\hat{p}_n}{\sqrt{n\hat{p}_n(1 - \hat{p}_n)}}\right)\right) - \frac{K}{(1+r)^T}\left(1 - \Phi\left(\frac{m - np_n^*}{\sqrt{np_n^*(1 - p_n^*)}}\right)\right)$$

$$= S_0 \, \Phi\left(\frac{n\hat{p}_n - m}{\sqrt{n\hat{p}_n(1 - \hat{p}_n)}}\right) - \frac{K}{(1+r)^T} \Phi\left(\frac{np_n^* - m}{\sqrt{np_n^*(1 - p_n^*)}}\right)$$

The notes stop here and write the answer. Here is the idea in words. $U^* \ge m$ means the call ends in the money, $S_T > K$, that is $Z_n > \ln(K/S_0)$. Under $p_n^*$, the walk $Z_n$ has mean $\simeq (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T$ and variance $\simeq \sigma^2 T$, so it is nearly $N\left((r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T, \sigma^2 T\right)$. Standardize:

$$P(Z_n > \ln(K/S_0)) \to \Phi\left(\frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) = \Phi(h - \sigma\sqrt{T})$$

Under $\hat{p}_n$ the mean shifts to $(r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T$, which gives $\Phi(h)$. And $(1+r)^T$ here means $(1 + r_n)^n = e^{rT}$.

## The Black–Scholes formula (p.132–133)

<!-- anim: convergence S0=100 K=100 r=0.05 sigma=0.2 T=1 say="The binomial price with $n$ steps settles on the Black--Scholes price." -->

So in the limit

$$C = S_0 \Phi(h) - K e^{-rT} \Phi(h - \sigma\sqrt{T})$$

where

$$h := \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

This is the Black–Scholes formula. It gives the price of a European call with maturity $T$ and strike price $K$, in a continuous-time market where the stock is a geometric BM and the bond grows continuously:

$$S_t = S_0 e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t}, \quad B_t = e^{rt}, \quad t \in [0, T]$$

Numbers. $S_0 = K = 100$, $r = 0.05$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $T = 1$: $h = \frac{0 + 0.07}{0.2} = 0.35$ and $h - \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.15$. $C = 100\,\Phi(0.35) - 100e^{-0.05}\Phi(0.15) = 63.68 - 53.23 = 10.45$.
