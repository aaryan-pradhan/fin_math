# p.111–114: Thm 4, the completeness theorem

## Notation check

The notes now write a stock's number as a superscript: $S_1^i(\omega_k)$ is the price of stock $i$ at $t = 1$ in outcome $\omega_k$. That is the same as $S_{1,i}(\omega_k)$ from p.105, and it matches $S_n^i$ from p.59. Everything else is as on p.109–111.

Running example, same as before: bond, $n = 2$ stocks, $N = 3$ outcomes, $r = 0.25$, $S_0 = (1, 1)$. Rows are outcomes (3), columns are the securities (3), each cell is the $t = 1$ price:

| | bond | stock 1 | stock 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\omega_1$ | $1.25$ | $2$ | $1$ |
| $\omega_2$ | $1.25$ | $0.5$ | $2$ |
| $\omega_3$ | $1.25$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |

## The statement (p.111)

**Theorem 4 (the completeness theorem).** An arbitrage-free market is complete iff there exists a unique EMM.

From p.109–111: complete means every claim is attainable. So Thm 4 says: "I can hedge every claim" and "there is exactly one $p^*$" are the same thing.

## Observation 1: the EMM is the solution of a linear system (p.111–112)

Using Thm 2, there is a $p^*$ such that $E^*\left(\frac{S_1}{1+r}\right) = S_0$. Write that out one stock at a time. Multiply both sides by $1 + r$, and write $E^*$ as the sum over outcomes. Add the condition that the $p_k^*$ add up to $1$. I get $n + 1$ equations in the $N$ unknowns $p_1^*, \dots, p_N^*$. One row per equation ($n + 1$ rows); the columns are the outcomes ($N$), plus the right side:

| equation | $\omega_1$ | $\dots$ | $\omega_N$ | $=$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| stock 1 | $p_1^* S_1^1(\omega_1)$ | $\dots$ | $+ p_N^* S_1^1(\omega_N)$ | $(1+r) S_0^1$ |
| $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ |
| stock $n$ | $p_1^* S_1^n(\omega_1)$ | $\dots$ | $+ p_N^* S_1^n(\omega_N)$ | $(1+r) S_0^n$ |
| sum | $p_1^*$ | $\dots$ | $+ p_N^*$ | $1$ |

So if the EMM is unique, the above system has a unique solution. The notes take this to mean $n + 1 = N$: as many equations as unknowns. (Strictly, a unique solution needs at least $N$ equations that are independent. Any extra ones would just repeat the others, so after dropping them we have $n + 1 = N$.) Then the system matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} S_1^1(\omega_1) & S_1^1(\omega_2) & \cdots & S_1^1(\omega_N) \\ S_1^2(\omega_1) & S_1^2(\omega_2) & \cdots & S_1^2(\omega_N) \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ S_1^n(\omega_1) & S_1^n(\omega_2) & \cdots & S_1^n(\omega_N) \\ 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

is non-singular, $\det A \ne 0$, and hence $A^{-1}$ exists. Row $i$ of $A$ is stock $i$ across the outcomes. The last row is the $1$s from "sum $= 1$". Column $k$ is outcome $\omega_k$.

Numbers. Here $n + 1 = 3 = N$. Plug the table into the system. Rows are equations (3), columns are outcomes (3), plus the right side:

| equation | $\omega_1$ | $\omega_2$ | $\omega_3$ | $=$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| stock 1 | $2 p_1^*$ | $+ 0.5 p_2^*$ | $+ 0.5 p_3^*$ | $1.25$ |
| stock 2 | $1 p_1^*$ | $+ 2 p_2^*$ | $+ 1 p_3^*$ | $1.25$ |
| sum | $p_1^*$ | $+ p_2^*$ | $+ p_3^*$ | $1$ |

So

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Expand $\det A$ along the first row: $2(2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1) - 0.5(1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1) + 0.5(1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1) = 2 - 0 - 0.5 = 1.5 \ne 0$. So the solution is unique, and it is the $p^* = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25)$ from p.107.

Check, plug it into each row: $2(0.5) + 0.5(0.25) + 0.5(0.25) = 1.25$. $0.5 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 1.25$. $0.5 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 1$. Same.

## Observation 2: hedging is the same matrix, transposed (p.112)

We observe a claim $X = X(\omega)$ can be replicated when there exists a portfolio $(\Delta, b)$, $\Delta = (\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_n)$, with $V_1(\omega_k) = X(\omega_k)$ in every outcome. One row per outcome ($N$ rows); the columns are the securities ($n + 1$), plus the claim:

| outcome | stock 1 | $\dots$ | stock $n$ | bond | $= X$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\omega_1$ | $\Delta_1 S_1^1(\omega_1)$ | $\dots$ | $+ \Delta_n S_1^n(\omega_1)$ | $+ b(1+r)$ | $X(\omega_1)$ |
| $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ |
| $\omega_N$ | $\Delta_1 S_1^1(\omega_N)$ | $\dots$ | $+ \Delta_n S_1^n(\omega_N)$ | $+ b(1+r)$ | $X(\omega_N)$ |

Call this system $(✡_0)$. It is a system of $N$ linear equations for $n + 1$ unknowns $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_n, b(1+r)$. Let's see why its matrix is $A^T$. Row $k$ of this system has coefficients $S_1^1(\omega_k), \dots, S_1^n(\omega_k), 1$. That is column $k$ of $A$. Rows of one are columns of the other, so the system matrix is $A^T$.

Numbers. Our $A^T$ is

$$A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 2 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

and its first row $(2, 1, 1)$ is exactly the $\omega_1$ row of the hedge table on p.110: $2\Delta_1 + 1\Delta_2 + 1.25b$.

## Proof of $\Leftarrow$: unique EMM gives completeness (p.112–113)

Suppose there is a unique EMM. From Obs. 1, $n + 1 = N$ and $A$ is non-singular, and hence so is $A^T$, since

$$\det A = \det A^T$$

$\therefore$ $(✡_0)$ has a unique solution for any right side $X$. That means for any claim $X$ there is a unique replicating strategy in the market, which proves that the market is complete.

Numbers. Let's hedge the claim that was not attainable with one stock on p.111: $X = (0, 1, 0)$. Write $\beta = b(1+r)$. Rows are outcomes (3):

| outcome | stock 1 | stock 2 | bond | $= X$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\omega_1$ | $2\Delta_1$ | $+ 1\Delta_2$ | $+ \beta$ | $0$ |
| $\omega_2$ | $0.5\Delta_1$ | $+ 2\Delta_2$ | $+ \beta$ | $1$ |
| $\omega_3$ | $0.5\Delta_1$ | $+ 1\Delta_2$ | $+ \beta$ | $0$ |

Subtract row $\omega_3$ from row $\omega_2$: $\Delta_2 = 1$. Subtract row $\omega_3$ from row $\omega_1$: $1.5\Delta_1 = 0$, so $\Delta_1 = 0$. Put both into row $\omega_3$: $1 + \beta = 0$, so $\beta = -1$ and $b = -\frac{1}{1.25} = -0.8$. So the hedge is: one share of stock 2, and borrow $0.8$. Its cost is $V_0 = 0 + 1 \times 1 - 0.8 = 0.2$.

Check, by Thm 3: $X^* = E^*\left(\frac{X}{1.25}\right) = \frac{0.25 \times 1}{1.25} = 0.2$. Same.

## Proof of $\Rightarrow$: completeness gives a unique EMM (p.113)

By Thm 2, for an NA market there is an EMM. We want to show: if the market is complete, then the EMM is unique.

Assume there are two EMMs, $p^*$ and $\tilde{p}^*$. Due to completeness every claim $X$ is attainable. So by Thm 3, its price doesn't depend on the choice of EMM, and for any claim $X$ we have

$$E^*\left(\frac{X}{1+r}\right) = \tilde{E}^*\left(\frac{X}{1+r}\right)$$

Write both sides as sums over outcomes and multiply by $1 + r$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^N p_k^* X(\omega_k) = \sum_{k=1}^N \tilde{p}_k^* X(\omega_k) \qquad (6)$$

Now pick the claims cleverly. In this step $i$ counts outcomes, not stocks (the notes' choice). Take

$$X_i(\omega_k) := \delta_{ik} = \begin{cases} 1, & k = i \\ 0, & \text{o.w.} \end{cases} \qquad i = 1, \dots, N$$

So $X_i$ pays $1$ in outcome $\omega_i$ and $0$ everywhere else. Rows are the claims ($N$), columns are the outcomes ($N$); the table is the identity:

| | $\omega_1$ | $\omega_2$ | $\dots$ | $\omega_N$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $X_1$ | $1$ | $0$ | $\dots$ | $0$ |
| $X_2$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\dots$ | $0$ |
| $\vdots$ | | | $\ddots$ | |
| $X_N$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\dots$ | $1$ |

Now the chain. The sum of $p_k^* \delta_{ik}$ keeps only the $k = i$ term. Then substitute $\delta_{ik} = X_i(\omega_k)$, then apply (6), then go back:

$$p_i^* = \sum_{k=1}^N p_k^* \delta_{ik} = \sum_{k=1}^N p_k^* X_i(\omega_k) = \sum_{k=1}^N \tilde{p}_k^* X_i(\omega_k) = \sum_{k=1}^N \tilde{p}_k^* \delta_{ik} = \tilde{p}_i^*$$

(The notes end with $\tilde{p}_k^*$. It should be $\tilde{p}_i^*$, since only the $k = i$ term survives.)

This holds for every $i = 1, \dots, N$. $\therefore$ $p^* = \tilde{p}^*$, meaning that the EMM is unique.

Numbers. In our two-stock market, $X_2 = (0, 1, 0)$ is the claim we just hedged for $0.2$. Any EMM must price it at $0.2$, so $\frac{\tilde{p}_2^*}{1.25} = 0.2$, which forces $\tilde{p}_2^* = 0.25$. In the same way, $X_1 = (1, 0, 0)$ costs $0.4$ (p.110), so $\tilde{p}_1^* = 0.4 \times 1.25 = 0.5$. And $X_3$: the three claims together pay $1$ in every outcome, which is $0.8$ bonds and costs $0.8$, so $X_3$ costs $0.8 - 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.2$ and $\tilde{p}_3^* = 0.25$. That is $p^*$ again.

Check, the one-stock market of p.111: there $X_2$ is not attainable, so (6) fails for it. Indeed $p^*$ priced it at $0.2$ and $q^*$ at $0.08$. Completeness is exactly what this proof needs.

## Remark (p.114)

The market should be rich enough for us to be able to replicate arbitrary claims. In pictures: enough independent payoff arrows to reach every point of $\mathbb{R}^N$.
