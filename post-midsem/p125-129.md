# p.125–129: Multiperiod binomial market

## Notation check

The notes now use $t$ for time and write the strategy as $(\Delta_t, b_t)$: $\Delta_t$ shares and $b_t$ bonds. That is $\Phi_t = (b_t, \Delta_t)$ from p.59. To keep the numbers easy, this file uses one small example throughout: $S_0 = 4$, $u = 2$, $d = 0.5$, $r = 0.25$, a call with $K = 4$, and $T = 2$.

## The model (p.125)

<!-- anim: binomial_tree T=2 S0=4 u=2 d=0.5 say="A stock at $S_0=4$ doubles or halves each period: $u=2$, $d=0.5$. Two periods." -->

There is one risky asset with price $S_t$. Each period it goes up or down:

$$S_t = u S_{t-1} \quad \text{or} \quad S_t = d S_{t-1}$$

and the bond is

$$B_t = (1+r)^t$$

It's the one-period model of p.87, repeated $T$ times.

Numbers. $4 \to 8$ or $2$, then $\to 16$, $4$ or $1$. Up-then-down and down-then-up both give $4$, so the tree recombines.

## Trading strategy (p.125)

A trading strategy $(\Delta_t, b_t)$, $t = 1, 2, \dots, T$, is the shares and bonds held during $(t-1, t]$, with the property

$$\Delta_t = \Delta_t(S_0, \dots, S_{t-1}), \quad b_t = b_t(S_0, \dots, S_{t-1})$$

In words: I pick what to hold for period $t$ at time $t - 1$, using only the prices seen so far.

## Self-financing (p.125)

The strategy is called self-financing if

$$\Delta_t S_t + b_t (1+r)^t = \Delta_{t+1} S_t + b_{t+1} (1+r)^t, \quad t = 1, 2, \dots, T-1 \qquad (1)$$

Left: what the old portfolio is worth at time $t$. Right: what the new one costs at the same prices. Equal means I only reshuffle, with no money added or taken out. So

$$t = 0: \quad V_0 = \Delta_1 S_0 + b_1$$

$$t = 1: \quad V_1 = \Delta_1 S_1 + b_1 (1+r) = \Delta_2 S_1 + b_2 (1+r)$$

Numbers. At $t = 1$, $S_1 = 8$, the old portfolio $(0.8, -1.28)$ is worth $0.8 \times 8 - 1.28 \times 1.25 = 4.8$. The new one $(1, -2.56)$ costs $8 - 3.2 = 4.8$. Same.

## Pricing a claim by replication (p.125–126)

To find the fair price of a claim $X = g(S_T)$, build a self-financing portfolio with value

$$V_t = \Delta_t S_t + b_t (1+r)^t, \quad V_0 = \Delta_1 S_0 + b_1$$

that pays exactly the claim at the end, on every path $\omega$:

$$V_T(\omega) = \Delta_T(\omega) S_T(\omega) + b_T(\omega)(1+r)^T = X(\omega), \quad \omega \in \Omega$$

Same payoff on every path means same price today, else there is an arbitrage. So the fair price of $X$ is $V_0$. We assume

$$d < 1 + r < u$$

Numbers. The call pays $g(s) = (s - 4)^+$: $12$, $0$, $0$ at $S_2 = 16, 4, 1$. And $0.5 < 1.25 < 2$.

## The last period (p.126)

<!-- anim: fork top="s" up="us" down="ds" say="Stand at $S_{T-1}=s$: next the stock is $us$ or $ds$." -->

Stand at $t = T - 1$ with $S_{T-1} = s$. Next the stock is $us$ or $ds$, and the claim pays $g(us)$ or $g(ds)$. I need $(\Delta_T, b_T)$ to pay the claim in both cases:

$$\Delta_T \, us + b_T (1+r)^T = g(us)$$

$$\Delta_T \, ds + b_T (1+r)^T = g(ds)$$

Two equations, two unknowns. Subtracting them gives $\Delta_T$; eliminating $\Delta_T$ gives $b_T$:

$$\Delta_T(s) = \frac{g(us) - g(ds)}{us - ds} = \frac{V_T[us] - V_T[ds]}{us - ds}$$

$$b_T(s) = \frac{u\, g(ds) - d\, g(us)}{(1+r)^T (u - d)} = \frac{u V_T[ds] - d V_T[us]}{(1+r)^T (u - d)}$$

Here $V_T[x] = g(x)$ is the value at $T$ when $S_T = x$.

Numbers. At $s = 8$: $\Delta_2 = \frac{12 - 0}{16 - 4} = 1$ and $b_2 = \frac{0 - 0.5 \times 12}{1.5625 \times 1.5} = -2.56$.

## The value one step back is an $E^*$ (p.126)

<!-- anim: backward_values T=1 S0=8 u=2 d=0.5 r=0.25 K=4 say="From $s=8$ the call pays $12$ or $0$. Average with $p^*=0.5$ and discount: $4.8$." -->

The portfolio costs $V_{T-1}[s] = \Delta_T s + b_T (1+r)^{T-1}$. Plug in the two formulas and simplify:

$$V_{T-1}[s] = \frac{1}{1+r}\left[p^* g(us) + (1 - p^*) g(ds)\right] = E^*\left(\frac{g(S_T)}{1+r} \,\Big|\, S_{T-1} = s\right)$$

where

$$p^* = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d} \in (0, 1)$$

So: the value today = the $p^*$-average of the two next values, divided by $1 + r$. That is the one-period rule of p.91, used at one node.

Numbers. $p^* = \frac{1.25 - 0.5}{2 - 0.5} = 0.5$. At $s = 8$: $V_1[8] = \frac{0.5 \times 12 + 0.5 \times 0}{1.25} = 4.8$. At $s = 2$: $V_1[2] = 0$.

## One more step back (p.127)

<!-- anim: backward_values T=2 S0=4 u=2 d=0.5 r=0.25 K=4 say="The whole example: values filled backward from the payoffs." -->

Repeat the same step at $t = T - 2$. Now the "claim" to copy is $V_{T-1}$, the money I'll need at $T - 1$:

$$\Delta_{T-1}[s] = \frac{V_{T-1}[us] - V_{T-1}[ds]}{us - ds}, \quad b_{T-1}[s] = \frac{u V_{T-1}[ds] - d V_{T-1}[us]}{(1+r)^{T-1}(u - d)}$$

$$V_{T-2}[s] = E^*\left[\frac{V_{T-1}(S_{T-1})}{1+r} \,\Big|\, S_{T-2} = s\right] = \frac{1}{1+r}\left[p^* V_{T-1}[us] + (1 - p^*) V_{T-1}[ds]\right]$$

Substituting $V_{T-1}$ gives an average over two steps. The middle price $uds$ is reached by two paths, hence the $\binom{2}{1} = 2$:

$$V_{T-2}[s] = \frac{1}{(1+r)^2} \sum_{j=0}^{2} \binom{2}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{2-j} g(u^j d^{2-j} s)$$

Here $j$ is the number of ups.

Numbers. At $s = 4$: $\Delta_1 = \frac{4.8 - 0}{8 - 2} = 0.8$, $b_1 = \frac{0 - 0.5 \times 4.8}{1.25 \times 1.5} = -1.28$, and $V_0 = \frac{0.5 \times 4.8}{1.25} = 1.92$. The sum gives the same: $\frac{0.25 \times 12}{1.5625} = 1.92$.

## The general formula (p.127)

<!-- anim: pascal_paths T=3 S0=4 u=2 d=0.5 j=2 say="Count the paths into each node: Pascal's triangle. The $3$ paths with two ups all end at $8$." -->

Keep stepping back. For $t = 1, 2, \dots, T$:

$$\Delta_t[s] = \frac{V_t[us] - V_t[ds]}{us - ds}, \quad b_t[s] = \frac{u V_t[ds] - d V_t[us]}{(1+r)^t (u - d)}$$

and

$$V_t[s] = \frac{1}{(1+r)^{T-t}} \sum_{j=0}^{T-t} \binom{T-t}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-t-j} g(u^j d^{T-t-j} s)$$

In words: from time $t$ there are $T - t$ steps left. $j$ ups end at $u^j d^{T-t-j} s$, and that happens with binomial probability. So $V_t$ is the discounted $E^*$ of the payoff. The stock obeys the same rule:

$$S_t = E^*\left(\frac{S_{t+1}}{1+r} \,\Big|\, S_t = s_t\right)$$

since $p^* u + (1 - p^*) d = 1 + r$.

Numbers. $0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 1.25 = 1 + r$.

## The discounted stock is a martingale (p.128)

The next price depends only on today's, so

$$S_t = E^*\left(\frac{S_{t+1}}{1+r} \,\Big|\, S_0 = s_0, \dots, S_t = s_t\right)$$

Divide by $(1+r)^t$. Under $p^*$ the discounted price

$$S_t^* = S_t (1+r)^{-t}$$

is a MG:

$$E(S_{t+1}^* \mid S_0^*, \dots, S_t^*) = S_t^*, \quad t = 0, 1, \dots, T-1$$

As in one period, $P^*$ is the only probability on the paths

$$\Omega = \{\omega = (v_1, \dots, v_T) : v_t = u \text{ or } d\}$$

that makes $S_t^*$ a MG.

Numbers. $E^*(S_1^*) = \frac{0.5 \times 8 + 0.5 \times 2}{1.25} = 4 = S_0^*$.

## The price at $t = 0$, and the random walk (p.128–129)

<!-- anim: walk path=uduudu step=0.69 label="Z_n=\ln(S_n/S_0)" say="The log-price steps $\pm\ln 2=\pm0.69$: a random walk." -->

At $t = 0$:

$$V_0[s_0] = \frac{1}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=0}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j} g(u^j d^{T-j} s_0) = E^*\left(\frac{g(s_0 e^{Z_T})}{(1+r)^T}\right)$$

where

$$Z_n := Y_1 + \dots + Y_n, \quad Y_k = \begin{cases} \ln u & \text{w.p. } p^* \\ \ln d & \text{w.p. } 1 - p^* \end{cases}$$

is a random walk with IID jumps, and

$$S_n = S_0 e^{Z_n}, \quad \ln\left(\frac{S_n}{S_0}\right) = Z_n = \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i$$

In words: each step multiplies the price by $u$ or $d$, so each step adds $\ln u$ or $\ln d$ to the log-price. $j$ ups give $e^{Z_T} = u^j d^{T-j}$. So the log-price is a random walk. Next we let $T \to \infty$ to get Black–Scholes.

Numbers. $\ln 2 = 0.69$ and $\ln 0.5 = -0.69$, so here $Z_n$ steps $\pm 0.69$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Up, down: $Z_2 = 0$ and $S_2 = 4e^0 = 4$.

## Doubts

**Q:** What does $V_T(\omega) = \Delta_T(\omega) S_T(\omega) + b_T(\omega)(1+r)^T = X(\omega)$ say?

**A:** At the end, I own $\Delta_T$ shares worth $S_T$ each and $b_T$ bonds worth $(1+r)^T$ each, so my portfolio is worth $V_T$. The equation says this equals the claim's payoff $X$ on every path $\omega$. Then the portfolio and the claim are the same thing, so they cost the same today: the claim's price is $V_0$. The $\omega$ on $\Delta_T$ and $b_T$ is there because I choose them at $T - 1$, depending on where the path is by then.

Numbers. On the path up, up: $1 \times 16 - 2.56 \times 1.5625 = 16 - 4 = 12 = X$.

**Q:** In $\Delta_T\, us + b_T (1+r)^T = g(us)$, why $(1+r)^T$ and not $(1+r)$, if the bond is held for only one period?

**A:** Because $b_T$ is a number of bonds, not an amount of money. One bond is worth $B_t = (1+r)^t$ at time $t$. So at $T - 1$ one bond costs $(1+r)^{T-1}$, and at $T$ it is worth $(1+r)^T$. My $b_T$ bonds are worth $b_T (1+r)^T$ at the end.

Your intuition is right in money terms. The money in the bond at $T - 1$ is $b_T (1+r)^{T-1}$, and one period of interest multiplies it by $(1+r)$:

$$b_T (1+r)^{T-1} \times (1+r) = b_T (1+r)^T$$

So only one period of growth happens. The other $(1+r)^{T-1}$ is just the bond's price when I bought it.

Numbers. $b_2 = -2.56$ bonds. At $t = 1$ a bond costs $1.25$, so I have borrowed $2.56 \times 1.25 = 3.2$. One period later I owe $3.2 \times 1.25 = 4$, which is $2.56 \times 1.25^2 = 4$. Same.

**Q:** In $V_{T-1}[s] = E^*\left(\frac{g(S_T)}{1+r} \mid S_{T-1} = s\right)$, what is $g(S_T)$, and what does the line say in words?

**A:** $g$ is the payoff rule of the claim, and $S_T$ is the final stock price. So $g(S_T)$ is what the claim pays at the end. For a call, $g(x) = (x - K)^+$.

In words, the line says: today's value is the average of tomorrow's two payoffs, weighted by $p^*$ and $1 - p^*$, then discounted by one period. From $s$ the price goes to $us$ (weight $p^*$) or to $ds$ (weight $1 - p^*$). Average the two payoffs $g(us)$ and $g(ds)$ with these weights, then divide by $1 + r$ to bring the result back one period. That number is exactly what the copying portfolio $(\Delta_T, b_T)$ costs, so it is the fair value.

Numbers. $s = 8$. Up: $S_T = 16$, $g(16) = 12$. Down: $S_T = 4$, $g(4) = 0$. Average: $0.5 \times 12 + 0.5 \times 0 = 6$. Discount: $\frac{6}{1.25} = 4.8$. The portfolio $\Delta_2 = 1$, $b_2 = -2.56$ costs $8 - 2.56 \times 1.25 = 4.8$. Same.

**Q:** How does the general formula $V_0[s_0] = \frac{1}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=0}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j} g(u^j d^{T-j} s_0)$ come out of the tree?

**A:** Read the tree as a list of paths. Each piece of the formula answers one question about them.

1. Where does a path end? Each step multiplies the price by $u$ or $d$. A path with $j$ ups and $T - j$ downs ends at $u^j d^{T-j} s_0$, whatever the order. So it pays $g(u^j d^{T-j} s_0)$.
2. How likely is one such path? The steps are independent, so multiply: $(p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j}$.
3. How many such paths are there? Choose which $j$ of the $T$ steps are the ups: $\binom{T}{j}$ ways. On the tree, the number of paths into a node is the sum of the paths into its two parents. That is Pascal's triangle $1 \mid 1\ 1 \mid 1\ 2\ 1 \mid 1\ 3\ 3\ 1$.
4. Average: add over the end nodes, (number of paths) $\times$ (probability of each) $\times$ (payoff). That is $E^*[g(S_T)]$.
5. Discount: each step back divides by $1 + r$, and there are $T$ steps, so divide by $(1+r)^T$.

Backward induction gives the same thing. One step back averages two nodes. Two steps back averages averages, and the middle node is reached from both sides, so its weight is $p^*(1-p^*) + (1-p^*)p^* = 2p^*(1-p^*)$. Each step back is one row of Pascal's rule. From time $t$ there are $T - t$ steps left, so the formula for $V_t[s]$ is the same with $T - t$ in place of $T$.

Numbers. $T = 3$, $S_0 = 4$, $u = 2$, $d = 0.5$, $p^* = 0.5$, call with $K = 4$. Each path has probability $0.5^3 = 0.125$. Rows are the end nodes (4), columns are $j$, end price, number of paths, payoff, and paths $\times$ $0.125$ $\times$ payoff (5):

| $j$ | end price | paths $\binom{3}{j}$ | payoff | contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $3$ | $32$ | $1$ | $28$ | $1 \times 0.125 \times 28 = 3.5$ |
| $2$ | $8$ | $3$ | $4$ | $3 \times 0.125 \times 4 = 1.5$ |
| $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $0$ | $0.5$ | $1$ | $0$ | $0$ |

$E^*[g(S_3)] = 5$, and $V_0 = \frac{5}{1.25^3} = 2.56$. Step-by-step backward induction on the tree gives $2.56$ too.

**Q:** How does $\frac{1}{(1+r)^T} \sum_{j=0}^{T} \binom{T}{j} (p^*)^j (1 - p^*)^{T-j} g(u^j d^{T-j} s_0)$ become $E^*\left(\frac{g(s_0 e^{Z_T})}{(1+r)^T}\right)$?

**A:** Nothing new is computed. The right side is the left side, with the final price given a new name. Three small moves.

Move 1: write the final price with $e$. Since $u = e^{\ln u}$ and $d = e^{\ln d}$,

$$u^j d^{T-j} s_0 = s_0 \, e^{j \ln u + (T - j) \ln d}$$

Move 2: give the exponent a name. Let $Y_k = \ln u$ if step $k$ is up and $Y_k = \ln d$ if it is down. Add them up: $Z_T = Y_1 + \dots + Y_T$. With $j$ ups and $T - j$ downs, that sum is $j \ln u + (T - j) \ln d$, exactly the exponent above. So the final price is

$$S_T = s_0 e^{Z_T}$$

Move 3: read the sum as an expectation. The chance of exactly $j$ ups is $\binom{T}{j}(p^*)^j(1-p^*)^{T-j}$; that's the binomial distribution. An expectation is (value) $\times$ (probability), added over all cases. So the sum is

$$\sum_{j=0}^{T} P^*(j \text{ ups}) \, g(s_0 e^{Z_T}) = E^*\left[g(s_0 e^{Z_T})\right]$$

The $\frac{1}{(1+r)^T}$ is a constant, so it can go inside the $E^*$. That is the right side.

Why bother? A product of $u$'s and $d$'s is hard to work with, but a sum is easy. Taking logs turns the product into a sum: $\ln\left(\frac{S_T}{s_0}\right) = Z_T = Y_1 + \dots + Y_T$, a random walk. A sum of many small steps becomes a normal by the CLT, and that is how we will get Black–Scholes.

Numbers. $T = 2$, $s_0 = 4$, $\ln 2 = 0.69$:

- $j = 2$: $Z_2 = 0.69 + 0.69 = 1.39$, $4 e^{1.39} = 16$.
- $j = 1$: $Z_2 = 0.69 - 0.69 = 0$, $4 e^0 = 4$.
- $j = 0$: $Z_2 = -1.39$, $4 e^{-1.39} = 1$.

These are the same three end prices as in the tree.
