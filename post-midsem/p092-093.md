# p.92–93: Put–call parity

## The setup (p.92)

<!-- anim: payoff K=1 kind=call,put say="A call and a put with the same strike $K$." -->

Take a call with price $C$ and a put with price $P$, both with the same strike $K$, in a market with no arbitrage (NA). Same single-period binomial market as p.88–92.

Build this portfolio:

- one share of the stock,
- one put on one share of the stock,
- a short position in one call on one share of the stock (p.93).

Short means I sold the call: I receive $C$ today, and at $t = 1$ I must pay its payoff.

Recall from p.92: $x^- = \max(-x, 0)$, so the put pays $(S_1 - K)^-$ and the call pays $(S_1 - K)^+$.

## Value at t = 0 (p.93)

I pay $S_0$ for the share and $P$ for the put, and I receive $C$ for the call I sold:

$$V_0 = S_0 + P - C$$

## Value at t = 1 (p.93)

The share is worth $S_1$. The put pays me $(S_1 - K)^-$. The call I sold costs me $(S_1 - K)^+$:

$$V_1 = S_1 + (S_1 - K)^- - (S_1 - K)^+$$

Now take out a minus sign from the last two terms:

$$V_1 = S_1 - \left((S_1 - K)^+ - (S_1 - K)^-\right)$$

Now use $x^+ - x^- = x$ for any number $x$. Why? If $x \ge 0$, then $x^+ = x$ and $x^- = 0$, so the difference is $x$. If $x < 0$, then $x^+ = 0$ and $x^- = -x$, so the difference is $0 - (-x) = x$. With $x = S_1 - K$:

$$V_1 = S_1 - (S_1 - K) = K$$

So the portfolio pays exactly $K$ at $t = 1$, whatever the stock does.

Numbers. Use p.91: $S_0 = 1$, $K = 1$, $u = 1.75$, $d = 0.5$, $r = 0.25$, $C = 0.36$, $P = 0.16$. Check $x^+ - x^- = x$: for $x = 0.75$, $0.75 - 0 = 0.75$. For $x = -0.5$, $0 - 0.5 = -0.5$. Good.

As a table. Each row is a time, and at $t = 1$ an outcome (3 rows). Each column is a security: stock, put, call, plus the sum (4 columns). Each cell is holding $\times$ price; the call holding is $-1$ because I sold it.

| | stock | put | call | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $1 \times S_0$ | $1 \times P$ | $-1 \times C$ | $V_0$ |
| $t = 1$ | $1 \times S_1$ | $1 \times (S_1 - K)^-$ | $-1 \times (S_1 - K)^+$ | $K$ |

With the numbers:

| | stock | put | call | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $1 \times 0.16 = 0.16$ | $-1 \times 0.36 = -0.36$ | $0.8$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $1 \times 1.75 = 1.75$ | $1 \times 0 = 0$ | $-1 \times 0.75 = -0.75$ | $1$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $-1 \times 0 = 0$ | $1$ |

Both $t = 1$ sums are $1 = K$. When the stock goes up, the call eats the gain. When it goes down, the put fills the loss.

## Using no-arbitrage (p.93)

Due to NA, today's value is the expected discounted value at $t = 1$, as on p.90:

$$E\left(\frac{V_1}{1+r}\right) = V_0$$

Now substitute $V_1 = K$. It's the same number in every outcome, so its expectation is just itself:

$$\frac{K}{1+r} = V_0$$

This makes sense: the portfolio is a sure $K$ at $t = 1$, just like $\frac{K}{1+r}$ bonds, which cost $\frac{K}{1+r}$ today.

Now substitute $V_0 = S_0 + P - C$:

$$S_0 + P - C = \frac{K}{1+r}$$

This is put–call parity.

Numbers. $\frac{K}{1+r} = \frac{1}{1.25} = 0.8$. And the $t = 0$ sum in the table is $1 + 0.16 - 0.36 = 0.8$. Same.

## Getting the put from the call (p.93)

Now solve for $P$. Add $C$ and subtract $S_0$ on both sides:

$$P = \frac{K}{1+r} - S_0 + C$$

Now substitute the numbers:

$$P = \frac{1}{1.25} - 1 + 0.36 = 0.8 - 1 + 0.36 = 0.16$$

This is the same $0.16$ we got on p.92 by pricing the put directly with $p^*$. So once I know the call price, parity gives me the put price with no extra work.

## Doubts

**Q:** In put–call parity, where is no-arbitrage used? Is the stock-and-bond hedge $(\Delta, b)$ of p.89 being applied to the claim "stock $+$ put $-$ call", and does that hedge work in every outcome?

**A:** Yes, that's exactly the idea. Treat the portfolio "1 stock, 1 put, short 1 call" as a claim $X$. Its payoffs are $X_u = X_d = K$, because $x^+ - x^- = x$ holds for every value of $S_1$. So it pays $K$ in every outcome, not just in some.

Now apply the perfect-hedge formulas (3) and (4) from p.89:

$$\Delta = \frac{X_u - X_d}{(u-d)S_0} = \frac{K - K}{(u-d)S_0} = 0$$

$$b = \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{(1+r)(u-d)} = \frac{K(u-d)}{(1+r)(u-d)} = \frac{K}{1+r}$$

So the hedge is no stock and $\frac{K}{1+r}$ bonds. Its cost is $V_0 = \frac{K}{1+r}$. As a table (rows: outcomes, 2; columns: bond, stock, claim, 3):

| outcome | bond | stock | claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| up | $\frac{K}{1+r} \times (1+r) = K$ | $0 \times uS_0 = 0$ | $K$ |
| down | $\frac{K}{1+r} \times (1+r) = K$ | $0 \times dS_0 = 0$ | $K$ |

When are $\Delta$ and $b$ defined? Formulas (3) and (4) only divide by $(u - d)S_0$ and $1 + r$, so they work for any claim as long as $u \ne d$. With $\Delta = 0$ here, $u$, $d$ and $p^*$ all drop out, and parity needs only $K$ and $r$.

No-arbitrage is used in one step. The portfolio and its hedge pay the same $K$ in every outcome, so they must cost the same today: $S_0 + P - C = \frac{K}{1+r}$. If not, buy the cheaper one and sell the dearer one.

Numbers. $K = 1$, $r = 0.25$: $\Delta = 0$ and $b = \frac{1.75 \times 1 - 0.5 \times 1}{1.25 \times 1.25} = \frac{1.25}{1.5625} = 0.8 = \frac{1}{1.25}$. Now suppose the call traded at $0.40$ instead of $0.36$. Then the portfolio costs $1 + 0.16 - 0.40 = 0.76 < 0.8$. Buy it, and borrow $0.76$ in the bond, so $V_0 = 0$. Rows are time and outcome (3); columns are bond, stock, put, call, plus the sum (5):

| | bond | stock | put | call | sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $-0.76 \times 1 = -0.76$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $1 \times 0.16 = 0.16$ | $-1 \times 0.40 = -0.40$ | $0$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $-0.76 \times 1.25 = -0.95$ | $1 \times 1.75 = 1.75$ | $1 \times 0 = 0$ | $-1 \times 0.75 = -0.75$ | $0.05$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $-0.76 \times 1.25 = -0.95$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $-1 \times 0 = 0$ | $0.05$ |

$V_0 = 0$, $V_1 = 0.05 > 0$ in both outcomes: an arbitrage, exactly as defined on p.87. No-arbitrage forbids this, so $C$ must be $0.36$.
