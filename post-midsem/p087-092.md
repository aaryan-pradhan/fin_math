# p.87–92: The binomial market and single-period pricing

## Notation check

Same symbols as before, with two small changes in the notes:

- Time is now written $t$ instead of $n$. It means the same snapshot times $t = 0, 1, \dots, T$.
- The strategy is now $(\Delta, b)$: $\Delta$ is the number of shares and $b$ is the number of bonds. On p.66 we wrote $B$ for the money in the bond. Since $B_0 = 1$, the number of bonds and the money in bonds at $t = 0$ are the same number.

Tables keep the same layout: rows are time or outcome, columns are securities (bond first, then stock), plus a sum column. Each cell is holding $\times$ price.

## The binomial market (p.87)

Times are $t = 0, 1, \dots, T$. There are two securities.

The bond (or bank account) yields a riskless rate $r$ of interest in each time period:

$$B_t = (1+r)^t, \quad t = 0, 1, \dots, T$$

The risky asset (stock) has price $S_t$ at time $t$. In each period it either goes up by a factor $u$:

$$S_t = u S_{t-1}$$

## Up or down (p.88)

<!-- anim: fork top="S_0" up="uS_0" down="dS_0" say="In one period the stock goes up to $uS_0$ or down to $dS_0$." -->

or it goes down by a factor $d$:

$$S_t = d S_{t-1}, \quad t = 1, 2, \dots, T$$

We assume

$$d < 1 + r < u$$

So the stock's down move is worse than the bond, and its up move is better. We'll see why this matters on p.90–91.

Now write $v_j$ for the factor in period $j$, so $v_j = d$ or $u$. Each period multiplies the price by its factor, so after $t$ periods

$$S_t = S_t(\omega) = v_1 \cdots v_t \, S_0$$

Here $\omega$ is the whole up/down path.

The price process is adapted to the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$. In words: to know $S_t$, I only need the moves up to time $t$, that is, $v_1, \dots, v_t$. Nothing from the future.

The tree in the notes:

$$S_0 \to uS_0 \text{ or } dS_0 \to u^2 S_0, \ udS_0, \ d^2 S_0 \to \dots$$

Notice that up-then-down and down-then-up land on the same price $udS_0$.

Numbers. Take the values from the example on p.91: $S_0 = 1$, $r = 0.25$, $u = 1.75$, $d = 0.5$. Check the assumption: $0.5 < 1.25 < 1.75$. Yes.

Let's tabulate time $t = 2$. Each row is an outcome, that is, a path of two moves (4 rows). Each column is a security, the bond and the stock (2 columns):

| path $\omega$ | bond $B_2 = (1+r)^2$ | stock $S_2 = v_1 v_2 S_0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $uu$ | $1.5625$ | $1.75 \times 1.75 \times 1 = 3.0625$ |
| $ud$ | $1.5625$ | $1.75 \times 0.5 \times 1 = 0.875$ |
| $du$ | $1.5625$ | $0.5 \times 1.75 \times 1 = 0.875$ |
| $dd$ | $1.5625$ | $0.5 \times 0.5 \times 1 = 0.25$ |

The bond column is the same in every row: it has no risk. Rows $ud$ and $du$ give the same stock price, $0.875$.

## Single-period binomial market: the strategy (p.88)

Now take just one period, $T = 1$. The outcomes are $\Omega = \{u, d\}$.

The trading strategy at $t = 0$ is

$$(\Delta, b)$$

where $\Delta$ is the number of shares and $b$ is the number of bonds.

The time-$t$ value of the portfolio is shares times stock price plus bonds times bond price. At $t = 0$, $B_0 = 1$. At $t = 1$, $B_1 = 1 + r$. So

$$V_t = V_t(\omega) = \begin{cases} \Delta S_0 + b B_0 = \Delta S_0 + b, & \text{at } t = 0 \\ \Delta S_1 + b B_1 = \Delta S_1 + b(1+r), & \text{at } t = 1 \end{cases}$$

As a table. Each row is a time, and at $t = 1$ also an outcome (3 rows). Each column is a security, plus the sum (3 columns):

| | bond | stock | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $b \times 1$ | $\Delta \times S_0$ | $V_0$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $b \times (1+r)$ | $\Delta \times uS_0$ | $V_1(u)$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $b \times (1+r)$ | $\Delta \times dS_0$ | $V_1(d)$ |

Numbers. Take $(\Delta, b) = (1, 0)$: one share, no bonds. With $S_0 = 1$, $u = 1.75$, $d = 0.5$, $r = 0.25$:

| | bond | stock | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $0 \times 1 = 0$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | $1$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $0 \times 1.25 = 0$ | $1 \times 1.75 = 1.75$ | $1.75$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $0 \times 1.25 = 0$ | $1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$ | $0.5$ |

## What a hedge is (p.88)

We have a claim $X$ paying at $T = 1$:

$$X(\omega) = X_u \text{ if } \omega = u, \qquad X(\omega) = X_d \text{ if } \omega = d$$

The portfolio will be a hedge given that

$$V_1(\omega) \ge X(\omega), \quad \omega \in \Omega = \{u, d\}$$

That is, in every outcome the portfolio pays at least what the claim pays.

Now substitute $V_1$ from the table, one row per outcome. The hedge condition is

$$\begin{cases} \Delta u S_0 + b(1+r) \ge X_u \\ \Delta d S_0 + b(1+r) \ge X_d \end{cases}$$

Numbers. Take the call from p.91: $K = 1$, so $X_u = (1.75 - 1)^+ = 0.75$ and $X_d = (0.5 - 1)^+ = 0$. Is $(\Delta, b) = (1, 0)$ a hedge? Up: $1.75 \ge 0.75$, yes. Down: $0.5 \ge 0$, yes. So it's a hedge, and it costs $V_0 = 1$.

## Solving for b (p.89)

Let's turn each condition into a bound on $b$. Take the first one. Subtract $\Delta u S_0$ from both sides:

$$b(1+r) \ge -uS_0 \Delta + X_u$$

Now divide by $1 + r$, which is positive, so the $\ge$ stays:

$$b \ge -\frac{uS_0}{1+r} \Delta + \frac{X_u}{1+r} \qquad (1)$$

Same moves on the second condition:

$$b \ge -\frac{dS_0}{1+r} \Delta + \frac{X_d}{1+r} \qquad (2)$$

Numbers. $\frac{uS_0}{1+r} = \frac{1.75}{1.25} = 1.4$ and $\frac{X_u}{1+r} = \frac{0.75}{1.25} = 0.6$. And $\frac{dS_0}{1+r} = \frac{0.5}{1.25} = 0.4$, $\frac{X_d}{1+r} = 0$. So

$$b \ge -1.4\Delta + 0.6 \qquad (1)$$

$$b \ge -0.4\Delta \qquad (2)$$

Check with $(\Delta, b) = (1, 0)$: $0 \ge -0.8$, and $0 \ge -0.4$. Both hold, as we found.

## The picture (p.89)

<!-- anim: plot fns="0.6-1.4*x; -0.4*x; 0.36-x" x=(0,1) y=(-0.8,0.8) labels="(1);(2);V_0=0.36" xlabel="\Delta" ylabel="b" say="Hedges lie above lines (1) and (2); the cheapest is the corner." -->

Now draw the $(\Delta, b)$ plane. Each condition says: be above a line.

- Line (1) has slope $-\frac{uS_0}{1+r}$. Since $u > 1 + r$, this slope is $< -S_0$. Steeper.
- Line (2) has slope $-\frac{dS_0}{1+r}$. Since $d < 1 + r$, this slope is $> -S_0$. Flatter.

All hedges are in the region above both lines. The two lines cross at one point. The perfect hedge is there.

The notes also draw a dashed line with slope $-S_0$. Along such a line, $\Delta S_0 + b$ stays the same, and $\Delta S_0 + b = V_0$ is what the hedge costs today. So I slide this dashed line down as far as it can go while still touching the hedge region. Its slope sits between the slopes of (1) and (2), so the last point it touches is the corner. The corner, the perfect hedge, is the cheapest hedge.

Numbers. Slopes: $-1.4 < -1 < -0.4$, with $-S_0 = -1$. The corner: set $-1.4\Delta + 0.6 = -0.4\Delta$, so $0.6 = \Delta$, and $b = -0.4 \times 0.6 = -0.24$. Its cost is $0.6 \times 1 - 0.24 = 0.36$. Compare the hedge $(1, 0)$, which cost $1$.

## The perfect hedge (p.89)

At the corner, both conditions hold with equality:

$$\Delta u S_0 + b(1+r) = X_u$$

$$\Delta d S_0 + b(1+r) = X_d$$

As a table. Each row is an outcome at $t = 1$ (2 rows). Each column is a security, plus what the claim pays (3 columns). Each row is one equation: bond cell $+$ stock cell $=$ claim.

| outcome | bond | stock | claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| up | $b \times (1+r)$ | $\Delta \times uS_0$ | $X_u$ |
| down | $b \times (1+r)$ | $\Delta \times dS_0$ | $X_d$ |

Now subtract the down row from the up row. The bond cells are the same, so they cancel:

$$\Delta (u - d) S_0 = X_u - X_d$$

Now divide by $(u - d)S_0$. Since $X = g(S_1)$, I can also write $X_u = g(uS_0)$ and $X_d = g(dS_0)$:

$$\Delta = \frac{X_u - X_d}{(u-d)S_0} = \frac{g(uS_0) - g(dS_0)}{uS_0 - dS_0} \qquad (3)$$

So $\Delta$ is the change in payoff over the change in stock price.

Now solve the up row for $b$. Subtract $\Delta u S_0$ and divide by $1 + r$:

$$b = \frac{X_u - \Delta u S_0}{1+r}$$

The notes jump to the final form, so let's fill it in. Substitute $\Delta$ from (3). Then $\Delta u S_0 = \frac{u(X_u - X_d)}{u - d}$, and

$$X_u - \frac{u(X_u - X_d)}{u-d} = \frac{(u-d)X_u - uX_u + uX_d}{u-d} = \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{u-d}$$

So

$$b = \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{(1+r)(u-d)} \qquad (4)$$

(3) and (4) form a perfect hedge for $X$.

Numbers. $X_u = 0.75$, $X_d = 0$. $\Delta = \frac{0.75 - 0}{1.25 \times 1} = 0.6$. $b = \frac{1.75 \times 0 - 0.5 \times 0.75}{1.25 \times 1.25} = \frac{-0.375}{1.5625} = -0.24$. Same corner as the picture.

## The time-0 value (p.89–90)

The time-$0$ value of the perfect hedge is

$$V_0 = \Delta S_0 + b$$

Now substitute (3) and (4). The $S_0$ cancels in the first term:

$$V_0 = \frac{X_u - X_d}{u - d} + \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{(1+r)(u-d)}$$

Now put both over the common denominator $(1+r)(u-d)$. The numerator is $(1+r)(X_u - X_d) + uX_d - dX_u$. Collect $X_u$: $(1 + r - d)X_u$. Collect $X_d$: $(u - (1+r))X_d$. So

$$V_0 = \frac{1}{1+r}\left[\frac{1+r-d}{u-d} X_u + \frac{u-(1+r)}{u-d} X_d\right]$$

Now give the two weights names:

$$p^* := \frac{1+r-d}{u-d}, \qquad 1 - p^* = \frac{u-(1+r)}{u-d}$$

They really add to $1$: $\frac{(1+r-d) + (u - 1 - r)}{u-d} = \frac{u-d}{u-d} = 1$.

So $V_0$ is a weighted average of the payoffs, discounted:

$$V_0 = \frac{1}{1+r} E^*(X) = E^*\left(\frac{X}{1+r}\right) = X^*$$

Here $E^*$ denotes expectation under the probability $P^*$ on $\Omega = \{u, d\}$ given by

$$P^*(\{u\}) = 1 - P^*(\{d\}) = p^*, \quad \text{where } p^* = \frac{1+r-d}{u-d} \in (0, 1)$$

owing to the condition $d < 1 + r < u$. That condition makes both $1 + r - d$ and $u - (1+r)$ positive, so both weights are between $0$ and $1$.

Numbers. $p^* = \frac{1.25 - 0.5}{1.75 - 0.5} = \frac{0.75}{1.25} = 0.6$, so $1 - p^* = 0.4$. Then $X^* = \frac{1}{1.25}(0.6 \times 0.75 + 0.4 \times 0) = \frac{0.45}{1.25} = 0.36$. Check: $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + b = 0.6 - 0.24 = 0.36$. Same.

## Why X* is the fair price (p.90)

We maintain that this calculated $X^*$ is the fair price of the claim $X$ at time $t = 0$. Indeed, if the claim was sold at a different price $X_0 \ne X^*$ at that time, that would have created an arbitrage opportunity.

Numbers. Say someone sells me the call for $X_0 = 0.30 < 0.36$. I buy it, and I do the opposite of the hedge: short $0.6$ shares and lend $0.24$. That brings in $0.36$. So today I pocket $0.36 - 0.30 = 0.06$. At $t = 1$ the call pays me $X$, and closing the reversed hedge costs me exactly $X$. Net $0$ in both outcomes. I keep $0.06$ for sure. If instead $X_0 > 0.36$, I do everything the other way round.

## The stock under P* (p.90–91)

Let's take the expected stock price under $p^*$:

$$E_{p^*}(S_1 \mid S_0) = p^* u S_0 + (1 - p^*) d S_0$$

Now substitute the formulas for $p^*$ and $1 - p^*$:

$$E_{p^*}(S_1 \mid S_0) = \frac{1+r-d}{u-d} uS_0 + \frac{u-(1+r)}{u-d} dS_0$$

Now expand the numerator: $u(1+r) - ud + ud - d(1+r) = (1+r)(u-d)$. The $(u - d)$ cancels:

$$E_{p^*}(S_1 \mid S_0) = (1+r) S_0$$

Now divide both sides by $1 + r$ (p.91):

$$E_{p^*}\left(\frac{S_1}{1+r} \,\Big|\, S_0\right) = S_0$$

So under $p^*$, the stock grows on average exactly like the bond. In discounted terms, it stays the same on average.

Numbers. $0.6 \times 1.75 + 0.4 \times 0.5 = 1.05 + 0.2 = 1.25 = (1 + 0.25) \times 1$. Then $\frac{1.25}{1.25} = 1 = S_0$. Yes.

## No-arbitrage as an interval (p.91)

(NA), no-arbitrage, means $d < 1 + r < u$. Multiply through by $S_0 > 0$ and divide by $1 + r$. Since $S_1(d) = dS_0$ and $S_1(u) = uS_0$, this gives

$$S_0 \in \left(\frac{S_1(d)}{1+r}, \frac{S_1(u)}{1+r}\right)$$

This means that the point $S_0$ can be represented as a convex combination of the interval's end points. That is, for $p^* \in (0, 1)$:

$$S_0 = p^* \frac{S_1(u)}{1+r} + (1 - p^*) \frac{S_1(d)}{1+r} = E^*\left(\frac{S_1}{1+r}\right)$$

Numbers. The interval is $\left(\frac{0.5}{1.25}, \frac{1.75}{1.25}\right) = (0.4, 1.4)$, and $S_0 = 1$ is inside. The combination: $0.6 \times 1.4 + 0.4 \times 0.4 = 0.84 + 0.16 = 1$. Same as $S_0$.

## Example: pricing a European call (p.91)

<!-- anim: backward_values T=1 S0=1 u=1.75 d=0.5 r=0.25 K=1 say="The call pays $0.75$ or $0$; with $p^*=0.6$ it is worth $0.36$ today." -->

Let $S_0 = 1$, $K = 1$, $r = 0.25$, $u = 1.75$, $d = 0.5$.

First $p^*$:

$$p^* = \frac{(1+r) - d}{u - d} = \frac{1.25 - 0.5}{1.75 - 0.5} = 0.6$$

Now the price:

$$X^* = \frac{1}{1+r}\left(p^* X_u + (1 - p^*) X_d\right)$$

Now substitute the call payoffs $X_u = (uS_0 - K)^+$ and $X_d = (dS_0 - K)^+$:

$$X^* = \frac{1}{1.25}\left[0.6 \times (1.75 - 1)^+ + 0.4 \times (0.5 - 1)^+\right]$$

The second payoff is $(-0.5)^+ = 0$:

$$X^* = \frac{1}{1.25}\left[0.6 \times 0.75\right] = 0.36$$

## The replicating portfolio (p.91–92)

Now apply (3):

$$\Delta = \frac{X_u - X_d}{(u-d)S_0} = \frac{0.75 - 0}{1.75 - 0.5} = 0.6$$

Now apply (4):

$$b = \frac{uX_d - dX_u}{(u-d)(1+r)} = -\frac{0.5 \times 0.75}{1.25 \times 1.25} = -0.24$$

So

$$(\Delta, b) = (0.6, -0.24)$$

At $t = 1$, check both outcomes:

$$V_1(u) = \Delta u S_0 + b(1+r) = 0.6 \times 1.75 + (-0.24) \times 1.25 = 0.75$$

$$V_1(d) = \Delta d S_0 + b(1+r) = 0.6 \times 0.5 + (-0.24) \times 1.25 = 0$$

So the price of the call is

$$V_0 = \Delta S_0 + b = 0.6 \times 1 + (-0.24) = 0.36 = X^* \ (= C)$$

All of it in one table. Each row is a time, and at $t = 1$ an outcome (3 rows). Each column is a security, plus the sum (3 columns):

| | bond | stock | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $-0.24 \times 1 = -0.24$ | $0.6 \times 1 = 0.6$ | $0.36$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $-0.24 \times 1.25 = -0.3$ | $0.6 \times 1.75 = 1.05$ | $0.75$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $-0.24 \times 1.25 = -0.3$ | $0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.3$ | $0$ |

The $t = 1$ sums are $0.75$ and $0$, exactly the call's payoffs $X_u$ and $X_d$. The $t = 0$ sum is the price.

## Pricing a European put (p.92)

<!-- anim: backward_values T=1 S0=1 u=1.75 d=0.5 r=0.25 K=1 kind=put say="The put pays $0$ or $0.5$; it is worth $0.16$ today." -->

The notes write $x^- = \max(-x, 0)$. So $(S - K)^- = \max(K - S, 0) = (K - S)^+$, which is the put payoff from p.65.

Same recipe, with the put payoffs:

$$P = \frac{1}{1+r}\left[p^*(uS_0 - K)^- + (1 - p^*)(dS_0 - K)^-\right]$$

Now substitute the numbers:

$$P = \frac{1}{1.25}\left[0.6 \times (1.75 - 1)^- + 0.4 \times (0.5 - 1)^-\right]$$

The first is $(0.75)^- = 0$. The second is $(-0.5)^- = 0.5$:

$$P = \frac{1}{1.25}(0.4 \times 0.5) = 0.16$$

Check with the hedge formulas. Put payoffs: $X_u = 0$, $X_d = 0.5$. Then $\Delta = \frac{0 - 0.5}{1.25} = -0.4$ and $b = \frac{1.75 \times 0.5 - 0}{1.25 \times 1.25} = 0.56$. So $V_0 = -0.4 + 0.56 = 0.16$. Same.

## Extras from the slides (p.101)

Two names, no new math:

- $P^*$, with $P^*(\{u\}) = p^*$ and $P^*(\{d\}) = 1 - p^*$, is called the **risk-neutral probability**. Same name as on p.82, for the same idea: under it, the stock grows on average exactly like the bond. Numbers: $0.6 \times 1.75 + 0.4 \times 0.5 = 1.25 = 1 + r$.
- $X^*$ is the **unique** fair price of $X$ at $t = 0$. Any other price $X_0 \ne X^*$ creates an arbitrage. That's what we checked above with $X_0 = 0.30$.

## Doubts

**Q:** Why is the intersection of lines (1) and (2) the best hedge? Is it because it costs the least?

**A:** Yes. Every point in the region is a hedge. What differs is the cost today, $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + b$. So "best" means cheapest, and the corner is the cheapest. Let's see why.

First, inside the region I can always lower $b$ until I hit the boundary. That keeps it a hedge and makes it cheaper. So the cheapest hedge is on the boundary.

Left of the corner, the boundary is line (1), $b = -\frac{uS_0}{1+r}\Delta + \frac{X_u}{1+r}$. Substitute into the cost:

$$V_0 = \Delta S_0\left(1 - \frac{u}{1+r}\right) + \frac{X_u}{1+r}$$

Since $u > 1 + r$, the bracket is negative. So the cost falls as $\Delta$ grows, and I move right, towards the corner.

Right of the corner, the boundary is line (2). Same substitution:

$$V_0 = \Delta S_0\left(1 - \frac{d}{1+r}\right) + \frac{X_d}{1+r}$$

Since $d < 1 + r$, the bracket is positive. So the cost rises as $\Delta$ grows, and I move left, towards the corner. Both sides push to the corner, so the minimum is there.

Numbers. On line (1) the cost is $V_0 = -0.4\Delta + 0.6$, and on line (2) it is $V_0 = 0.6\Delta$. At the corner $\Delta = 0.6$, both give $0.36$. Take one hedge on each side.

$(\Delta, b) = (0.5, -0.1)$, on line (1). Rows are time and outcome (3), columns are securities plus the sum (3):

| | bond | stock | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $-0.1 \times 1 = -0.1$ | $0.5 \times 1 = 0.5$ | $0.4$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $-0.1 \times 1.25 = -0.125$ | $0.5 \times 1.75 = 0.875$ | $0.75$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $-0.1 \times 1.25 = -0.125$ | $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ | $0.125$ |

$(\Delta, b) = (0.7, -0.28)$, on line (2). Same layout:

| | bond | stock | sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | $-0.28 \times 1 = -0.28$ | $0.7 \times 1 = 0.7$ | $0.42$ |
| $t = 1$, up | $-0.28 \times 1.25 = -0.35$ | $0.7 \times 1.75 = 1.225$ | $0.875$ |
| $t = 1$, down | $-0.28 \times 1.25 = -0.35$ | $0.7 \times 0.5 = 0.35$ | $0$ |

The claim pays $0.75$ (up) and $0$ (down). The first hedge pays $0.125$ extra when the stock goes down. The second pays $0.125$ extra when it goes up. That extra is what makes them cost more: $0.40$ and $0.42$, against $0.36$. Only the corner pays exactly $X$ in both outcomes, with nothing wasted. That's why it's called the perfect hedge.
