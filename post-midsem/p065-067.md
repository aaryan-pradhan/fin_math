# p.65–67: Pricing a call by a duplicating portfolio

## Stock and bond (p.65)

Now we have two things in our market. The stock price is $$S_t$$. The bond price is $$B_t$$, with interest rate $$r \ge 0$$. The bond grows like

$$B_t = (1+r)^t B_0$$

Numbers: $$B_0 = 100$$, $$r = 0.05$$. At $$t = 1$$, $$B_1 = 1.05 \cdot 100 = 105$$. At $$t = 2$$, $$B_2 = 1.05^2 \cdot 100 = 110.25$$. The bond has no risk. You always know what it pays.

## The one-step tree (p.65–66)

The stock starts at $$S_0 = 100$$. At time $$1$$:

$$S_1^a = 130 \text{ with } p = 0.4$$

$$S_1^b = 80 \text{ with } 1 - p = 0.6$$

The times are $$T = 0$$ and $$T = 1$$.

We sell a European call at $$T = 1$$ with strike $$K = 110$$. Its price today is $$C_0$$, the thing we want. Its payoff is

$$X = (S_1 - K)^+$$

There is also a risk-free investment at interest rate $$r = 0.05$$.

Let's work out $$X$$ in each case. If $$S_1 = 130$$: $$X = (130 - 110)^+ = 20$$. If $$S_1 = 80$$: $$X = (80 - 110)^+ = 0$$.

## The classical way (p.66)

The first idea: take the expected payoff, then discount it to today.

At $$t = 1$$, take the expectation under $$p$$:

$$E_p(X) = 20 \times 0.4 + 0 \times 0.6 = 8$$

Now discount by one period, dividing by $$1 + r$$. So the value of the call at $$t = 0$$ is

$$C_0 = \frac{1}{1+r} E_p(X)$$

Now substitute $$E_p(X) = 8$$ and $$r = 0.05$$:

$$C_0 = \frac{8}{1.05} = 7.62$$

But the Black–Scholes answer is

$$C_0^{BS} = 9.52$$

They don't match. Let's see which one is right, and why.

## Building a portfolio that copies the call (p.66)

Let's determine a portfolio which at $$T = 1$$ has the same payoff as the call.

A portfolio is a pair

$$\underset{\sim}{\Pi} = (\Delta, B)$$

where $$\Delta$$ is the number of stock shares, and $$B$$ is the amount in the bond.

The notes give the answer $$\Delta = 0.4$$, $$B = -30.48$$. (The notes write $$-30.98$$ in one place, but every later line uses $$-30.48$$, and $$40 - 30.48 = 9.52$$. So $$-30.48$$ is the right value.)

Where do these come from? The notes jump here, so let's fill it in. At $$t = 1$$ the portfolio is worth $$\Delta S_1 + B(1+r)$$. I want this to equal $$X$$ in both cases.

Let's put it in a table. Each row is an outcome at $$t = 1$$ (2 rows). The columns are what one share is worth, what one unit of bond is worth, and the call payoff I must copy:

| outcome | stock $$S_1$$ | bond $$1 + r$$ | call $$X$$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| up | $$130$$ | $$1.05$$ | $$20$$ |
| down | $$80$$ | $$1.05$$ | $$0$$ |

Each row gives one equation: $$\Delta \times$$ (stock column) $$+ \, B \times$$ (bond column) $$=$$ (call column).

If $$S_1 = 130$$, I want $$X = 20$$:

$$130\Delta + 1.05B = 20$$

If $$S_1 = 80$$, I want $$X = 0$$:

$$80\Delta + 1.05B = 0$$

Now subtract the second equation from the first. The $$B$$ terms cancel:

$$50\Delta = 20$$

Now divide by $$50$$:

$$\Delta = 0.4$$

Now substitute $$\Delta = 0.4$$ into the second equation:

$$32 + 1.05B = 0$$

Now subtract $$32$$ and divide by $$1.05$$:

$$B = -\frac{32}{1.05} = -30.48$$

A negative $$B$$ means I borrow $$30.48$$ at rate $$r$$, instead of lending it.

## Price of the portfolio today (p.66)

At $$t = 0$$ the stock costs $$100$$ and the bond holding costs $$B$$. So

$$V_0(\Pi) = \Delta \times 100 + B$$

Now substitute $$\Delta = 0.4$$, $$B = -30.48$$:

$$V_0(\Pi) = 40 - 30.48 = 9.52$$

In words: I buy $$0.4$$ shares for $$40$$, using $$30.48$$ of borrowed money and $$9.52$$ of my own.

## Checking the payoff at t = 1 (p.66–67)

At $$t = 1$$ the shares are worth $$\Delta S_1$$, and the bond has grown to $$B(1+r)$$. So

$$V_1(\Pi) = \Delta S_1 + B(1+r)$$

Now substitute $$\Delta = 0.4$$, $$B = -30.48$$, $$1 + r = 1.05$$, for each case:

$$V_1(\Pi) = \begin{cases} 0.4 \times 130 - 30.48 \times 1.05, & S_1 = 130 \\ 0.4 \times 80 - 30.48 \times 1.05, & S_1 = 80 \end{cases}$$

Now multiply out. $$0.4 \times 130 = 52$$, $$0.4 \times 80 = 32$$, and $$30.48 \times 1.05 \approx 32$$:

$$V_1(\Pi) = \begin{cases} 52 - 32 = 20, & \text{if } S_1 = 130 \\ 32 - 32 = 0, & \text{if } S_1 = 80 \end{cases}$$

And that is exactly the call's payoff:

$$V_1(\Pi) = X = (S_1 - K)^+$$

## The fair price (p.67)

The portfolio and the call pay the same thing in every case. So the price of the duplicating portfolio must equal the price of the call. Here

$$\underset{\sim}{\Pi} = (\Delta, B) = (0.4, -30.48)$$

So the fair price of the call at $$t = 0$$ is the price of the portfolio:

$$C_0^{BS} = 100 \times 0.4 - 30.48$$

$$C_0^{BS} = 40 - 30.48 = 9.52$$

If one were to price the call differently, then a risk-free profit (arbitrage) would be possible. This price is based on the no-arbitrage principle.

Let's see that arbitrage with the classical price. Suppose the market sells the call at $$7.62$$. I buy the call for $$7.62$$, and I do the opposite of $$\Pi$$: short $$0.4$$ shares and lend $$30.48$$. That brings in $$9.52$$. So today I pocket $$9.52 - 7.62 = 1.90$$.

At $$t = 1$$, the call pays me $$X$$, and closing my reversed portfolio costs me exactly $$X$$. Net: $$0$$, in both cases. So I keep the $$1.90$$ for sure. That's why $$7.62$$ is the wrong price. Notice that $$p = 0.4$$ never entered the portfolio calculation at all.

## Extras from the slides (p.85)

The slide also writes $$B = -30.98$$. That's a typo. Check it: $$0.4(100) - 30.98 = 9.02$$, not $$9.52$$. And $$0.4(130) - 30.98(1.05) = 52 - 32.53 = 19.47$$, not $$20$$. With $$B = -30.48$$ both lines come out right, so keep $$-30.48$$.
