# p.59–63: Pricing a stock option by arbitrage, and the arbitrage theorem

## Money now vs money later (p.59)

Let's start with one idea. An amount $$v$$ that you get at time $$t$$ in the future is not worth as much as $$v$$ given to you right now.

So we say: $$v$$ at time $$t$$ is worth, today,

$$v e^{-\alpha t}$$

Here $$\alpha$$ is the discount factor, and $$e^{-\alpha t}$$ is the discount function.

Let's put numbers in. Take $$v = 100$$, $$\alpha = 0.1$$, $$t = 1$$. Then today's worth is $$100 e^{-0.1} \approx 90.48$$. So Rs 100 a year from now is like Rs 90.48 in hand today.

## The one-period stock and the option (p.59)

Now the setup. A stock costs $$100$$ at time $$0$$. At time $$1$$ its price is either $$200$$ or $$50$$. Only these two.

There is also an option. It costs Rs $$C$$ at time $$0$$. It gives the right to buy one share at time $$1$$ for the strike price

$$K = 150 \text{ per share}$$

What is the option worth at time $$1$$? Let's see. If the price is $$200$$, I use the option. I buy at $$150$$ and the share is worth $$200$$, so I gain $$200 - 150 = 50$$. If the price is $$50$$, buying at $$150$$ is a loss, so I don't use it. It is worth $$0$$.

## Buying stock and options together (p.59)

Suppose at time $$0$$ I buy $$x$$ units of stock and $$y$$ units of option.

Now I value my holding at time $$1$$. Each share is worth the time-1 price. Each option is worth $$50$$ or $$0$$, as we just saw. So

$$\text{Value} = \begin{cases} 200x + 50y, & \text{if price is } 200 \\ 50x, & \text{if price is } 50 \end{cases}$$

Quick check with $$x = 1$$, $$y = 2$$. If the price is $$200$$, value is $$200 + 100 = 300$$. If the price is $$50$$, value is $$50$$.

## Make the holding the same in both cases (p.60)

Let's choose $$y$$ so that both values are equal. Then I don't care which way the price goes. Set the two cases equal:

$$200x + 50y = 50x$$

Now subtract $$200x$$ from both sides:

$$50y = -150x$$

Now divide by $$50$$:

$$y = -3x$$

So if $$y = -3x$$, the value of the holding at time $$1$$ is $$50x$$, no matter what.

A negative $$y$$ means I sell options instead of buying them. With $$x = 1$$, $$y = -3$$: I own one share and I have sold three options. If the price is $$200$$, value is $$200 - 3 \cdot 50 = 50$$. If the price is $$50$$, value is $$50$$. Same number. Good.

## Cost and gain (p.60)

What did this cost me at time $$0$$? I pay $$100$$ for each share and $$C$$ for each option. Here $$C$$ is the unit cost of an option. So with $$x$$ shares and $$y = -3x$$ options,

$$\text{Original cost} = 100x + C y$$

Now substitute $$y = -3x$$:

$$\text{Original cost} = 100x - 3xC$$

My gain is what I hold at time $$1$$ minus what I paid:

$$\text{gain} = 50x - (100x - 3xC)$$

Now open the bracket:

$$\text{gain} = 50x - 100x + 3xC$$

Now collect terms and take $$x$$ out:

$$\text{gain} = x(3C - 50)$$

So the sign of the gain depends on $$3C$$ against $$50$$:

$$\text{gain} = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } 3C = 50 \\ +\text{ve}, & \text{if } 3C > 50 \text{ (take } x > 0) \\ -\text{ve}, & \text{if } 3C < 50 \text{ (with } x > 0) \end{cases}$$

And when it is negative for $$x > 0$$, I just flip the sign of $$x$$, and it turns positive. The two examples show both cases.

## Example 1: C = 20 (p.60)

Take $$C = 20$$, unit cost per option. Here $$3C = 60 > 50$$, so take $$x > 0$$. Let $$x = 1$$, $$y = -3$$.

So I purchase 1 unit of stock and at the same time sell 3 units of option. Initially this costs me

$$100 - 3 \cdot 20 = 100 - 60 = 40$$

My holding at time $$1$$ is $$50x = 50$$.

Guaranteed profit:

$$50 - 40 = \text{Rs } 10$$

Check with the formula: $$x(3C - 50) = 1 \cdot (60 - 50) = 10$$. Same.

## Example 2: C = 15 (p.60–61)

Take $$C = 15$$. Here $$3C = 45 < 50$$, so I flip: $$x = -1$$, $$y = 3$$. I sell one share and buy three options.

Selling the share brings in $$100$$. Buying three options costs $$3 \cdot 15 = 45$$. So my initial gain is

$$100 - 3 \times 15 = 55$$

At time $$1$$, the value of holding is $$50x$$. Substitute $$x = -1$$:

$$50 \cdot (-1) = -50$$

That is, I must give back a share worth $$50$$ in total value, after the options pay off.

Guaranteed profit:

$$55 - 50 = \text{Rs } 5$$

Check with the formula: $$x(3C - 50) = (-1)(45 - 50) = 5$$. Same.

## What arbitrage means (p.61)

A sure-win betting scheme is called an arbitrage. That is, a risk-free profit.

In this example we just saw: $$C = 20$$ gives Rs 10 for sure, and $$C = 15$$ gives Rs 5 for sure. The only option cost $$C$$ that does not give an arbitrage is when the gain is zero:

$$3C = 50$$

Now divide by $$3$$:

$$C = \frac{50}{3} \approx 16.67$$

## Same answer through probabilities (p.61)

Now let's look at it another way. Say the price goes to $$200$$ with probability $$p$$, and to $$50$$ with probability $$1 - p$$.

Our wagers are the stock and the option. We ask for a $$p$$ under which each wager has expected return zero:

$$E(\text{return on wager}) = 0$$

First the stock. The return from purchasing 1 unit of stock is

$$\text{return} = \begin{cases} 200 - 100 = 100, & \text{price is } 200 \\ 50 - 100 = -50, & \text{price is } 50 \end{cases}$$

Set its expectation to zero:

$$E(\text{return stock}) = 0$$

Now write the expectation as value times probability, summed:

$$100p - 50(1 - p) = 0$$

Now add $$100$$ to both sides (written as $$100p + 100(1-p)$$ on the left), and you get the form in the notes:

$$100 = 200p + 50(1 - p)$$

This says: today's price equals the expected price tomorrow. Now open the bracket:

$$100 = 200p + 50 - 50p$$

Now subtract $$50$$ and collect $$p$$:

$$50 = 150p$$

Now divide by $$150$$ (p.62):

$$p = \frac{1}{3}$$

Check: $$200 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 50 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 66.67 + 33.33 = 100$$. Yes.

## Pricing the option with p = 1/3 (p.62)

The return from purchasing one share of option is

$$\text{return} = \begin{cases} 50 - C, & \text{if price is } 200 \\ -C, & \text{if price is } 50 \end{cases}$$

Set its expected return to zero:

$$E(\text{return option}) = 0$$

Now substitute $$p = \frac{1}{3}$$ and $$1 - p = \frac{2}{3}$$:

$$(50 - C) \times \frac{1}{3} - C \times \frac{2}{3} = 0$$

Now open the bracket:

$$\frac{50}{3} - \frac{C}{3} - \frac{2C}{3} = 0$$

Now collect the $$C$$ terms. $$\frac{C}{3} + \frac{2C}{3} = C$$:

$$\frac{50}{3} - C = 0$$

So $$C$$ is the option's payoff $$200 - 150$$ times its probability:

$$C = (200 - 150) \times \frac{1}{3}$$

$$C = \frac{50}{3}$$

This is the same $$C$$ as the no-arbitrage answer. Check numerically: $$(50 - 16.67) \cdot \frac{1}{3} - 16.67 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 11.11 - 11.11 = 0$$.

## Three outcomes (p.62)

Now let the stock go from $$100$$ to one of three prices at time $$1$$:

$$300 \text{ with prob } p_1, \quad 200 \text{ with prob } p_2, \quad 50 \text{ with prob } 1 - p_1 - p_2$$

with $$0 < p_1 < 1$$ and $$0 < p_2 < 1$$. The option still costs $$C$$ with strike $$K = 150$$.

The option pays $$300 - 150 = 150$$ at price $$300$$, pays $$200 - 150 = 50$$ at price $$200$$, and pays $$0$$ at price $$50$$.

Same two conditions as before. Stock: today's price equals expected price tomorrow:

$$100 = 300p_1 + 200p_2 + 50(1 - p_1 - p_2)$$

Option: expected return zero. That is $$(150 - C)p_1 + (50 - C)p_2 - C(1 - p_1 - p_2) = 0$$. The $$C$$ terms add up to $$C(p_1 + p_2 + 1 - p_1 - p_2) = C$$, so

$$C = 150p_1 + 50p_2$$

with $$0 < p_1 < 1$$, $$0 < p_2 < 1$$.

The notes stop here. Let's see what the first equation says. Open the bracket and subtract $$50$$:

$$50 = 250p_1 + 150p_2$$

Now divide by $$50$$:

$$1 = 5p_1 + 3p_2$$

That's one equation for two unknowns, so there are many $$(p_1, p_2)$$. Try two:

- $$p_1 = 0.1$$: then $$3p_2 = 0.5$$, $$p_2 = \frac{1}{6}$$. So $$C = 15 + 8.33 = 23.33$$.
- $$p_1 = 0.15$$: then $$3p_2 = 0.25$$, $$p_2 = \frac{1}{12}$$. So $$C = 22.5 + 4.17 = 26.67$$.

So with three outcomes, these two conditions don't pin $$C$$ down to one number. Different $$p$$'s give different $$C$$'s.

## The arbitrage theorem: setup (p.62–63)

Now the general picture. Take an experiment whose set of possible outcomes is

$$S = \{1, 2, \dots, m\}$$

There are $$n$$ wagers. If amount $$x$$ is bet on wager $$i$$, then the return $$x \, r_i(j)$$ is earned if the outcome of the experiment is $$j \in S$$. So $$r_i(\cdot)$$ is the return function for a unit bet on wager $$i$$.

Let's lay all the $$r_i(j)$$ out as a table. Each row is a wager $$i$$, and each column is an outcome $$j$$. The entry in row $$i$$, column $$j$$ is what a unit bet on wager $$i$$ returns when outcome $$j$$ happens.

| | outcome $$1$$ | outcome $$2$$ | $$\dots$$ | outcome $$m$$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| wager $$1$$ | $$r_1(1)$$ | $$r_1(2)$$ | $$\dots$$ | $$r_1(m)$$ |
| wager $$2$$ | $$r_2(1)$$ | $$r_2(2)$$ | $$\dots$$ | $$r_2(m)$$ |
| $$\vdots$$ | $$\vdots$$ | $$\vdots$$ | | $$\vdots$$ |
| wager $$n$$ | $$r_n(1)$$ | $$r_n(2)$$ | $$\dots$$ | $$r_n(m)$$ |

So $$i$$ counts rows ($$n$$ wagers), and $$j$$ counts columns ($$m$$ outcomes).

A betting scheme is a vector of bets, one per wager:

$$\underset{\sim}{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$$

If the outcome of the experiment is $$j$$, then I add up the return of every wager:

$$\text{return from } \underset{\sim}{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i r_i(j)$$

In the table: I go down column $$j$$, multiply each row's entry by that row's bet $$x_i$$, and add.

Let's map our two-outcome example onto this. Wagers (rows): $$i = 1$$ is the stock, $$i = 2$$ is the option, so $$n = 2$$. Outcomes (columns): $$j = 1$$ means price $$200$$, $$j = 2$$ means price $$50$$, so $$m = 2$$. With $$C = 20$$:

| | $$j = 1$$ (price $$200$$) | $$j = 2$$ (price $$50$$) |
|---|---|---|
| $$i = 1$$ stock | $$r_1(1) = 100$$ | $$r_1(2) = -50$$ |
| $$i = 2$$ option | $$r_2(1) = 50 - 20 = 30$$ | $$r_2(2) = -20$$ |

Take the scheme $$\underset{\sim}{x} = (1, -3)$$. If $$j = 1$$: $$1 \cdot 100 - 3 \cdot 30 = 10$$. If $$j = 2$$: $$1 \cdot (-50) - 3 \cdot (-20) = 10$$. That's our Rs 10 from Example 1.

The idea: either there is a $$\underset{\sim}{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_m)$$ on the set of possible outcomes under which each wager has expected return $$0$$, or else there is a betting scheme that guarantees a positive win.

## The arbitrage theorem: statement (p.63)

**Theorem (Arbitrage theorem).** Exactly one of the following is true. Either

(i) there exists a $$\underset{\sim}{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_m)$$ for which

$$\sum_{j=1}^{m} p_j \, r_i(j) = 0 \quad \forall \, i = 1, \dots, n$$

or

(ii) there exists a betting scheme $$\underset{\sim}{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$$ for which

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \, r_i(j) > 0 \quad \forall \, j = 1, \dots, m$$

Read (i) and (ii) off the table. In (i), I fix a row $$i$$, walk across it, and weight each entry by $$p_j$$; every row must sum to $$0$$. In (ii), I fix a column $$j$$, walk down it, and weight each entry by $$x_i$$; every column must come out positive.

Let's check both sides on our example.

With $$C = 20$$, case (ii) holds, since $$(1, -3)$$ wins $$10$$ for every $$j$$. So (i) must fail. Let's see it fail. The stock needs $$100p_1 - 50p_2 = 0$$, so $$p_1 = \frac{1}{3}$$, $$p_2 = \frac{2}{3}$$. Then the option gives $$30 \cdot \frac{1}{3} - 20 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 10 - 13.33 = -3.33 \neq 0$$. So no such $$\underset{\sim}{p}$$ exists.

With $$C = \frac{50}{3}$$, case (i) holds with $$\underset{\sim}{p} = (\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3})$$, as we checked on p.62. So by the theorem, no sure-win scheme exists. That matches the gain $$x(3C - 50) = 0$$ for every $$x$$.

## Extras from the slides (p.82–83)

The slides repeat this lecture. They add only names:

- The $$p = \frac{1}{3}$$ that gives every wager expected return $$0$$ is called the risk-neutral probability.
- In the theorem, $$\underset{\sim}{p}$$ is a probability vector, so $$p_j \ge 0$$ and $$\sum_j p_j = 1$$. Case (i) is called "No Arbitrage". Case (ii) is called "Arbitrage Opportunity".

So in our example: at $$C = \frac{50}{3}$$, the risk-neutral vector is $$(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3})$$. It sums to $$1$$, and we are in case (i). At $$C = 20$$ we are in case (ii).

## Doubts

**Q:** In $$r_i(j)$$, what do $$i$$ and $$j$$ index? Can it be a table?

**A:** Look at it as a table. Row $$i$$ is a wager (there are $$n$$), column $$j$$ is an outcome (there are $$m$$), and the entry is $$r_i(j)$$. With $$C = 20$$, the stock row is $$(100, -50)$$ and the option row is $$(30, -20)$$. Condition (i) works across a row: $$100 \cdot \frac{1}{3} - 50 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 0$$. Condition (ii) works down a column: with $$\underset{\sim}{x} = (1, -3)$$, column $$1$$ gives $$100 - 90 = 10 > 0$$. I've put both tables into the arbitrage theorem section.
