# p.64–65: Forwards, futures, calls, puts and derivative securities

## Forwards (p.64)

Let's define the first contract. Forwards are contracts that give the market participant the right to buy or sell an underlying financial asset at a time $T$ in the future, or in a future time interval $(T, T']$, at a fixed price $K$.

There are two sides to such a contract:

- Long position: entering into a contract to buy.
- Short position: entering into a sale contract.

Example. Today I agree to buy one share at $T = 1$ year for $K = 100$. I hold the long position. The person who agrees to sell it to me holds the short position. If the share is worth $120$ at $T$, I still pay only $100$. If it is worth $90$, I still pay $100$.

## Forwards vs futures (p.64)

Forwards and futures involve hedging for their settlement. That is, the market participant has both the right and the obligation to buy or sell.

Notice the word obligation. In my example above, when the share is worth $90$, I can't walk away. I must pay $100$, so I lose $10$.

The difference between the two:

- Futures are traded on financial markets.
- Forwards are based on an individual agreement between the participants, without market intervention.

## The plus notation (p.64)

Before options, we need one piece of notation:

$$x^+ = \begin{cases} x, & \text{if } x > 0 \\ 0, & \text{if } x \le 0 \end{cases}$$

That is,

$$x^+ = \max(x, 0)$$

Numbers: $(5)^+ = \max(5, 0) = 5$. And $(-3)^+ = \max(-3, 0) = 0$. So $x^+$ keeps positive numbers and turns everything else into $0$.

We will write $(S_t)$ for the price of the stock at time $t$.

## European call (p.64)

A call option gives the right to buy. In a European call, I can buy only at the fixed time $T$, at price $K$.

The value of a European call at time $T$ is

$$X = (S_T - K)^+$$

Let's see why. At time $T$ the share costs $S_T$ in the market. If $S_T > K$, I use my right: I buy at $K$, and the share is worth $S_T$, so I gain $S_T - K$. If $S_T \le K$, buying at $K$ makes no sense, so I don't use it. I gain $0$. That is exactly $\max(S_T - K, 0)$.

The picture in the notes: the stock path starts at $S_0$, below the line $K$. It wiggles up. At time $T$ I read off $S_T$ and compare it with $K$. Only the value at $T$ matters, not the path before it.

Numbers with $K = 100$:

- $S_T = 120$: $X = (120 - 100)^+ = 20$.
- $S_T = 90$: $X = (90 - 100)^+ = (-10)^+ = 0$.

So a call is like a forward without the obligation. When $S_T = 90$, the forward lost $10$, but the call just pays $0$.

## American call (p.65)

The American call gives the buyer the right to buy a security at any time $\tau$, where

$$\tau \in [0, T]$$

at a price $K$.

So now I choose when to buy. If I buy at time $\tau$, the value is

$$X = (S_\tau - K)^+$$

The picture: the time axis runs from $0$ to $T$, and $\tau$ can be anywhere in between.

Numbers with $K = 100$. Say the stock is at $130$ at $\tau = 0.5$ and falls to $95$ by $T = 1$. If I exercise at $\tau = 0.5$, I get $(130 - 100)^+ = 30$. A European call would only see $S_T = 95$, so it gives $(95 - 100)^+ = 0$.

## Put option (p.65)

A put option gives the right to sell. With a European put, I can sell at time $T$ at price $K$. Its value is

$$X = (K - S_T)^+$$

Same reasoning, flipped. If $S_T < K$, I buy the share in the market for $S_T$ and sell it through the put for $K$. I gain $K - S_T$. If $S_T \ge K$, selling at $K$ makes no sense, so I gain $0$.

Numbers with $K = 100$:

- $S_T = 90$: $X = (100 - 90)^+ = 10$.
- $S_T = 120$: $X = (100 - 120)^+ = (-20)^+ = 0$.

So the put pays when the price falls, and the call pays when it rises.

## Derivative security in general (p.65)

Now let's put all of these under one definition. Mathematically, a derivative security (or contingent claim) with maturity (expiry) date $T$ in our market is a function

$$X = X(\omega) = g(S_T(\omega)) \ge 0$$

of the underlying asset price $S_T$ at time $T$.

Here $\omega$ is the outcome of the randomness. Fix $\omega$, and you get one price $S_T(\omega)$. Feed it into $g$, and you get one payoff. Since $g$ never goes below zero, $X \ge 0$.

Our two options fit this:

- European call: $g(s) = (s - K)^+$.
- European put: $g(s) = (K - s)^+$.

The financial interpretation of $X$ is that the contract will pay its owner the amount $X$ at time $T$.

Numbers. Take $K = 100$ and suppose that in outcome $\omega$ the price is $S_T(\omega) = 120$. Then the call pays $g(120) = (120 - 100)^+ = 20$ at $T$, and the put pays $(100 - 120)^+ = 0$ at $T$.
