Brownian Motion Additional Problems

"Assignment additional: BM, SBM, Sto. Cal., White noise, etc.", Exercises 1–34 (Ross, Introduction to Probability Models, Ch. 10). Each problem is followed by its solution. Sources: BM-2.pdf (problems) and BM-2soln.pdf (solutions). Where the source has no solution (Q9, Q10, Q34, and parts of Q4, Q15 and Q31), the solution is added here and marked.

Throughout, $\{B(t),t\ge0\}$ (also written $X(t)$ in the solutions) is standard Brownian motion and $T_a$ is the time it first hits $a$. "Martingale stopping theorem" means $E[Y(T)]=E[Y(0)]$.

Problem 1 — Distribution of $B(s)+B(t)$

Problem. What is the distribution of $B(s)+B(t)$, $s\le t$?
Solution. $B(s)+B(t)=2B(s)+(B(t)-B(s))$. Here $2B(s)\sim N(0,4s)$ and $B(t)-B(s)\sim N(0,t-s)$, and they are independent. So $$B(s)+B(t)\sim\mathbf{N(0,\,3s+t)}.$$

Problem 2 — Bridge between two known values

Problem. Compute the conditional distribution of $B(s)$ given $B(t_1)=A$ and $B(t_2)=B$, where $0<t_1<s<t_2$.
Solution. The distribution of $B(s)-A$ given the two values equals that of $B(s-t_1)$ given $B(0)=0$ and $B(t_2-t_1)=B-A$. By (10.4) this is normal with mean $\frac{s-t_1}{t_2-t_1}(B-A)$ and variance $\frac{s-t_1}{t_2-t_1}(t_2-s)$. So $$B(s)\mid\ldots\ \sim\ N\!\left(A+\frac{(s-t_1)(B-A)}{t_2-t_1},\ \frac{(s-t_1)(t_2-s)}{t_2-t_1}\right).$$

Problem 3 — $E[B(t_1)B(t_2)B(t_3)]$

Problem. Compute $E[B(t_1)B(t_2)B(t_3)]$ for $t_1<t_2<t_3$.
Solution. $$\begin{aligned}E[B(t_1)B(t_2)B(t_3)]&=E\big[B(t_1)B(t_2)\,E[B(t_3)\mid B(t_1),B(t_2)]\big]=E[B(t_1)B^2(t_2)]\\&=E\big[B(t_1)\,E[B^2(t_2)\mid B(t_1)]\big]\overset{(*)}=E\big[B(t_1)\{(t_2-t_1)+B^2(t_1)\}\big]\\&=E[B^3(t_1)]+(t_2-t_1)E[B(t_1)]=\mathbf 0 .\end{aligned}$$ $(*)$: given $B(t_1)$, $B(t_2)\sim N(B(t_1),t_2-t_1)$. And $E[B^3(t)]=0$ because $B(t)$ is normal with mean 0.

Problem 4 — $T_a$ is finite but has infinite mean

Problem. Show that $P\{T_a<\infty\}=1$ and $E[T_a]=\infty$, $a\ne0$.
Solution. (a) By (10.6), $$P\{T_a<\infty\}=\lim_{t\to\infty}P\{T_a\le t\}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{|a|/\sqrt t}^\infty e^{-y^2/2}dy=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2/2}dy=2P\{N(0,1)>0\}=1 .$$ (b) Use $E[T_a]=\int_0^\infty P\{T_a>t\}\,dt$ with (10.7). Completed here: $$P\{T_a>t\}=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^{|a|/\sqrt t}e^{-y^2/2}dy\ \sim\ \frac{2|a|}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\quad(t\to\infty),$$ and $\int^\infty t^{-1/2}dt=\infty$, so $E[T_a]=\infty$.
The source prints the constant as "$2/\sqrt{2r}$". It is $2/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

Problem 5 — $P\{T_1<T_{-1}<T_2\}$

Problem. What is $P\{T_1<T_{-1}<T_2\}$?
Solution. $$\begin{aligned}P\{T_1<T_{-1}<T_2\}&=P\{\text{hit }1\text{ before }-1\}\times P\{\text{hit }-1\text{ before }2\mid\text{hit }1\text{ before }-1\}\\&=\tfrac12\,P\{\text{down }2\text{ before up }1\}=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13=\mathbf{\tfrac16}.\end{aligned}$$ The second factor comes from restarting the BM when it first hits $1$.

Problem 6 — Not recovering the purchase price

Problem. You own one share of a stock whose price follows standard BM. You bought it at $b+c$, $c>0$, and the present price is $b$. You will sell when the price reaches $b+c$ or after an additional time $t$, whichever comes first. What is the probability that you do not recover your purchase price?
Solution. You recover the price if and only if the BM goes up $c$ by time $t$. So $$P\{\text{not recover}\}=1-P\Big\{\max_{0\le s\le t}X(s)\ge c\Big\}=1-\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{c/\sqrt t}^\infty e^{-y^2/2}dy=2\Phi\!\left(\frac c{\sqrt t}\right)-1 .$$

Problem 7 — Maximum over $[t_1,t_2]$

Problem. Compute an expression for $P\Big\{\max_{t_1\le s\le t_2}B(s)>x\Big\}$.
Solution. Let $M=\{\max_{t_1\le s\le t_2}X(s)>x\}$ and condition on $X(t_1)$: $$P(M)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty P(M\mid X(t_1)=y)\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi t_1}}e^{-y^2/2t_1}dy .$$ Here $P(M\mid X(t_1)=y)=1$ for $y\ge x$. For $y<x$, by the reflection principle, $$P(M\mid X(t_1)=y)=P\Big\{\max_{0<s<t_2-t_1}X(s)>x-y\Big\}=2P\{X(t_2-t_1)>x-y\}.$$ Putting these together: $$P(M)=P\{X(t_1)\ge x\}+\int_{-\infty}^x2\,\bar\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{t_2-t_1}}\right)\frac{e^{-y^2/2t_1}}{\sqrt{2\pi t_1}}dy .$$

Problem 8 — Random walk limit and gambler's ruin

Problem. A random walk moves up or down $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ in each time step $\Delta t$, with probabilities $p$ and $1-p$, where $p=\tfrac12(1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t})$. (a) Argue that as $\Delta t\to0$ the limit is a Brownian motion with drift rate $\mu$. (b) Using (a) and the gambler's ruin results (Section 4.5.1), compute the probability that a BM with drift rate $\mu$ goes up $A$ before going down $B$, $A,B>0$.
Solution. (a) $X(t)=\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{[t/\Delta t]}X_i$ with $X_i=\pm1$ (up/down). Then $E[X_i]=2p-1=\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=1-\mu^2\Delta t$, so $$E[X(t)]=\sqrt{\Delta t}\left[\tfrac t{\Delta t}\right]\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}\to\mu t,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X(t))=\Delta t\left[\tfrac t{\Delta t}\right](1-\mu^2\Delta t)\to t .$$ (b) Gambler's ruin with $\pm1$ steps: the probability of going up $A$ before down $B$ is $\dfrac{1-(q/p)^B}{1-(q/p)^{A+B}}$. Here $q/p=\dfrac{1-\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}{1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, and the steps have size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so the barriers become $A/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and $B/\sqrt{\Delta t}$: $$(*)\quad\frac{1-\left[\frac{1-\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}{1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}\right]^{B/\sqrt{\Delta t}}}{1-\left[\frac{1-\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}{1+\mu\sqrt{\Delta t}}\right]^{(A+B)/\sqrt{\Delta t}}}.$$ With $h=\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and $n=1/h$: $\lim\left[\frac{1-\mu h}{1+\mu h}\right]^{1/h}=\lim\left[\frac{1-\mu/n}{1+\mu/n}\right]^n=\frac{e^{-\mu}}{e^{\mu}}=e^{-2\mu}$. So the limit of $(*)$ is $$\frac{1-e^{-2\mu B}}{1-e^{-2\mu(A+B)}}.$$

Problem 9 — Joint density of $X(s)$, $X(t)$

Problem. Let $\{X(t)\}$ be a BM with drift coefficient $\mu$ and variance parameter $\sigma^2$. What is the joint density of $X(s)$ and $X(t)$, $s<t$?
Solution (not in source). $X(s)\sim N(\mu s,\sigma^2s)$, and $X(t)-X(s)\sim N(\mu(t-s),\sigma^2(t-s))$ independently. By the change of variables $(x,y)\mapsto(x,y-x)$ (Jacobian 1), $$f_{X(s),X(t)}(x,y)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2s}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu s)^2}{2\sigma^2s}}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2(t-s)}}e^{-\frac{(y-x-\mu(t-s))^2}{2\sigma^2(t-s)}} .$$

Problem 10 — Conditional distribution given $X(s)=c$

Problem. Let $\{X(t)\}$ be a BM with drift coefficient $\mu$ and variance parameter $\sigma^2$. What is the conditional distribution of $X(t)$ given $X(s)=c$ when (a) $s<t$; (b) $t<s$?
Solution (not in source; same as BM Short Problems, Problem 5). (a) $N\big(c+\mu(t-s),\ \sigma^2(t-s)\big)$. (b) $N\!\left(\dfrac{tc}s,\ \dfrac{\sigma^2t(s-t)}s\right)$. The drift drops out once the endpoint is fixed.

Problem 11 — Multiplicative walk → GBM

Problem. A process changes every $h$ time units. Its new value is its old value multiplied by $e^{\sigma\sqrt h}$ with probability $p=\tfrac12(1+\tfrac\mu\sigma\sqrt h)$, or by $e^{-\sigma\sqrt h}$ with probability $1-p$. Show that as $h\to0$ it converges to geometric Brownian motion with drift coefficient $\mu$ and variance parameter $\sigma^2$.
Solution. Let $X(t)$ be the value at $t=nh$. Let $X_i=1$ if the $i$-th change is up and $0$ otherwise. With $u=e^{\sigma\sqrt h}$ and $d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt h}$: $$X(t)=X(0)u^{\sum X_i}d^{n-\sum X_i}=X(0)d^n(u/d)^{\sum_{i=1}^nX_i},\qquad\log\frac{X(t)}{X(0)}=-\frac th\sigma\sqrt h+2\sigma\sqrt h\sum_{i=1}^{t/h}X_i .$$ By the CLT this becomes normal as $h\to0$, and independent $X_i$ give independent increments. Also $$E\left[\log\tfrac{X(t)}{X(0)}\right]=-\tfrac th\sigma\sqrt h+2\sigma\sqrt h\tfrac th\cdot\tfrac12\left(1+\tfrac\mu\sigma\sqrt h\right)=\mu t,\qquad\operatorname{Var}\left[\log\tfrac{X(t)}{X(0)}\right]=4\sigma^2h\tfrac thp(1-p)\to\sigma^2t,$$ using $p\to\tfrac12$.

Problem 12 — No sure win: pricing an option

Problem. A stock sells at \$50 per share. After one period its price (in present-value dollars) will be either \$150 or \$25. An option to buy $y$ units at time 1 can be bought at cost $cy$. (a) What should $c$ be for there to be no sure win? (b) If $c=4$, explain how you could guarantee a sure win. (c) If $c=10$, explain how you could guarantee a sure win. (d) Use the arbitrage theorem to verify your answer to (a).
Solution. Buy $x$ units of stock and $y$ options. At time 1 the holdings are worth $150x+25y$ if the price is 150, and $25x$ if it is 25. With $y=-5x$ the value is $25x$ in both cases. The cost is $50x-5xc$, so the profit is $$25x-50x+5xc=x(5c-25).$$
  1. $c=\mathbf 5$: no sure win.
  2. $c=4$: sell $|x|$ shares and buy $5|x|$ options (i.e. $x<0$). Profit $5|x|$ whatever happens.
  3. $c=10$: buy $x>0$ shares and sell $5x$ options. Profit $25x>0$.
  4. The only $(p,1-p)$ on $(150,25)$ that makes buying the stock a fair bet has $50=150p+25(1-p)$, so $p=\tfrac15$. No arbitrage needs the option to be fair under it too: $c=25\cdot\tfrac15=5$.

Problem 13 — The remark after Example 10.2

Problem. Verify the statement made in the remark following Example 10.2. (In that example, outcome $i$ of a race pays odds $o_i$, and betting $x_j\propto(1+o_j)^{-1}$ on every $j$, when $\sum_k(1+o_k)^{-1}<1$, gives a sure win.)
Solution. Take $x_j=\dfrac{(1+o_j)^{-1}}{1-\sum_k(1+o_k)^{-1}}$. If the outcome is $i$, the total winnings are $$x_io_i-\sum_{j\ne i}x_j=\frac{o_i(1+o_i)^{-1}-\sum_{j\ne i}(1+o_j)^{-1}}{1-\sum_k(1+o_k)^{-1}}=\frac{(1+o_i)(1+o_i)^{-1}-\sum_j(1+o_j)^{-1}}{1-\sum_k(1+o_k)^{-1}}=1 .$$ This is the same whichever $i$ occurs, so the win is sure.

Problem 14 — Three-state arbitrage bounds

Problem. A stock's price is 100 now, and at time 1 it will be 50, 100 or 200. An option to buy $y$ shares at time 1 for the (present-value) price $ky$ costs $cy$. (a) If $k=120$, show that an arbitrage opportunity occurs if and only if $c>80/3$. (b) If $k=80$, show that there is no arbitrage opportunity if and only if $20\le c\le40$.
Solution. Buying the stock is a fair bet under $(p_1,p_2,1-p_1-p_2)$ on $(50,100,200)$ iff $100=50p_1+100p_2+200(1-p_1-p_2)$, i.e. $3p_1+2p_2=2$.
  1. The option ($k=120$) is also fair iff $c=80(1-p_1-p_2)$. Solving with $3p_1+2p_2=2$ gives $p_1=c/40$, $p_2=(80-3c)/80$, $1-p_1-p_2=c/80$. All three lie in $[0,1]$ iff $80\ge3c$. So arbitrage exists iff $c>80/3$.
  2. For $k=80$: $c=20p_2+120(1-p_1-p_2)$. Solving with $3p_1+2p_2=2$ gives $p_1=(c-20)/30$, $p_2=(40-c)/20$, $1-p_1-p_2=(c-20)/60$. These are all in $[0,1]$ iff $20\le c\le40$.

Problem 15 — Black–Scholes cost at three strikes

Problem. A stock is worth 100 now. The logarithm of its price follows a BM with drift coefficient $\mu=2$ and variance parameter $\sigma^2=1$. Give the Black–Scholes cost of an option to buy the stock at time 10 for a cost of (a) 100 per unit; (b) 120 per unit; (c) 80 per unit. The continuously compounded interest rate is 5 percent.
Solution. The drift $\mu$ does not enter the Black–Scholes price. Use $\alpha=0.05$, $\sigma=1$, $x_0=100$, $t=10$, and $C=x_0\Phi(\sigma\sqrt t+b)-Ke^{-\alpha t}\Phi(b)$ with $b=\dfrac{\alpha t-\sigma^2t/2-\log(K/x_0)}{\sigma\sqrt t}$.
  1. $K=100$: $b=(0.5-5-0)/\sqrt{10}=-1.423$, so $$C=100\,\Phi(1.739)-100e^{-0.5}\Phi(-1.423)\approx\mathbf{91.2}.$$
  2. (computed here) $K=120$: $b=-1.481$, $C\approx\mathbf{90.3}$.
  3. (computed here) $K=80$: $b=-1.352$, $C\approx\mathbf{92.2}$.
With $\sigma^2t=10$ the option is almost the stock itself, so the price barely moves with the strike.
The source lists the parameters as "$\sigma=.05,\ \sigma=1$" and writes the discount as $e^{-5}$. The first is the rate $\alpha=0.05$, and the discount factor is $e^{-\alpha t}=e^{-0.5}$; the $91.2$ uses $e^{-0.5}$.

Problem 16 — Martingales have constant mean

Problem. A process $\{Y(t)\}$ is a martingale if, for $s<t$, $E[Y(t)\mid Y(u),0\le u\le s]=Y(s)$. If $\{Y(t)\}$ is a martingale, show that $E[Y(t)]=E[Y(0)]$.
Solution. Taking expectations of the defining equation gives $E[Y(s)]=E\big[E[Y(t)\mid Y(u),0\le u\le s]\big]=E[Y(t)]$. So $E[Y(t)]$ is constant, equal to $E[Y(0)]$.

Problem 17 — BM is a martingale

Problem. Show that standard Brownian motion is a martingale.
Solution. $$E[B(t)\mid B(u),0\le u\le s]=E[B(s)\mid\cdot]+E[B(t)-B(s)\mid\cdot]=B(s)+E[B(t)-B(s)]=B(s),$$ using independent increments.

Problem 18 — $B^2(t)-t$ is a martingale

Problem. Show that $Y(t)=B^2(t)-t$ is a martingale. What is $E[Y(t)]$?
Hint. First compute $E[Y(t)\mid B(u),0\le u\le s]$.
Solution. By independent increments, $E[B^2(t)\mid B(u),0\le u\le s]=E[B^2(t)\mid B(s)]$. Given $B(s)$, $B(t)\sim N(B(s),t-s)$, so this equals $B^2(s)+t-s$. Hence $$E[B^2(t)-t\mid B(u),0\le u\le s]=B^2(s)-s .$$ This depends only on $B^2(s)$. Conditioning on $B(u)$, $u\le s$, and using the above, $E[B^2(t)-t\mid B^2(u),0\le u\le s]=B^2(s)-s$. So it is a martingale, and $E[Y(t)]=E[Y(0)]=\mathbf0$.

Problem 19 — The exponential martingale

Problem. Show that $Y(t)=\exp\{cB(t)-c^2t/2\}$ is a martingale for any constant $c$. What is $E[Y(t)]$?
Solution. Knowing $Y(t)$ is equivalent to knowing $B(t)$. Given $B(s)$, $B(t)\sim N(B(s),t-s)$, so by the normal MGF $$E[Y(t)\mid Y(u),u\le s]=e^{-c^2t/2}E[e^{cB(t)}\mid B(s)]=e^{-c^2t/2}e^{cB(s)+(t-s)c^2/2}=e^{-c^2s/2}e^{cB(s)}=Y(s).$$ So it is a martingale, and $E[Y(t)]=E[Y(0)]=\mathbf1$.

Problem 20 — Hitting the line $2-4t$

Problem. Let $T=\min\{t:B(t)=2-4t\}$, the first time standard BM hits the line $2-4t$. Use the martingale stopping theorem to find $E[T]$.
Solution. $E[B(T)]=E[B(0)]=0$, and $B(T)=2-4T$. So $2-4E[T]=0$, which gives $E[T]=\mathbf{1/2}$.

Problem 21 — First passage of BM with drift

Problem. Let $X(t)=\sigma B(t)+\mu t$ with $\mu>0$, and for $x>0$ let $T=\min\{t:X(t)=x\}=\min\{t:B(t)=\tfrac{x-\mu t}\sigma\}$. Use the martingale stopping theorem to show that $E[T]=x/\mu$.
Solution. $E[B(T)]=0$ with $B(T)=(x-\mu T)/\sigma$. So $E[(x-\mu T)/\sigma]=0$, which gives $E[T]=x/\mu$.

Problem 22 — Probability of hitting $A$ before $-B$

Problem. Let $X(t)=\sigma B(t)+\mu t$, and for $A,B>0$ let $p$ be the probability that $X$ hits $A$ before $-B$. (a) Let $T$ be the first time the process hits $A$ or $-B$. Use this $T$ and the martingale of Exercise 19 to show that $E[\exp\{c(X(T)-\mu T)/\sigma-c^2T/2\}]=1$. (b) Let $c=-2\mu/\sigma$ and show that $E[\exp\{-2\mu X(T)/\sigma^2\}]=1$. (c) Use (b) and the definition of $T$ to find $p$.
Hint. What are the possible values of $\exp\{-2\mu X(T)/\sigma^2\}$?
Solution. (a) By Problem 19 and the stopping theorem, $E[\exp\{cB(T)-c^2T/2\}]=E[\exp\{cB(0)\}]=1$. Substituting $B(T)=[X(T)-\mu T]/\sigma$ gives (a).
(b) With $c=-2\mu/\sigma$: $-2\mu[X(T)-\mu T]/\sigma^2-(2\mu/\sigma)^2T/2=-2\mu X(T)/\sigma^2$.
(c) $X(T)=A$ w.p. $p$ and $-B$ w.p. $1-p$. So $1=pe^{-2\mu A/\sigma^2}+(1-p)e^{2\mu B/\sigma^2}$, and $$p=\frac{1-e^{2\mu B/\sigma^2}}{e^{-2\mu A/\sigma^2}-e^{2\mu B/\sigma^2}} .$$

Problem 23 — Mean exit time with drift

Problem. Let $X(t)=\sigma B(t)+\mu t$, and let $T$ be the first time $X$ hits $A$ or $-B$ ($A,B>0$). Use the martingale stopping theorem and Exercise 22(c) to find $E[T]$.
Solution. $E[B(T)]=0$ with $B(T)=[X(T)-\mu T]/\sigma$ gives $E[X(T)]=\mu E[T]$. Now $E[X(T)]=pA-(1-p)B$ with $p$ from 22(c). So $$E[T]=\frac{A(1-e^{2\mu B/\sigma^2})-B(e^{-2\mu A/\sigma^2}-1)}{\mu(e^{-2\mu A/\sigma^2}-e^{2\mu B/\sigma^2})} .$$

Problem 24 — Variance of the first passage time

Problem. Let $X(t)=\sigma B(t)+\mu t$ with $\mu>0$, $x>0$, and $T=\min\{t:X(t)=x\}$ (as in Exercise 21). Use the martingale of Exercise 18 and the result of Exercise 21 to show that $\operatorname{Var}(T)=x\sigma^2/\mu^3$.
Solution. By the stopping theorem and Problem 18, $E[B^2(T)-T]=0$ with $B(T)=[X(T)-\mu T]/\sigma$. So $E[(X(T)-\mu T)^2/\sigma^2-T]=0$. Since $X(T)=x$, this gives $E[(x-\mu T)^2]=\sigma^2E[T]$. From Problem 21, $E[T]=x/\mu$, so $E[(x-\mu T)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\mu T)$. Hence $$\operatorname{Var}(\mu T)=\sigma^2x/\mu\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Var}(T)=\sigma^2x/\mu^3 .$$

Problem 25 — Mean and variance of two Itô integrals

Problem. Compute the mean and variance of (a) $\int_0^1t\,dB(t)$ and (b) $\int_0^1t^2\,dB(t)$.
Solution. Both means are $0$. By the Itô isometry: $$\operatorname{Var}\left[\int_0^1t\,dB\right]=\int_0^1t^2dt=\mathbf{\tfrac13},\qquad\operatorname{Var}\left[\int_0^1t^2\,dB\right]=\int_0^1t^4dt=\mathbf{\tfrac15}.$$

Problem 26 — Time inversion

Problem. Let $Y(t)=tB(1/t)$ for $t>0$ and $Y(0)=0$. (a) What is the distribution of $Y(t)$? (b) Compute $\operatorname{Cov}(Y(s),Y(t))$. (c) Argue that $\{Y(t)\}$ is a standard Brownian motion process.
Solution. (a) Normal with $E[Y(t)]=tE[B(1/t)]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y(t))=t^2\cdot\tfrac1t=t$. So $Y(t)\sim N(0,t)$.
(b) For $s\le t$: $\operatorname{Cov}(sB(1/s),tB(1/t))=st\,\operatorname{Cov}(B(1/s),B(1/t))=st\cdot\tfrac1t=s$.
(c) $Y$ is Gaussian with the same mean and covariance functions as BM, which is also Gaussian. So it is BM.
The source writes "when $s\le t$" on both lines of (b). The step $\min(1/s,1/t)=1/t$ uses $s\le t$.

Problem 27 — Brownian scaling

Problem. Let $Y(t)=B(a^2t)/a$ for $a>0$. Argue that $\{Y(t)\}$ is a standard Brownian motion process.
Solution. $E[Y(t)]=\tfrac1aE[B(a^2t)]=0$. For $s<t$: $\operatorname{Cov}(Y(s),Y(t))=\tfrac1{a^2}\operatorname{Cov}(B(a^2s),B(a^2t))=\tfrac1{a^2}a^2s=s$. $Y$ is clearly Gaussian, so it is BM.

Problem 28 — Independence of $B(s)-\frac stB(t)$ and $B(t)$

Problem. For $s<t$, argue that $B(s)-\frac stB(t)$ and $B(t)$ are independent.
Solution. $\operatorname{Cov}(B(s)-\tfrac stB(t),B(t))=s-\tfrac st\cdot t=0$. The pair is jointly Gaussian, and for jointly Gaussian variables zero covariance implies independence.

Problem 29 — Brownian bridge → BM

Problem. Let $\{Z(t)\}$ be a Brownian bridge. Show that $Y(t)=(t+1)Z(t/(t+1))$ is a standard Brownian motion process.
Solution. $Y$ is Gaussian with $E[Y(t)]=0$. For $s\le t$, using $\operatorname{Cov}(Z(u),Z(v))=u(1-v)$ for $u\le v$: $$\operatorname{Cov}(Y(s),Y(t))=(s+1)(t+1)\frac s{s+1}\left[1-\frac t{t+1}\right]=s .$$ It has the same mean and covariance as BM, and these determine a Gaussian process, so it is BM.

Problem 30 — Poisson increment covariance

Problem. Let $X(t)=N(t+1)-N(t)$, where $\{N(t)\}$ is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$. Compute $\operatorname{Cov}[X(t),X(t+s)]$.
Solution. For $s<1$, $N(t)$ is independent of $N(t+s+1)-N(t+s)$, so $$\operatorname{Cov}=\operatorname{Cov}(N(t+1),N(t+s+1))-\operatorname{Cov}(N(t+1),N(t+s))=\lambda(t+1)-\lambda(t+s)=\lambda(1-s),$$ using $\operatorname{Cov}(N(s),N(t))=\lambda\min(s,t)$. For $s\ge1$ the two increments do not overlap, so they are independent and the covariance is $0$. $$\operatorname{Cov}[X(t),X(t+s)]=\lambda(1-s)^+ .$$

Problem 31 — Time to the next Poisson event

Problem. Let $\{N(t)\}$ be a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, and let $Y(t)$ be the time from $t$ until the next event. (a) Argue that $\{Y(t)\}$ is a stationary process. (b) Compute $\operatorname{Cov}[Y(t),Y(t+s)]$.
Solution. (a) From any time $t$, the continuation of a Poisson process is again a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$.
(b) Condition on $Y(t)=y\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. If $y<s$, the next event happens before $t+s$ and the process restarts, so $E[Y(t)Y(t+s)\mid y]=y\cdot\tfrac1\lambda$. If $y>s$, then $Y(t+s)=y-s$. So $$E[Y(t)Y(t+s)]=\int_0^sy\tfrac1\lambda\lambda e^{-\lambda y}dy+\int_s^\infty y(y-s)\lambda e^{-\lambda y}dy,$$ $$\operatorname{Cov}(Y(t),Y(t+s))=\int_0^sye^{-\lambda y}dy+\int_s^\infty y(y-s)\lambda e^{-\lambda y}dy-\frac1{\lambda^2}.$$ Evaluated here: the first integral is $\frac{1-e^{-\lambda s}(1+\lambda s)}{\lambda^2}$ and the second (put $y=s+u$) is $e^{-\lambda s}\left(\frac s\lambda+\frac2{\lambda^2}\right)$. So $$\operatorname{Cov}(Y(t),Y(t+s))=\mathbf{\frac{e^{-\lambda s}}{\lambda^2}}.$$

Problem 32 — Differences of a weakly stationary process

Problem. Let $\{X(t),-\infty<t<\infty\}$ be weakly stationary with covariance function $R_X(s)=\operatorname{Cov}[X(t),X(t+s)]$. (a) Show that $\operatorname{Var}(X(t+s)-X(t))=2R_X(0)-2R_X(s)$. (b) If $Y(t)=X(t+1)-X(t)$, show that $\{Y(t)\}$ is also weakly stationary, with $R_Y(s)=2R_X(s)-R_X(s-1)-R_X(s+1)$.
Solution. (a) $\operatorname{Cov}(X(t+s)-X(t),X(t+s)-X(t))=R(0)-R(s)-R(s)+R(0)=2R(0)-2R(s)$.
(b) $\operatorname{Cov}(X(t+1)-X(t),\,X(t+s+1)-X(t+s))=R_X(s)-R_X(s-1)-R_X(s+1)+R_X(s)$. This depends only on $s$, and the mean of $Y$ is constant (zero), so $Y$ is weakly stationary with that $R_Y$.
The source prints the problem statement with $2R_X(t)$. It should be $2R_X(s)$, which is what the solution derives.

Problem 33 — Random harmonic process

Problem. Let $Y_1,Y_2$ be independent unit normals and, for a constant $w$, $X(t)=Y_1\cos wt+Y_2\sin wt$. (a) Show that $\{X(t)\}$ is weakly stationary. (b) Argue that $\{X(t)\}$ is stationary.
Solution. (a) $E[X(t)]=0$, and $$\operatorname{Cov}(X(t),X(t+s))=\cos wt\cos w(t+s)+\sin wt\sin w(t+s)=\cos(w(t+s)-wt)=\cos ws,$$ which depends only on $s$.
(b) (not in source) $X$ is a Gaussian process, as a linear combination of the Gaussian $Y_1,Y_2$. A Gaussian process's finite-dimensional distributions are fixed by its mean and covariance functions, and both are shift-invariant by (a). So it is strictly stationary.

Problem 34 — Power spectral density

Problem. Let $\{X(t)\}$ be weakly stationary with covariance function $R(s)=\operatorname{Cov}(X(t),X(t+s))$, and let $\tilde R(w)$ be its power spectral density. (i) Show that $\tilde R(w)=\tilde R(-w)$. It can be shown that $R(s)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\tilde R(w)e^{iws}dw$. (ii) Use this to show that $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\tilde R(w)\,dw=2\pi E[X^2(t)]$.
Solution (not in source). (i) $\tilde R(w)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty R(s)e^{-iws}ds$. $R$ is real and even: $R(-s)=\operatorname{Cov}(X(t),X(t-s))=\operatorname{Cov}(X(t-s),X(t))=R(s)$. Substituting $s\to-s$ gives $\tilde R(-w)=\int R(-s)e^{-iws}ds=\tilde R(w)$.
(ii) Put $s=0$ in the inversion formula: $R(0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\tilde R(w)dw$, and $R(0)=\operatorname{Var}X(t)$. So $\int\tilde R(w)dw=2\pi\operatorname{Var}X(t)$, which equals $2\pi E[X^2(t)]$ when $EX(t)=0$.
The identity with $E[X^2(t)]$ needs a zero-mean process (the usual convention for spectral densities). For a general mean it holds with $\operatorname{Var}X(t)$.
